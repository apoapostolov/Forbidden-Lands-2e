# Forbidden Lands 2E Writing Guide

## Purpose

This guide defines the writing voice for this repository as it appears in the integrated manuscript in `corebook/`.

It is meant to help a human writer, an editor, or an AI assistant produce prose that belongs in this book.

The target is not generic fantasy prose.
The target is not modern game-tech copy.
The target is not clever imitation for its own sake.

The target is the voice already present in this manuscript:

- harsh
- clear
- grounded
- physical
- unsentimental
- myth-shadowed, but never vague when rules matter

## Corpus Priority

When learning the style, use this priority order:

1. chapter-opening fiction and epigraphs
2. setting and flavor exposition in `corebook/01-front-matter.md`
3. profession, kin, talent, and magic introductions
4. item and weapon descriptions in `corebook/10-gear.md`
5. rules explanation prose in `corebook/05-combat-and-damage.md`, `corebook/06-critical-injuries.md`, and `corebook/08-journeys.md`

The voice lives in all of these together, not in any single passage.

## How AI Should Learn This Style

Do not try to "copy style" by asking for a surface imitation of a fantasy author.
That usually produces mush: too many adjectives, too much fog, not enough judgment.

Use this method instead.

### 1. Build a representative style set

Select short passages from across the manuscript, not one favorite scene.
Include:

- fiction openings
- atmospheric setting paragraphs
- practical flavor explanations
- rules prose with strong voice
- item and weapon descriptions

This prevents overfitting to one register.

### 2. Separate style from topic

Study recurring habits, not just repeated nouns.
Track:

- sentence length and rhythm
- how often the prose uses direct statement versus image
- degree of metaphor
- preferred verbs
- preferred level of bodily detail
- how headings, lists, and examples are framed
- punctuation habits
- how often the prose names emotions directly

The point is to learn how the manuscript speaks, not merely what it speaks about.

### 3. Learn contrastively

For every "do this" pattern, define a "do not drift into this" pattern.

Examples:

- concrete, blood-and-stone physicality
  not lyrical mist without consequence
- plain old-world diction
  not faux-archaic parody
- short forceful rule prose
  not legalistic over-qualification
- strong flavor embedded in facts
  not purple flavor disconnected from play

### 4. Retrieve exemplars before writing

Before drafting a section, pull a few relevant local exemplars from the manuscript.

If writing:

- rules text, retrieve rules text
- item description, retrieve item description
- flavor-setting prose, retrieve flavor-setting prose
- mixed fiction-and-rules explanation, retrieve both

Do not rely on memory alone.

### 5. Use preference comparison, not one-shot generation

Generate at least two phrasings when the tone matters.
Then compare them against this guide:

- which one sounds more native to the manuscript
- which one is clearer
- which one uses stronger nouns and verbs
- which one avoids modern commentary
- which one best supports play

### 6. Evaluate style explicitly

A draft is not done because it sounds "cool."
Check:

- could this sentence exist beside the current manuscript without drawing attention
- did the line trade clarity for mood
- did it become too modern, too clinical, or too theatrical
- did it say more than the book needs
- does the rule still resolve cleanly at the table

## Core Voice

The manuscript speaks in two close registers:

1. a grim, lived-in fantasy register for setting, items, powers, and people
2. a direct instructional register for rules

These are not separate voices.
They are the same voice adjusting pressure.

The shared qualities are:

- confidence
- compression
- physical specificity
- emotional restraint
- low tolerance for fluff

## Tone

The world is dangerous, old, and intimate.
The prose should feel like it knows the weight of iron, mud, weather, blood, smoke, hunger, and fear.

The tone is:

- dark without being melodramatic
- severe without being numb
- practical without being flat
- mythic without losing the body

The book does not sentimentalize survival.
It also does not gloat over suffering.

## Diction

Prefer words with weight, texture, and use.

Examples of the manuscript's preferred diction:

- blade, haft, grip, haft, wedge, spike, cord, hearth, ruin, mire, blood, smoke, frost, strain, rot, burden, bent, split, cracked, scarred

Prefer:

- plain English with a low-medieval feel
- craft words
- body words
- travel words
- weather words
- material words

Avoid:

- breezy contemporary phrasing
- therapy language unless the manuscript already supports it
- abstract institutional language
- inflated epic diction
- pseudo-archaic noise like `thou`, `forsooth`, `hath`, unless quoting a specific in-world source

## Syntax And Rhythm

Most strong manuscript prose uses one of three sentence shapes:

1. short declarative statement
2. medium-length explanatory sentence with one clear turn
3. longer descriptive sentence built from concrete clauses, not decorative filler

Good rhythm:

- varied, but controlled
- often starts with the object or fact
- places the strongest noun or verb near the end
- uses lists when the reader needs them

Bad rhythm:

- endless cascading clauses
- repetitive subject-verb openings
- three adjectives where one noun would do
- "voicey" filler that delays the real point

## Paragraph Structure

The manuscript does not build paragraphs as essays.
It builds them as units of function.

Most paragraphs in the corebook do one of these jobs:

1. establish a world truth
2. define a rule concept
3. explain a mechanical consequence
4. describe an object, weapon, spell, or practice
5. walk the reader into a more detailed subsection

That means most paragraphs are short to medium in length.
They usually contain one governing idea and, at most, one turn.

### Common paragraph shapes

#### 1. Statement -> consequence

Used in rules and practical flavor.

Shape:

- first sentence names the thing plainly
- second sentence gives consequence, use, or limit

Example pattern:

- "The mace is an ancient and effective weapon."
- then how it is built, why it is common, and what role it fills

#### 2. World condition -> player implication

Used in setting, gear economy, travel, and stronghold text.

Shape:

- open with what the world is like
- follow with what that means in play

This is common in descriptions of scarcity, weather, trade, and kin relations.

#### 3. Definition -> procedure -> exception

This is one of the core rule paragraph shapes.

Shape:

- define the action or term
- tell the reader how it works
- add the exception only if needed

The order matters.
Do not begin with the exception.

#### 4. Description -> distinction

Used heavily in gear and weapon writing.

Shape:

- describe what the object is in material terms
- distinguish it from a nearby object
- close with use, limitation, or culture

This is how the manuscript prevents item descriptions from becoming generic catalog copy.

### Paragraph length

Broadly:

- fiction epigraphs may run longer if their cadence earns it
- setting and flavor paragraphs often run 3 to 6 sentences
- rules paragraphs often run 1 to 4 sentences
- item descriptions often run 2 to 5 sentences
- examples are usually compact and self-contained

If a paragraph grows long, it should still feel linear.
The manuscript does not favor spiral paragraphs that wander through tone before reaching meaning.

### Paragraph endings

The manuscript often ends paragraphs on:

- a hard fact
- a practical warning
- a cultural distinction
- a use case
- a note of danger

This gives the prose weight.
Avoid ending on filler such as soft qualifiers or self-conscious commentary.

## Imagery

Imagery should be tactile and consequential.

Good imagery in this manuscript tends to come from:

- blood
- stone
- iron
- wood
- weather
- beasts
- decay
- distance
- firelight

Use imagery to sharpen the world, not to drown the sentence.

One hard image is better than three soft ones.

## Emotion

Emotion is usually shown through:

- action
- restraint
- aftermath
- choice under pressure
- what the body does

Do not overname feelings.
The manuscript trusts the reader.

Better:

- "He could not bear the hearth any longer."

Worse:

- "He felt deeply conflicted and emotionally overwhelmed by the oppressive circumstances."

## Rules Prose

Rules prose must sound like the same book, but it must resolve cleanly.

Rules prose should:

- define the thing early
- state the condition plainly
- give the effect cleanly
- name the exceptions only when needed
- keep examples brief and useful

Rules prose may carry atmosphere, but never at the cost of procedure.

Use:

- direct statement
- active voice
- stable terminology
- short paragraphs
- bullets and tables when they improve play

Do not:

- hide rules inside flavor
- invent synonyms for established game terms
- stack multiple exceptions into one sentence if they can be split

## Sentence Size And Structure

Sentence length in this manuscript is varied, but not random.
Variation is used for control, not decoration.

### Short sentences

Short sentences are used for:

- hard rules
- warnings
- emphasis
- closing blows

They are often plain subject-verb-object statements.

Examples of function:

- define an action
- state a penalty
- close a paragraph with force

### Medium sentences

Medium sentences are the manuscript default.
They carry most of the book.

They usually:

- start with the thing being described
- add one clarifying clause
- end on purpose, limitation, or effect

This is the safest sentence shape for new writing in this book.

### Long sentences

Longer sentences appear when the manuscript needs:

- layered physical description
- a sequence of related facts
- a setting paragraph with accumulating pressure

But even then, the sentence usually stays structurally simple.
It often uses chained concrete clauses rather than abstract subordination.

Good long sentence:

- accumulates objects, actions, or conditions
- remains readable aloud
- keeps the main clause clear

Bad long sentence:

- keeps delaying the main point
- packs in three exceptions and two metaphors
- sounds like notes that were never edited

### Preferred sentence habits

The manuscript often prefers:

- active voice
- concrete subjects
- verbs that show use, pressure, or motion
- clauses linked by `and`, `but`, `instead`, `while`, `if`

It less often prefers:

- nested parenthetical logic
- abstract nominal phrases
- bureaucratic formulations

### Sentence openings

Common openings:

- the object itself: `The mace...`
- the world condition: `Markets in the Ravenlands...`
- the rule action: `Casting a spell...`
- the category label followed by a colon in rules sections

Less common and generally weaker:

- throat-clearing openings
- empty anticipatory phrasing like `It is worth noting that`
- commentary about the writing itself

### Compression

The manuscript compresses by selecting stronger nouns and verbs, not by omitting needed explanation.

Prefer:

- `The blade widens toward the tip.`

Over:

- `The blade has a tendency to become wider as it approaches the tip area.`

Prefer:

- `You fall prone.`

Over:

- `Your character is considered to have fallen into a prone position.`

## Flavor Explanations

This manuscript is strongest when flavor explanation does two jobs at once:

1. it teaches the world
2. it tells the reader how to think about the rule

Examples include:

- gear descriptions that explain scarcity, craft, and use
- magic introductions that explain danger and worldview
- front-matter setting prose that frames the whole game as free, perilous roaming in a cursed land

When writing flavor explanation:

- begin from material reality
- tie the object or rule to the land
- explain who uses it, why, and under what pressures
- end on utility, danger, scarcity, or cultural meaning

## Mixed Fiction And Rules

Some passages must blend theme and procedure.
When doing this:

- open with the human or worldly truth
- move quickly into usable explanation
- return to theme only if the closing line earns it

This is the right shape:

- setting truth
- practical explanation
- specific consequence

This is the wrong shape:

- decorative lore
- decorative lore
- decorative lore
- buried mechanic

## Explanatory Text

Explanatory text in this manuscript exists to reduce reader uncertainty.
It should answer the next sensible question before the reader has to ask it.

Good explanatory text usually does one or more of these things:

- clarifies why the rule exists
- distinguishes one item or action from a similar one
- ties a mechanic back to the world
- prevents a likely table mistake

It should not:

- restate the same rule in softer words
- justify every design choice
- spin into essay mode

### Where explanatory text belongs

Use explanatory text:

- after an unfamiliar subsystem
- after a rule that breaks a common expectation
- in item and weapon descriptions where physical construction affects play
- when a cultural or setting fact changes how the reader interprets a rule

Do not add explanatory text where a clean rule line is enough.

### Explanatory density

One of the manuscript's strengths is that it varies density.

Dense when:

- teaching a subsystem
- introducing a new chapter domain
- clarifying edge cases

Light when:

- repeating an established pattern
- listing a straightforward action
- describing a familiar item category

When extending the manuscript, match density to novelty.

## Examples

Examples are used sparingly, but where they appear they solve a real teaching problem.

The manuscript tends to use examples when a scenario requires the reader to combine several small rules:

- timing
- action economy
- attributes and skills
- initiative
- damage
- healing windows
- opposed rolls
- travel consequences

Examples are not there for decoration.
They are there to show a rules chain in motion.

### When an example is necessary

Add an example when:

- two or more small rules interact in a sequence
- the timing of actions matters
- a rule has a likely mistaken reading
- the reader needs to see how fiction turns into procedure
- the result of a roll changes what options are available next

Do not add an example when:

- the rule is already obvious from one sentence
- the example only repeats the rule without adding sequence
- the example introduces more complexity than the rule itself

### What a good example does

A strong example in this manuscript usually:

1. names the acting character
2. states the situation in fiction terms
3. states the relevant roll or table
4. gives the result
5. shows the consequence
6. sometimes shows the next decision that follows from that consequence

This gives the reader a miniature play sequence.

### Example size

Most examples should stay short.

Good size:

- one compact paragraph
- enough detail to teach the chain
- no extra color that obscures the point

Longer examples are justified only if the subsystem is itself layered.

### Example language

Examples should read like actual play filtered through the manuscript voice.

They should:

- stay concrete
- use names
- name real rolls and outcomes
- keep the fiction active

They should not:

- become chatty
- become transcript-like
- explain every number twice

### Pattern for multi-rule examples

When multiple small rules must be shown together, use this order:

1. fiction setup
2. triggering rule
3. roll and result
4. immediate consequence
5. follow-on state

For instance:

- adventurer is Broken
- critical injury is rolled
- time limit is established
- recovery attempt fails or succeeds
- next play pressure becomes clear

This is better than presenting all the rules abstractly and hoping the reader combines them alone.

## Scenario Construction For Examples

When inventing a teaching example for this book:

- choose a typical, high-pressure situation
- use only the rules needed for that lesson
- avoid edge cases unless the example is specifically there to teach one
- end with a clear state that the table can imagine continuing from

Good scenarios:

- a retreat under pressure
- a healing attempt against a lethal injury
- a forced march that turns into exhaustion
- an ambush that shows initiative and sneak interaction
- a spell cast under risk of mishap

Weak scenarios:

- abstract dice-only demonstrations
- overly elaborate story scenes
- examples that require outside chapter knowledge the reader likely does not have yet

## Structural Guidance By Section Type

### Chapter introduction

Use:

- a strong opening paragraph
- 2 to 5 medium paragraphs
- a movement from world truth to player implication

### Rules subsection

Use:

- short opening definition
- short paragraphs or bullets
- example only if multiple rules interact

### Item or weapon entry

Use:

- first sentence naming what it is
- second sentence on construction or distinction
- closing sentence on use, weakness, or cultural spread

### Flavor-mechanic bridge

Use:

- one short atmospheric sentence or clause
- direct explanation immediately after

Do not let the bridge become a monologue.

## What To Emphasize

Emphasize:

- scarcity
- rough craft
- bodily risk
- weather and terrain
- social tension between kin
- old wounds in the land
- personal ambition rather than hero destiny
- the difference between surviving and thriving

## What To Avoid

Avoid these common failures.

### Generic fantasy sludge

Signs:

- ancient, mystical, whispered, shadowy, eldritch in every paragraph
- too many adjectives
- atmosphere with no object

### Modern explanatory drift

Signs:

- "the user"
- "functionality"
- "mobility solution"
- "trauma-informed" unless discussing proposal-space editorial reasoning rather than manuscript prose

### Clinical overcorrection

Signs:

- pathology labels where the manuscript would use bodily description
- medical terminology that sounds post-industrial rather than field-healer plausible

### Empty cruelty

Signs:

- suffering described for spectacle alone
- grimness without human weight

## Authorial Posture

The book sounds like it respects the reader and expects them to keep up.

It does not:

- apologize
- wink
- over-explain obvious fantasy concepts
- flatter the player

It does:

- state things cleanly
- trust strong nouns
- move from world to use
- let danger stand

## AI Drafting Workflow

When using AI to draft manuscript prose:

1. identify the target register
   - fiction
   - flavor explanation
   - rules
   - mixed flavor and rules
2. retrieve 3 to 6 local exemplar passages in the same register
3. write a short style brief from those exemplars before drafting
4. draft
5. run a contrastive revision pass
   - too modern?
   - too vague?
   - too ornate?
   - too clinical?
   - too generic?
6. run a rules pass if mechanics are involved
7. read aloud for rhythm
8. inspect paragraph shape and ask whether each paragraph has only one real job
9. if a cluster of rules interacts, decide whether a short example is required

## Quick Style Checklist

Before accepting any new prose, ask:

- Is it concrete?
- Is it harsh without becoming silly?
- Is it clear?
- Does it sound lived-in rather than cinematic?
- Does it use strong nouns and verbs?
- Does it avoid both purple fantasy and dry tech prose?
- If it is rules text, can it be resolved fast at the table?
- If it is flavor, does it teach the world rather than merely decorate it?
- Does each paragraph have a clear structural job?
- Are the sentence lengths varied with purpose?
- If several small rules interact, would a short example teach the reader faster than more explanation?

## House Dos

- Do use direct, physical language.
- Do let the land feel old, hostile, and inhabited.
- Do keep rule terms stable.
- Do prefer one strong image over several weak ones.
- Do let equipment, magic, and violence feel material.
- Do write people as driven, tired, greedy, proud, fearful, stubborn, or hungry before writing them as "epic."

## House Don'ts

- Don't write like a parody of medieval speech.
- Don't write like a modern content designer.
- Don't write like a horror poet unless the passage truly calls for it.
- Don't replace grounded physical description with psychology jargon.
- Don't let flavor obscure procedure.
- Don't smooth the world into polite neutrality.

## Final Standard

If a new paragraph could be dropped into this manuscript and feel as though it had always been there, the guide has been followed.

If it sounds merely competent, stylish, or dark, that is not enough.

It must sound like this book.

## Appendix - Model Paragraph Templates

These are not fill-in-the-blank formulas.
They are working shapes.

Use them when a draft feels loose, overly modern, or structurally uncertain.

### 1. Rules Explanation Template

Use for:

- an action
- a condition
- a subsystem step
- a rule exception that needs clean framing

Structure:

1. name the thing
2. state what triggers it
3. state what happens
4. add the exception only if needed

Template shape:

- `[Thing]` happens when `[trigger or condition]`.
- When it does, `[primary effect]`.
- If `[important exception]`, `[exception effect]`.

Model:

- `A forced march happens when the company keeps moving after ordinary travel should end. When it does, the marcher's body begins to fail under the extra strain. If the ground is bad or the weather turns, the roll should grow harsher, not softer.`

Why it fits:

- starts with definition
- moves directly to consequence
- keeps the exception subordinate

### 2. Item Or Weapon Description Template

Use for:

- gear entries
- weapon descriptions
- tools
- crafted objects

Structure:

1. what it is
2. how it is built or distinguished
3. what kind of use or culture surrounds it

Template shape:

- `The [item] is [plain category and use].`
- `It is made or shaped [physical description], which makes it [distinction, strength, or weakness].`
- `It is common among [people, places, or use case], and is favored when [practical reason].`

Model:

- `The hunting spear is a long thrusting weapon made for boar, bear, and anything else that closes hard. Its shaft is stout, and the head is often set above a crossbar to keep a charging beast from running all the way up the wood. It is favored by hunters, watchmen, and any traveler who expects the wild to rush them head-on.`

Why it fits:

- concrete materials
- practical distinction
- social and world placement

### 3. Setting Paragraph Template

Use for:

- chapter intros
- place descriptions
- cultural framing
- world-state explanation

Structure:

1. state the condition of the world
2. show pressure or conflict inside it
3. end on what that means for those who live there

Template shape:

- `[Place or people] is [state of the world].`
- `[Pressure, danger, old wound, or tension] shapes how life is lived there.`
- `Because of this, [the practical or human consequence].`

Model:

- `The border villages are not empty, only thinned and watchful. Old fear still clings to the roads, and every ruin nearby carries the memory of something taken or left behind in haste. Because of this, strangers are weighed before they are welcomed, and every useful hand is valued until it becomes a threat.`

Why it fits:

- atmospheric, but not vague
- grounded in consequence
- suited to Ravenlands tone

### 4. Flavor-And-Rule Bridge Template

Use for:

- mechanics that need world texture
- brief flavor before an action or subsystem
- transitions from fiction to procedure

Structure:

1. one world-facing truth
2. one practical explanation

Template shape:

- `In the Forbidden Lands, [world truth].`
- `That is why [rule or procedure].`

Or:

- `[World truth].`
- `In play, this means [rule consequence].`

Model:

- `In the Forbidden Lands, a camp is never only a place to sleep. It is your warmth, your lookout, your dry gear, and sometimes the thin line between a company and a heap of bodies by dawn. In play, this means poor shelter should matter whenever weather, cold, or nightly danger are in question.`

Why it fits:

- short
- thematic
- resolves into procedure quickly

### 5. Example Paragraph Template

Use for:

- interaction of multiple small rules
- timing questions
- action chains
- injury, travel, spell, or combat sequences

Structure:

1. fiction setup
2. triggering roll or rule
3. result
4. consequence
5. new situation

Template shape:

- `[Character] is in [situation].`
- `They [take action or trigger rule], so [roll or table result].`
- `The result is [number or outcome], which means [immediate consequence].`
- `Now [new pressure, choice, or state].`

Model:

- `Sereva is already limping when the wolfkin spear catches her in the side and breaks her Strength. She rolls on the slash table and gets a lethal result, with only hours before the wound kills her. Her companion binds the wound but fails the first HEALING roll. Now the company must either find help before dark or watch her die by the fire they dared not leave.`

Why it fits:

- shows rules in motion
- keeps fiction live
- ends with playable pressure

### 6. Mixed List-And-Prose Template

Use for:

- chapter setup followed by bullets
- a subsystem introduction
- serviceable explanatory text before a table or options list

Structure:

1. one short orienting paragraph
2. bullets that preserve the same voice

Template shape:

- short paragraph naming the domain and why it matters
- bullets beginning with strong labels or nouns

Model:

- `Trade in the Ravenlands is local, suspicious, and shaped by need more than abundance. A fair price in one village may be madness in the next.`
- `- Iron is dear where no smith can work it.`
- `- Salt travels farther than grain.`
- `- Fine cloth often costs a favor before it costs silver.`

Why it fits:

- preserves voice while improving scanability

### 7. Anti-Template Warning

If a paragraph starts to sound like this, stop and rebuild it:

- abstract opener
- generic fantasy adjective
- delayed subject
- three clauses of atmosphere
- buried rule at the end

Bad shape:

- `In the dark and ancient reaches of the world, where many strange and uncanny things can sometimes occur under certain conditions, travelers may find that they are subject to unusual hardships that can, in some cases, affect their movement.`

Why it fails:

- no object
- no pressure
- no usable rule
- no manuscript voice

### Template Use Rule

Use templates to recover the manuscript's structure, not to flatten every paragraph into sameness.

If the draft already sounds like this book, keep the draft.
If it drifts, rebuild it from one of these shapes.
