## Battles & Sieges

> *The field smelled wrong before anyone crossed it. Damp earth and week-old ash and the thing underneath both — that particular rot you only understand once. Three hundred men on the left flank and none of them would look at the ground. They watched the tree line instead. They watched the hill. They watched each other.*
>
> *Brec had marched eight days for this. His boots had failed at the third river crossing and he'd wrapped his feet in canvas stripped from a dead man's pack. He did not think about the dead man. He thought about the hill, and the men on it, and whether the archers on their right could reach the crest before the cavalry found the gap. He had been told the cavalry would not find the gap. He did not believe this.*
>
> *"Move when the horn sounds," said the officer behind him. The officer had a horse and clean boots and had never, to Brec's knowledge, been required to use either to kill someone. Brec said nothing. The man beside him — a dwarf named Galdur who smelled of iron and whose helmet was too large and sat permanently crooked — said nothing either.*
>
> *The horn sounded.*
>
> *Brec moved.*

### What This Chapter Is

War is rare in the Ravenlands. The land is too poor, the settlements too scattered, and the armies too hungry to sustain open conflict for long. Most disputes end in raids, ambushes, or the kind of brutality that doesn't require flanking maneuvers.

But sometimes a stronghold changes hands. Sometimes a warlord assembles enough bodies to call what he has an army. Sometimes the Rust Church sends its brothers in force, or a dwarf hold finally runs out of patience with the orcs at the pass. In those moments, the rules for a single fight between a handful of adventurers no longer serve.

This chapter gives the Gamemaster tools to run battles — open-field engagements between organized forces — and sieges, where one side holds walls and the other tries to bring them down. It operates at the level of troops, not individual combatants, abstracting the chaos of hundreds of soldiers into dice pools that the characters can still influence through their individual action.

The rules build in order. Begin with army structure. Then move to how troops fight. Then sieges. Then the long campaign that starves armies before the enemy does. The Talents, example units, and stronghold rules follow. At the end, the Battle Events tables give the Gamemaster material to make each fight a scene rather than a calculation.

When combat between individual characters becomes necessary in the middle of a larger engagement, return to the normal combat rules in Chapter 5.

### To Run a Battle Tonight

Use this order for a quick battle preparation:

1. Build each side into troops and place them in left, center, and right sections.
2. Note reserves, commanders, weapon types, protection, and starting advantages.
3. Roll **INSIGHT** for deployment and set the final ground.
4. Roll the general's speech and divide morale points.
5. Resolve any ranged approach turns before melee contact.
6. Run the battle turn loop until the fight is decided.
7. End the battle on rout, retreat, surrender, or nightfall.
8. Then handle prisoners, wounded, plunder, occupation, supply, and the rest.

The rest of the chapter expands this path for sieges, campaign pressure, and the hard work that follows the clash.

### The Order of Battle

> *The Alderlander war-captain Torkel had twenty years of wars behind him and he arranged his troops the same way every time. Heavy infantry in the center to hold. Light men on the wings to bend without breaking. Skirmishers back far enough to shoot without catching a cavalry charge in the side. He did not explain this to anyone. He expected competent subordinates to read his intention from the disposition of the lines.*
>
> *The problem with this campaign was that no one under him was competent, and the problem before that was that he'd run out of money to hire the ones who were.*

Every army enters a battle with an **order of battle** — a deliberate arrangement of its forces into lines and sections that shapes how those forces fight. This is not an abstract formation. It is the fundamental tool of pre-battle planning, and getting it wrong before a sword is drawn costs more men than getting it wrong during the fighting.

#### Game Time

As in the original rules, battles are resolved in 15-minute increments, meaning each die roll represents what occurred during one turn.

At the end of each turn, after applying any modifiers corresponding to decisions and events, the opposing sides will each make a roll for each part of their order of battle, for a total of three rolls.

If it becomes necessary to focus on what happens to one or more particular characters, the flow of time shifts back to normal combat rounds.

#### Army Lines

Lines represent the depth of an army on the battlefield. They allow for staggered tactics, covering fronts in response to enemy movements, relieving exhausted troops, and protecting the rear. Primarily, they prevent hundreds of warriors from crowding the front line — which would be chaotic and counterproductive. More bodies than the line can absorb do not improve it. They clog it.

The **first line** is the vanguard — the troops in active combat at the start of the battle. Any troops beyond the first line are **reserve lines**, held back and not counted in each side's battle rolls. They wait for their turn: to replace broken troops, to exploit a gap, or to form a second defense if the first shatters.

The maximum number of dice a troop can gain from its size is **+5**. Any soldiers beyond that threshold belong in a second or third line. Since infantry reach this cap at 100 soldiers — one base die per 20 — a force of 150 infantry would fill one line with 100 and place the remaining 50 in a reserve line with 2 base dice. If this logic is not yet clear, it will become so once the troop dice rules are explained.

Given the relatively small army sizes in the Ravenlands, most forces will never field more than one line — each line is also divided into sections, which further limits how many troops each side can effectively deploy. The rules account for the exception, not the expectation.

#### Battle Line Sections

Each battle line divides into three sections: **left**, **center**, and **right**. Each section engages the opposing section across from it. Each turn, both sides roll once per section — three rolls per side, per turn.

A group of soldiers assigned to a single section is called a **troop**. The maximum base dice a troop can have from sheer numbers is +5. The maximum troop size varies by type.

For any rule that refers to an adjacent section, the center is adjacent to both wings. Each wing is adjacent only to the center.

Under normal circumstances, each section fights the section directly opposite it. If one section destroys or routs its opponent, it pivots inward to attack the nearest surviving enemy section, gaining a numerical advantage.

#### Troop Replacement

Frontline troops do not absorb individual reinforcements from the rear. The movement of single soldiers between different chains of command disrupts formations and erases the cohesion that makes a trained troop effective.

The real decision is when to relieve a battered troop entirely. A commander orders a frontline troop to withdraw; the next troop in that section moves up in the following turn. The withdrawn troop, if not destroyed, may regroup in the rear. The general may order it back at the start of any subsequent turn — no roll required — but it cannot fight on the same turn it advances.

If a troop is **destroyed** — its last base die eliminated by damage — it is gone. No regrouping, no return. Its men are dead or fled.

#### Committing Reserves

At the start of a turn, before the battle roll, the general may commit one reserve troop from a section's rear line.

A reserve committed to a section does one of two things:

- If the frontline troop still stands, the reserve relieves it and takes that section's next battle roll. The withdrawn troop falls back to the rear and may not fight again that turn.
- If the frontline troop was destroyed, routed, or withdrawn on the previous turn, the reserve moves up and holds the section for this turn.

A reserve cannot be committed to a section and also be held for another purpose in the same turn. Once committed, it is part of the line.

If a frontline troop is destroyed before it can make its own attack roll, and a reserve already waits in that section's rear line, the general may throw that reserve in at once. The reserve makes that section's roll in the ruined troop's place, but with 1 fewer advantage die on that roll. Fresh soldiers can close a gap. They do not do it in perfect order.

If a section was emptied on the previous turn and no reserve is committed to replace it on the following turn, that section is conceded. The enemy controls that ground and may pivot inward at the start of the turn.

#### Troop Uniformity

A troop must be composed of soldiers sharing the same general fighting characteristics. Infantry unit mixed with cavalry does not produce cavalry — it produces confusion. Troops of different types form their own sections. The exception is the **Important Character**, who joins any troop as its leader or champion regardless of composition.

### Troop Dice

> *Galdur explained it later, sitting against a tree with his helmet finally off and a bandage around his right hand.*
>
> *"It's not about how many of you there are. It's about how many of you there are who are worth counting."*

Every troop in combat has three categories of dice: **base dice**, **advantage dice**, and **protection dice**. Each type uses the same six-sided die, but their successes work differently. Separate them by color when possible.

Base dice come from numbers. Advantage dice come from quality, position, and circumstance. Protection dice come from shields, armor, and similar gear.

#### Base Dice

Base dice come from the troop's size. The threshold to gain one base die depends on the troop type. Maximum base dice is **5**.

| TYPE | UNIT THRESHOLD | MAX UNITS | DAILY COST (per unit) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Infantry | 20 | 100 | 5 copper |
| Skirmishers | 20 | 100 | 5 copper |
| Cavalry | 5 | 25 | 25 copper |
| Monster | 1 | 5 | — |

**Monster** units are large, dangerous creatures with monster attacks. Skeletons, ghouls, and other creatures that operate as standard characters belong in infantry or skirmisher categories.

**Cost** is daily supply — food, water, fodder. It doubles if soldiers also receive wages. Most armies in the Ravenlands do not pay coin during a campaign. They pay in plunder, or in the promise of plunder.

The GM may still note the troop's exact muster in soldiers or beasts. Battle does not count those bodies one by one. It counts full fighting groups. One base die normally stands for twenty infantry or skirmishers, five cavalry, or one monster. In siege defense, infantry and skirmishers use the tighter wall count of ten per base die instead. A troop keeps its current base dice as long as enough soldiers remain to fill those groups. When its remaining strength falls below the next full group, it loses one base die. Men removed this way may be dead, maimed, scattered, or too shaken to keep their place in the line. The rule tracks fighting strength, not a burial tally.

#### Advantage Dice

Advantage dice represent the troop's edge in the clash — better training, stronger position, or some other battlefield advantage. Roll them with the troop. Their successes cancel enemy advantage successes first. Any successes left after that become the troop's edge in the clash and are spent under **Pressing the Advantage** below. Advantage dice can never exceed the troop's base dice. Each qualifying characteristic below grants +1 advantage die, up to a maximum of +5.

| ADVANTAGE | DICE |
|---|---:|
| Well-trained (3+ months training, or professional soldiers) | +1 |
| Orc or Dwarf infantry | +1 |
| Misgrown infantry | +2 |
| Elven skirmishers | +2 |
| Fervor (religious, racial, or deep ancestral hatred) | +1 |
| Important Character present | +1 |
| Larger units fighting smaller units | +1 |
| Flanking attack | +1 |
| Rear attack | +2 |
| Fighting for survival (cornered, no retreat possible) | +1 |

**Well-trained** means a minimum of three months of dedicated military training, or a lifetime spent in combat. Professional soldiers, mercenaries, and warriors whose rank depends on their fighting ability qualify. Peasants conscripted last week do not.

**Orc or Dwarf infantry** are stronger and more enduring than comparable human soldiers under the physical and psychological pressure of extended battle. This advantage applies only when they form the whole or clear majority of the troop.

**Misgrown infantry** carry supernatural qualities that make them harder to kill, less predictable, and more frightening to face. Troops fighting Misgrown for the first time are rattled before the first blow lands.

**Elven skirmishers** combine exceptional long-range vision with the physical precision that makes their volleys more accurate and better coordinated than comparable human archers.

**Important Character present** applies not only to PCs but to any figure in the story with a name and a history — including antagonists, rival commanders, and significant NPCs. A general the army knows and trusts is worth an advantage die. So is a warlord the enemy has been trying to kill for two campaigns.

**Larger units** fighting smaller ones gain the die. Cavalry fighting infantry. Monsters fighting cavalry. A goblin on a wolf may deny a size advantage die to a human on foot, because the wolf closes the size gap. Size must be judged honestly against the units present.

**Fervor** requires genuine, consuming hatred or faith — the Rust Brothers against Elves and Orcs, Galdanes against anything with a demonic taint. Minor rivalries do not qualify.

**Flanking** applies when a section has defeated its opponent and turns to strike an adjacent section from the side. It may also arise from narrative circumstances the Gamemaster judges appropriate.

**Rear** applies when a troop attacks an enemy already engaged from the front. It requires a deliberate maneuver, not just arriving from behind.

**Fighting for survival** applies when a troop faces certain annihilation with no route of retreat — cornered troops, soldiers who have been encircled, defenders of the final gate. They are not brave. They are out of options. The effect is the same.

#### Veterans

A troop that has survived 3 or more engagements intact — not rebuilt, not folded into another force, but the same soldiers who went in — becomes Veterans. They have learned what training does not teach.

**Advantage dice upgrade.** Veterans substitute their advantage dice with dice one step higher on the ladder: D6 → D8 → D10 → D12. A troop with D8 advantage dice needs 3 more engagements to reach D10. This upgrade reflects accuracy, timing, and survival habit at the unit level. The base dice are unchanged — Veterans still need numbers to field strength.

**First morale check immunity.** Veterans automatically pass the first morale check they would otherwise make each engagement. They have seen worse. After the first check, morale loss applies as normal.

**Commander loss.** When a Veterans troop's Important Character is killed or removed from the field, the troop must make a Formidable morale check. These soldiers knew that officer. The loss is not just tactical.

**Earned, not purchased.** Veterans cannot be recruited as a starting quality. They must be earned through play. The GM tracks which allied troops have reached Veterans status and when they last saw battle.

### Arms and Armor

Troop dice tell you how many soldiers stand, how sharp their edge is, and how much punishment they can take. Arms and armor decide how that force fights.

#### Weapon Type

Each troop carries one weapon type. Weapon type does not grant base dice. It decides when the troop attacks, what targets it handles well, and whether it can strike before contact or from a distance. Switching weapons at the start of a battle turn is permitted — mounted troops may dismount and become infantry, changing their base dice accordingly.

| WEAPON | EFFECTS | ADDED COST |
|---|---|---:|
| Unarmed | Attacks second | 0 |
| Natural weapon or short weapon (dagger, claws) | Attacks second. Attack +2 vs. polearms | 0 |
| One-handed weapon | — | 0 |
| Two-handed weapon | Attack +1 vs. infantry and skirmishers | 10 |
| Polearm | Attacks first. Attack +2 vs. cavalry. Two-handed | 5 |
| Thrown weapon | Free attack before melee. Attack +1 vs. cavalry | 5 |
| Light ranged weapon (shortbow, sling) | Range 1. Two-handed. Attacks second. Demoralized in melee | 5 |
| Longbow | Range 2. Two-handed. Attacks second. Demoralized in melee | 10 |
| Crossbow | Range 1. Attack +1. Attacks second. Two-handed. Demoralized in melee | 10 |

**Attacks First:** The troop rolls its dice before the enemy. If both troops have Attacks First, neither does — roll simultaneously.

**Attacks Second:** The enemy rolls first. The troop absorbs casualties before responding.

**Free Attack:** Before entering melee, the troop makes one attack roll without enemy retaliation. This uses no action slot and costs no morale. It can be used once per battle per instance of this ability, either when advancing or when the enemy advances.

**Range X:** Adds X extra turns before the advancing enemy troop reaches melee range. Only applies to troops in motion toward close combat. Troops with Range cannot attack enemy rear lines. Skirmisher troops have Range by default.

**Two-handed:** Cannot carry a shield or thrown weapons simultaneously.

**Demoralized:** The troop cannot spend morale points to absorb demoralization results while this condition applies. Skirmisher troops forced into melee always trigger this.

**Attack +X:** Additional dice added as if advantage dice but not subject to the base dice cap.

**Cost:** Weapon costs are added to the unit's daily maintenance per soldier. An infantry troop of 100 soldiers armed with polearms costs 1,000 copper per day before wages. Maintaining an army is its own campaign.

#### Protection Type

Most fighters in the Ravenlands wear cloth or rough leather and carry whatever shield they could find or steal. The gap between equipped professional soldiers and armed peasants is not subtle.

| PROTECTION | EFFECTS | ADDED COST |
|---|---|---:|
| Small shield | Protection 1. Protection 2 vs. projectiles | 0 |
| Large shield | Protection 2. Protection 3 vs. thrown. Protection 4 vs. projectiles. Morale +1 | 5 |
| Leather armor | Protection 2. Morale +1 | 5 |
| Chainmail | Protection 4. Protection 2 vs. projectiles and polearms. Protection 1 vs. blunt and thrown. Morale +2 | 10 |
| Plate armor | Protection 6. Morale +4. Slow 1 | 25 |

**Protection X:** Extra dice rolled to absorb damage. Each success reduces damage by 1. Rolls of 1 have no additional effect on protection dice. Protection dice may be rolled even when the troop is not directly engaged — for instance, against skirmisher fire.

**Slow X:** Increases the Range rating of enemy troops by X when approaching this troop, because this troop advances slowly enough that it can be peppered longer.

**Morale +X:** Added to the troop's starting morale for the battle, due to the confidence that good armor provides.

### The Battle Sequence

#### Screening and Pickets

Before deployment, an army with cavalry, skirmishers, or another troop suited to scouting may assign one troop to screen the line and keep pickets on the likely approaches. The troop still fights where it was assigned once the battle begins.

If both sides do this, both commanders roll **SCOUTING**. If only one side screens, that side wins automatically.

The winner chooses one:

- gain +1 bonus die on the coming **INSIGHT** deployment roll
- shift one troop one section left or right after deployment but before the speech
- deny the enemy any benefit from hidden approach or uncertain ground on the first turn

An army that kept proper pickets through the dark can never be treated as having no sentries for a night attack unless those pickets were first scattered, bribed, or killed.

#### Before The Battle: Deployment

The generals face each other across the field they did not choose. One of them reads it better.

Both sides organize their troops before the battle begins. Infantry, cavalry, and skirmishers sort into separate troops. Troops with different advantages should not share a section if those advantages cannot apply to all soldiers — any advantage that does not apply to every soldier in that troop is lost.

The exception is an Important Character. They may join any troop regardless of its composition, acting as its champion, commander, or leader. In this case, however, the character sacrifices all their own trait-based advantages to contribute — the troop gains the single advantage die for having an Important Character present, and no more from the character.

Once organized, both generals make an **INSIGHT** roll. The winner chooses the exact ground and positions their troops after seeing the enemy's disposition — a significant advantage. Ties re-roll.

If one army is large enough to field more than one line, this must be declared before deployment. It cannot be a surprise.

#### The General's Speech

After deployment and the **INSIGHT** contest, but before the first ranged exchange or battle roll, each general speaks to their troops. This can be brief — a hard sentence, a name repeated — or long enough to make the enemy wonder what is being said. Roll **PERFORMANCE**. Each success grants the army's troops a combined pool of **morale points** — see Morale Points below for how they work — split among them as the general sees fit. Push the roll if the situation demands it — each result of 1 on any pushed die removes 1 morale point from any one troop of the general's choice.

Most generals weight the center first, or whichever section must decide the day. Morale spread too thin across the whole line is easier to lose than morale piled where the line is expected to bend hardest.

A general without the words for this may delegate to a Named Man or subordinate commander, but that commander becomes the one rolling.

> *Senna was not a soldier. She was a crier's daughter who knew the difference between the words that move people and the words that only sound like them. She stood in front of the center line with the morning cold and gave Torkel's speech because Torkel had not spoken before a battle in eleven years — since the second campaign, since the pause where he was still finding the next sentence and a man in the fourth row laughed.*
>
> *Her voice carried clean through the frost. When she was done, Torkel sent the stoutest share of that new steadiness to the center, where the line would have to stand and take the worst of it. Galdur got some on the left. Idris got some on the right. Idris did not need much. Torkel gave it to him anyway because Idris had held the ford ambush and this was how you kept men loyal without coins.*

#### The Challenge

Before deployment, a commander or champion may issue a formal challenge to the opposing side — single combat to settle the dispute without a battle, or at minimum to demonstrate the force of will behind the line.

Both sides must agree. If the challenged side refuses, the side that refused loses 1D3 morale points across all their troops. The soldiers who watched their leader decline remember it.

**If accepted:** The duel resolves in standard personal combat using the normal rules from Chapter 5. The winner's side gains morale points equal to the victor's current Strength attribute distributed however the general chooses. The loser's side loses the same number. After the duel, the battle still occurs — a champion's duel is theater as much as resolution, and rarely ends wars.

**PC champions.** A PC who accepts may substitute another relevant skill for MELEE — an archer might request an archery duel, a diplomat a verbal contest decided by MANIPULATION. The opposing commander may accept or reject the alternate format. The morale stakes apply to either format regardless.

This section may be skipped entirely at the GM's discretion. Not every conflict has leaders willing to put their bodies before their armies.

> *The man Vidar sent out was called Haerek. He was very large and he stood in the killing ground between the two armies with a white flag and enough patience to wait for an answer.*
>
> *Torkel declined. The refusal ran the line like cold water. Not enough to break men, but enough to make them shift their feet and look aside. You could see it: those nearest to Torkel stopped leaning forward. Laughter from the Krymark wall carried.*
>
> *That evening, Galdur said: "You should have sent me."*
>
> *"You would have lost," Torkel said.*
>
> *"Maybe. But they would have watched a dwarf stand in front of a man twice his size and not move. That's worth more than hearing them laugh from the wall."*
>
> *Torkel had no answer for this and did not pretend to have one, which was one of the things that made him a good general.*

#### Battle Turns

Each battle turn represents fifteen minutes of fighting. At the end of each turn, engaged troops roll their dice simultaneously — unless Attacks First, Attacks Second, or Range effects alter that sequence.

During the turn, the general and troop commanders may issue orders, important characters may move, and any pending special effects resolve before the roll.

#### Turn Order

Run each battle turn in this order:

1. Move generals and Important Characters.
2. Commit reserves or relieve battered troops.
3. Attempt **REGROUP** rolls for fleeing groups.
4. Resolve special effects that happen before the clash — returning feigned retreats, battle magic, weather changes, and similar effects.
5. Resolve ranged approach turns, free attacks, and any **Attacks First / Attacks Second** sequence.
6. Roll each engaged section.
7. Apply protection, damage, morale loss, and demoralization.
8. Decide pursuit, pivot, reserve advance, or retreat for any section whose fight has now been decided.

If a timing dispute arises, earlier steps happen before later ones.

#### The Battle Roll

Each engaged troop assembles its dice and rolls:

Before counting damage, apply any weapon properties that affect the clash. **Attacks First**, **Attacks Second**, **Free Attack**, and **Range** decide whether the roll happens now, earlier, or after the enemy. Any **Attack +X** from weapons is added now. These dice count as advantage dice for the roll, but are not limited by the troop's base dice.

**Base successes** inflict 1 point of damage on the opposing troop per success. Each damage point reduces the enemy troop's base dice by 1 — those soldiers are dead or too broken to fight.

**Advantage successes** are compared against the enemy's advantage successes. They cancel each other out first. Any successes left after that are the section's edge. Spend that edge on one or more effects from **Pressing the Advantage** below.

**Protection successes** cancel incoming damage, one success per point of damage.

**Demoralization** — rolls of 1 on base dice — forces morale checks. Each result of 1 represents a group of soldiers wavering or fleeing.

Resolve each section's clash separately. The concept of simultaneous rolls reflects the reality that both troops are cutting and dying at the same moment.

> *Vidar's first sortie out of Krymark's south gate came on the third morning, when Holk's crew was still unloading timber for the catapult frame. Sixty infantry in chainmail and shields — the idea was to burn the equipment before it was built.*
>
> *Torkel's center was eighty soldiers with polearms and leather, with Galdur's thirty dwarves folded in to stiffen the line.*
>
> *Vidar sent sixty men in chainmail and shields, fast enough to reach the timber if no one stopped them.*
>
> *The first crash went harder for Torkel than Vidar expected. The center took the sortie in the mud, the dwarves locked the line, and men started dropping before they had reached the frame.*
>
> *By the time the first push had spent itself, near forty of Vidar's men were down, broken, or stumbling back toward the gate. A knot of Torkel's front rank had begun to waver under the return blows, but the courage Senna had put into them held. No one ran.*
>
> *Vidar's men still took a bite out of the center before they were thrown back. Twenty of Torkel's soldiers were gone from the line by the end of the first quarter hour.*
>
> *First fifteen minutes. The dwarves had not broken. The catapult would be built.*

#### Pressing the Advantage

A section with the edge does not always turn it straight into bodies. Sometimes it buys ground, time, disorder, or the one opening that changes the whole field.

After cancelling opposing advantage successes, spend any successes left as follows. Each named choice may be taken only once per section per turn. No section may choose more than one option that inflicts direct damage. Successes not spent are lost in the press of the fighting. A choice that refers to the next turn is lost if that section is no longer engaged when the next turn begins. The GM should favor the choices that fit the ground, the troop type, and the weapons in hand.

**Spend 1 success:**

- **Drive the blow home.** Inflict 1 point of damage on the enemy troop.
- **Press them back.** The enemy loses 1 advantage die on its next roll in this section.
- **Pin them in place.** The enemy cannot pivot or pursue from this section on its next turn.
- **Cut down the signalers.** The enemy loses 1 morale point.
- **Carry the hurt clear.** One friendly Broken Important Character or one fleeing group from this section reaches the rear in order.
- **Take the footing.** On corpses, rubble, mud, stairs, or other bad ground, the enemy attacks second on the next turn unless it already would.

**Spend 2 successes:**

- **Open a gap.** One adjacent friendly section gains 1 advantage die on its next roll against an already engaged enemy section.
- **Break their rotation.** The enemy cannot relieve this section with a reserve on its next turn.
- **Throw them off the work.** On a wall, breach lip, ford, ferry, bridgehead, gate, stair, or ladder-top, the enemy loses all position-based advantage dice in this section on its next turn.
- **Silence the standard.** The enemy loses its Important Character advantage die in this section on its next turn.
- **Disorder the shields.** The enemy halves its protection dice in this section on its next roll, rounded up.

**Spend 3 successes:**

- **Cut them to pieces.** Inflict 2 points of damage on the enemy troop. If this section is still engaged on the next turn, the enemy gains 1 advantage die against that troop as the line opens and order is lost.
- **Roll the flank.** One adjacent friendly section counts as making a flanking attack on its next roll if a route exists.
- **Panic the line.** The enemy section makes an immediate morale roll. If it fails, it loses 1 base die at once to flight.
- **Seize the stair or gate.** In siege, breach, ford, ferry, or bridgehead fighting, this section no longer grants **Attacks First** or position advantage to the enemy from the next turn onward unless fresh defenders reclaim it.
- **Ride through the rear.** Cavalry or skirmishing cavalry only. If a route exists behind this section and no baggage guard bars it, the troop may strike baggage or engines at once as though it had already won the section.

#### Taking Damage

Once both sides have rolled and spent their edge, total the damage coming into each troop.

Count 1 damage for each base success, then add any damage created by **Pressing the Advantage**. After that, roll protection dice. Each protection success cancels 1 damage. Each point of damage left removes 1 base die and 1 morale point.

If the troop rolls at least one protection success, reduce the morale loss from that exchange by 1, to a minimum of 0. A line that still trusts its shields or mail holds a little longer.

When a base die is removed, the troop loses one full fighting group: usually twenty infantry or skirmishers, five cavalry, or one monster, or ten defenders on a wall. If the GM keeps an exact muster, reduce it by that amount.

If neither side attacked first or second, both sides take their remaining damage from that exchange even if one troop is destroyed.

Use this same sequence for free attacks, ranged approach turns, and ordinary clashes. Then resolve demoralization from results of 1 on base dice.

#### Morale Points

A troop's morale points represent its collective will to hold. They come from the general's speech, from armor bonuses, and from the troop's own circumstances.

When a rule calls for a **morale roll**, roll a number of D6 equal to the troop's current morale points. Any 6 means the troop holds. No 6 means the roll failed. The rule that called for the roll tells you what the failure costs.

A troop with no morale points does not roll. It fails automatically.

When a troop rolls 1s on its base dice, each 1 triggers demoralization — one fleeing group breaks from the line. At battle scale, one fleeing group equals one base die's worth of soldiers for that troop type: twenty infantry or skirmishers, five cavalry, or one monster unit. The face value on the die is not counted as exact bodies. It marks the panic, not the headcount.

The troop can spend one morale point to nullify each result of 1. When morale points run out, the 1s are not nullified. Each unresolved result removes one base die and sends that fleeing group to the rear, where it may be rallied later.

Each point of damage that reduces a troop's base dice also reduces its morale points by 1. Losses grind down will as well as numbers.

**Death to Cowards.** The general may order rear-line troops to execute fleeing soldiers — cut them down before they reach safety. Each turn the order stands, up to 1D6 soldiers are killed. The count is added to neither side's base dice. They are simply gone.

When soldiers are executed this way, each remaining troop in the army makes a morale roll. Success: add 1 morale point. Failure: lose 1 morale point. Some soldiers watch the executions and decide the general is the greater threat to them. Others hold harder.

Within 1D3 days following a battle in which this order was given, roll one D6 for the army as a whole. On a result of 6+, the army's loyalty holds. On a lower result, the general loses 1D3 base dice worth of soldiers to desertion. Borrowed loyalty has a cost that comes due when the army is not in immediate danger.

This is a real order. It has been given. It works. What follows is a separate problem.

> *By the third push the center had spent what heart Senna had put into it. Then the line rippled and twenty soldiers went to the rear all at once. The rear-line troops let them go. Torkel had not given the Death to Cowards order.*
>
> *He had considered it. He decided that executing his own men in front of the survivors would cost him more in the next engagement than losing twenty men cost him in this one. This calculation was correct. He had not made it quickly, and the time it took said something about the kind of general he had been in his second campaign.*
>
> *The section held with a gap where another rank should have been. It held through two more hard exchanges, which was enough.*
>
> *Brec was in it for all five. Afterward he could not account clearly for turns three and four. He remembered the press, the man on his left going down, the man on his right filling the space without being told to. He did not remember the soldiers who ran. That was not cowardice. That was how the mind manages what it cannot use.*

#### No Quarter

A general may declare that no prisoners will be taken. The declaration is given before the first roll or at any point during the battle. It is announced across the line, relayed by officers, and cannot be quietly withdrawn once the fighting is underway.

**Effect on the enemy.** Any enemy troop that learns of the declaration gains the **Fighting for Survival** advantage die for the remainder of the battle. There is nowhere to go. Soldiers who cannot surrender fight harder than soldiers who can.

**Effect after the battle.** All enemy prisoners are executed unless the general explicitly reverses the order. Reversing it mid-battle requires a Demanding **PERFORMANCE** roll. On failure, the soldiers do not stop. They have been given permission and have no reason to hear a new order over the noise and momentum of what is already in motion.

**Future engagements.** The declaration is remembered. Enemy commanders who learn of it treat any offer of surrender from this force as worthless. Garrisoned defenders who know no quarter will be given fight to the last man rather than yielding a gate. The army that earned this reputation will not undo it by being generous once.

> *Torkel never gave the order. Vara had. On the third campaign, before he knew her well, he had watched her give it and watched what followed and decided it was one of the tools he would keep in reserve.*
>
> *The garrison at the north tower had held for eleven days. They had supplies. They had walls. They had twelve soldiers. Vara declared no quarter on the morning of the twelfth day — not in anger, simply because a negotiated surrender would take three more days at minimum and the campaign had four days of food left.*
>
> *The twelve soldiers heard the declaration. They stopped trying to hold the tower. They started trying to take people with them.*
>
> *The assault that should have cost four men cost nine. The twelve defenders died. So did nine of Vara's soldiers who had expected a tower with twelve defenders in it to be a matter of half an hour.*
>
> *Vara recorded the outcome in her accounts. She did not write that it had been a mistake.*

#### Troop Regrouping

At the start of each turn, commanders may attempt to rally fleeing troops. The general may make one **REGROUP** roll per turn — **PERFORMANCE** or **MANIPULATION**. Each success brings back one fleeing group and prepares it to fight again from the rear. Each result of 1 on this roll loses that group permanently.

Important characters acting as troop commanders may also roll separately — PERFORMANCE or MANIPULATION, the same as the general — but only if they are not actively fighting on the front line.

Fleeing troops that are not rallied within two turns of fleeing leave the battle permanently.

This roll may be pushed. However, any 1s rolled on skill dice during a push cannot be re-rolled — they count as results and remove fleeing groups permanently.

#### Pursuit

When a troop routes — its last base die fleeing or destroyed — the victorious side may pursue. Only cavalry or skirmishing cavalry can pursue effectively. Infantry cannot run down a routing force.

**The pursuit roll:** The pursuing cavalry troop rolls their full dice against the routing troop's remaining base dice. Each success removes soldiers from the field permanently. They cannot regroup, rally, or return.

**The cost:** A cavalry troop that pursues abandons the main battle line for that turn. They do not contribute to the main battle roll. If the line suffers a reversal while they are gone, they cannot reinforce in time.

Pursuit is a choice, not an obligation. The general who orders it wins that flank completely. They may also return to find the center broken.

> *Idris broke Vidar's cavalry on the right flank in the fourth turn. He ordered pursuit without asking Torkel.*
>
> *Idris's Aslene riders ran them down in the open and did not look back until Vidar's horse was gone — not retreating, not regrouping, simply scattered.*
>
> *Torkel's center took two damage in that turn without cavalry on the flank. Three men died in those fifteen minutes who would not have died if Idris had wheeled instead of chased. Idris did not learn about the three men until the following day. He would have made the same decision again. Whether that was correct is a question each general answers for themselves, ideally before the pursuit begins.*

#### Winning a Section

A troop that destroys or routes the troop directly opposite it has won that section. The victory matters at the start of the next turn, not in the middle of the same clash.

If the enemy commits a reserve to that section on the following turn, the section remains contested. The winning troop must face the fresh enemy before it can exploit the gap.

If no fresh enemy takes the section, the winning troop chooses one:

- **Hold the ground.** Stay in place, dress the line, and deny the section to the enemy.
- **Pivot inward.** Turn against the nearest engaged enemy section. This counts as a flanking attack on that turn.
- **Pursue.** If the defeated enemy routed and the troop is cavalry or skirmishing cavalry, pursue instead of pivoting.

If a troop wins a section and the adjacent enemy section is already engaged from the front, a commander who times the wheel cleanly may turn the flank into a rear attack on the following turn. The GM should allow this only when the route truly exists on the field.

#### Ordered Retreat

A general who chooses to withdraw — ceding the field rather than fighting to destruction — may order a full retreat. This is not defeat without decision. It is the use of what remains.

**The roll.** The general makes a Demanding **PERFORMANCE** roll. Each success holds one section in good order during withdrawal. Sections not held in order make a morale roll as they fall back — any dice removed are permanent losses. A section that routes during a retreat counts as destroyed for the battle's outcome.

**If no successes are rolled:** The retreat collapses. Every section makes a morale roll immediately. The army is no longer retreating. It is routing. The only difference is whether the general is still trying.

**Effect.** The army abandons the field. The enemy holds the ground and may pursue. The retreating army loses all accumulated morale points but retains its surviving troops. An army that retreats in order may regroup, resupply, and fight again. An army that routes requires 1D6 days before surviving troops can be organized into coherent sections.

**Rearguard.** Any section may be sacrificed as a rearguard. The rearguard holds the field and makes battle rolls at the normal rate against any pursuing enemy while the rest of the army withdraws. The rearguard does not retreat with the army. What survives of it — alive, captured, or scattered — is the GM's judgment.

> *On the second engagement, Torkel called the retreat at the end of turn five.*
>
> *The order was heard cleanly by three sections — cavalry first, center infantry second, the left flank walking backward with shields raised. Galdur's dwarves on the left had not heard the signal in time. By the time the order reached them through the noise, men had already been cut down or scattered from the line.*
>
> *Torkel left a rearguard of thirty infantry at the tree line. He did not explain what he was asking — a rearguard that understands its situation too clearly sometimes runs before the cavalry is clear. He told them to hold until the horn sounded twice.*
>
> *Eight of the rearguard reached camp that evening. Twenty-two did not. Their names were recorded. The surviving eight were given Torkel's ration of wine, which was not much, but was everything he had available to give.*

#### Victory and Defeat

A battle does not end when someone decides it has. It ends when one of these conditions is met.

**Rout.** All of one side's active troops in the first line have been destroyed or broken. Reserve lines, if any, must advance on the following turn or the army has conceded the field by inaction.

**Concession.** The losing general orders a retreat or sends word to the enemy accepting defeat. A general who concedes before total destruction preserves more. One who waits too long may not have anything left to concede with.

**Night.** A battle running past sundown without a torch arrangement (see Night Attack) ends by mutual exhaustion. Neither side holds more than it held at dusk. Casualties stand. A night pause is not a concession — the battle resumes the following morning unless one side withdraws during the dark.

**Siege fall.** The defenders no longer hold any wall section connecting the interior to the exterior, or they formally surrender. A fortress with a breached gate and enemy troops in the courtyard but defenders still on the inner wall is not yet taken.

The side that holds the field controls it. They collect prisoners, strip the dead, and make the decisions that come after.

#### Surrender

A commander who wishes to end the fighting without total destruction may offer surrender.

**Field surrender.** A general who raises a white banner ceases all troop rolls on their side. Their troops hold position. The enemy general decides whether to accept or continue the assault. An enemy who has declared No Quarter may not accept. An enemy with reasons to want a live surrender — ransom, political leverage, intelligence — typically does.

**Garrison surrender.** A besieged commander who sends a messenger requesting terms is offering to surrender the fortress. Both sides cease active operations during negotiation. The blockade continues. Terms are negotiated over 1D3 days. If negotiations fail, the battle resumes from the point it stopped.

**Terms.** The victor sets them. Minimum: all weapons surrendered, prisoners transferred, stronghold handed over intact. Additional terms are leverage — extracted from the gap between what the surrendering side needs and what they cannot refuse.

**After the inner wall breaks.** A garrison that refuses to surrender after the outer walls are breached and enemy troops are inside the courtyard may be offered no further terms at the GM's discretion. The attacker is now inside. Negotiation at that point is noise. For surrender negotiations during an ongoing siege, see Surrender and Parley under Sieges.

### Special Combat Conditions

#### Ranged Troops and Range

Troops with Range 1 or Range 2 attack the enemy before melee contact. This adds extra battle turns played before the battle — during those turns, only ranged troops attack. Their targets may roll protection dice and use advantage dice only to cancel the skirmishers' advantage dice. They cannot retaliate.

The Range effect applies only to advancing troops. Skirmisher troops should not fire into a general melee with allied troops already engaged — there is a real chance of striking friends.

**Skirmisher morale.** Skirmisher troops firing from distance — not yet forced into melee and not being targeted by enemy skirmishers in a battle roll — results of 1 on their base dice do not trigger demoralization. They are not in immediate danger. The moment they are being engaged directly by other skirmishers or forced into melee, morale loss applies as normal.

**Exception:** Skirmishers on the flank or rear of an enemy troop already engaged may fire freely. There are no allies in that arc.

**Friendly Fire.** Skirmisher volleys lack precision. If skirmishers fire into a general melee already involving allied troops, each result of 1 on their base dice inflicts 1 point of damage on one allied troop in that melee instead of the enemy. The ally rolls protection as normal. If more than one allied troop is exposed in that section, the GM assigns the hit to the troop most exposed. The exception above does not apply here — arc-clear fire from the flank is safe; firing into a melee your own side is in is not.

#### Skirmisher Troops in Melee

Skirmisher troops are trained to avoid hand-to-hand fighting. If forced into it, they apply the Demoralized condition — their morale points no longer absorb results of 1 on base dice. The enemy's swords are already in their faces.

**Skirmishing Cavalry.** Cavalry troops with ranged weapons are different. They can shoot and ride clear, keeping distance as a weapon. This **mobility advantage** means they do not suffer Attacks Second or Demoralized while firing — unless the enemy surpasses them in successes on a battle roll. If that happens, the enemy has closed the distance and is among them. They lose mobility advantage immediately and must apply their weapon penalties.

Mobility advantage recovers if the cavalry outscores the enemy in a subsequent turn — they have broken contact and re-established their range.

#### Commander Movement

At the start of each turn, an Important Character may move to any troop in any section or rank, granting that troop their advantage die. The battlefield is large, but a commander on horseback can reach anywhere in one turn.

A commander who moves to rally fleeing troops in the rear may not return to the front line in the same turn.

**Commander flight.** If a general's own troop is destroyed or routes, the general must make a Hard **INSIGHT** roll to avoid routing with them. On failure, the general flees with the troop. On success, they may transfer immediately to any adjacent surviving section.

A general who has fled — by failure or choice — removes their contribution from every troop in the army immediately: all morale points granted by the general's speech at the start of the battle are lost from every section, and the "Important Character present" advantage die is lost from every section. The army fights what remains of the battle without its organizing intelligence.

An Important Character who is not the general but whose troop is destroyed is Broken and removed from play for the remainder of the engagement.

#### Terrain

The ground beneath an army's feet changes the fight. Before deployment, the GM assigns a terrain type to each section of the battlefield based on the actual ground.

**High Ground.** A troop deployed on elevated terrain — a hill, a ridge, a raised embankment — gains 1 advantage die. Cavalry descending from high ground in a charge gains an additional D6 for that turn only. Cavalry charging uphill loses 1 base die.

**Muddy or Wet Ground.** Waterlogged soil or heavy rain slows heavy forces. Cavalry troops rolling on wet ground reduce their base dice by 1. Infantry and skirmishers are unaffected.

**Forest or Broken Ground.** Cavalry cannot fight effectively in dense woodland, marsh, or rocky scree. Any cavalry troop ordered to advance through broken ground loses its attack roll entirely that turn. Infantry and skirmishers move freely.

**River Crossing.** A troop advancing across a river or contested ford while under fire uses half their base dice (rounded up) on the crossing turn only — a crossing takes one battle turn. Once across, they roll normally.

**Prepared Ground.** A defending general who has held position for 24 hours may prepare the ground in front of their line with stakes, pitfalls, or abatis. Cavalry attacking into prepared ground loses 2 base dice on the attack roll. Infantry attacks are unaffected — prepared ground is an anti-cavalry measure.

**Field Works.** An army that holds position for 3 or more days and has an **ENGINEER** troop assigned to the task may construct field works: a light trench backed by a timber or earth palisade. Engineers assigned to field works may not perform other engineering tasks during those 3 days.

Field works grant the defending line **+1 advantage die** against infantry attacks in addition to any other terrain bonus. They also include the anti-cavalry stakes effect — an army behind field works automatically has Prepared Ground without the 24-hour requirement. A line behind field works is harder to assault and slower to break. It is also committed ground: a force behind field works cannot execute an Ordered Retreat without abandoning the works and losing any benefit.

If the army must move before the 3 days are complete, the partial works provide no benefit.

> *Galdur's dwarves held the left flank for three days while Torkel negotiated with Idris over the cavalry's terms for the next engagement. No order to dig was given. Dwarves in a static position for three days find the ground worth listening to. By the time Idris arrived, the left flank was behind a knee-high earthwork and a rough palisade of stripped saplings.*
>
> *Torkel inspected it on the morning of the fourth day. He said nothing about it to anyone, which was the way he acknowledged work done without being asked.*

Terrain is not secret. Both generals see the same ground. What matters is who uses it.

> *Before Krymark's south field, Torkel held his line back until he had seen where Vidar meant to stand. Vidar committed first. Torkel read the ground better.*
>
> *Vidar's infantry held the ridge above the ford. Anyone advancing on them went uphill and through wet ground. Torkel accepted that for the center. He sent Idris's cavalry to the right, where the ground was flat and open. He moved Galdur's dwarves to the left, where the marsh would slow horses but not infantry. He accepted that the center would bleed climbing the ridge and arranged the wings to make that bleeding worthwhile.*
>
> *Idris descended from the far ridge at a gallop in the second exchange, coming downhill with the whole slope under his horses. It was not a subtle maneuver. The right flank of Vidar's line had never faced Aslene cavalry on open ground before and it showed in the way they received the charge, which was badly.*

#### Weather

Roll a D6 at the start of each battle. On a result of 1, the weather is adverse. Roll a second D6 to determine the condition.

| D6 | CONDITION |
|---|---|
| 1 | Heavy rain or snow. All ranged attacks lose 1 die. Cavalry movement reduces to infantry rate |
| 2 | Dense fog. Both sides roll only half their advantage dice (round up). Flanking and rear attack advantage dice do not apply on the first turn |
| 3 | Bitter cold. Every troop begins with 1 fewer morale point. Accumulated demoralization triggers on results of 1 and 2 immediately, even on the first turn |
| 4 | Strong crosswind. Ranged attacks with light ranged weapons lose 1 die if firing into the wind; gain 1 die if firing with it. Direction determined by the GM |
| 5 | Dust or sand. As fog, but begins on the second turn and clears at the start of the fourth |
| 6 | Unseasonable heat. After 3 battle turns, every troop loses 1 base die to exhaustion, regardless of casualties |

Weather in open battle re-rolls at the start of each new day. Siege conditions do not change mid-siege unless the GM marks a seasonal shift.

#### Night Attack

A general may launch an assault between sundown and dawn. Night attacks are almost always the attacker's initiative — defenders have warning only if they maintain sentries.

**No sentries:** The attacker gains a free first turn. The defending side does not roll that turn — they are not yet assembled. From the second turn, the battle continues normally.

**Sentries present:** Both sides roll from the first turn, but advantage dice are halved (rounded up) for both sides for the first 2 turns. Night is chaos for everyone, including the attackers.

**Torchlight.** A defending force that has lit torches and braziers removes the advantage dice penalty for themselves only. The attackers moving into lit ground are easier to target — the defenders regain their full advantage dice from the second turn onward.

To advance troops in darkness without disorganization, the attacking general must make a Commanding **SCOUTING** roll before initiating the attack. On failure, the attacking force loses 1D3 base dice to confusion before the first roll. On success, the attack proceeds cleanly.

> *The night attack on the south wall was Senna's idea. She acknowledged she was wrong about it before Torkel did, which was the quickest she had ever been wrong about anything.*
>
> *Torkel got most of the center to the rally point, but not all. Forty men never found it. They were in the wrong field when Krymark's sentries heard a horse shift and raised the alarm.*
>
> *Torches lit inside two minutes. After that the men on the wall could see clearly enough while Torkel's soldiers were still feeling their way in the dark. The attackers went in thinned, half-blind, and colder than they should have been, after an hour spent walking in the wrong direction.*
>
> *No section fell. Three ladders burned. Holk recorded the ladders in his accounts and mentioned them at every meeting afterward, not in anger, simply as a number that needed to appear somewhere.*

#### Feigned Retreat

A cavalry or skirmisher troop may execute a feigned retreat — appearing to break, drawing the enemy forward, then wheeling to strike. The maneuver requires an Important Character present in the troop.

**The roll:** The commanding Important Character or troop commander makes an Opposed roll — their **PERFORMANCE** against the opposing general's **INSIGHT**. On success: the troop withdraws in apparent disorder and may re-enter combat at the start of the next turn, gaining a free D8 pursuit die that is not subject to the base dice cap. On failure: the retreat becomes genuine. The troop must make a morale roll this turn, and if they lose morale points, those losses count as if the troop had fled.

Feigned retreat may be attempted once per troop per battle. Infantry cannot execute this maneuver except in exceptional circumstances the GM judges plausible.

> *Vara commanded the Aslene skirmishing cavalry on Torkel's right in the second engagement. When Vidar's cavalry pressed her riders back, she let them press — just raggedly enough to look like a line about to break. The officer following her was Vidar's subordinate, not Vidar, and he had more enthusiasm than patience.*
>
> *Vara sold the lie and he bought it. She wheeled at the treeline, her riders already drawing. The cavalry pursuing her took a volley they had not anticipated and then Idris's flanking attack, which they had anticipated even less.*
>
> *The first shock of her turn cut deeper than it should have. The cavalry section she hit was already wounded from the volley. It was done in two turns.*
>
> *Her father had taught her the maneuver with one rule: use it only when you are already certain, not when you need the outcome to save you. She had used it twice in her career. Both times she had been certain. This was the second time.*

#### Ambush

An ambush is not a battle. It is an arrangement — one side already in position, the other in transit without knowing what is waiting. It requires prepared ground, patience, and an enemy that does not know it is coming.

**Setting an ambush.** The attacking commander must hold a concealment advantage: forest, broken ground, a ravine, a prepared ruin, or any terrain that hides an army from a marching column. Before the target enters the kill ground, the attacking general makes a **SCOUTING** roll:

- **2+ successes:** The ambush is set. The target is unaware.
- **1 success:** The position is imperfect. The attacker does not gain the free first turn but doubles all advantage dice on the first turn.
- **0 successes:** The target notices signs. Both sides deploy as a standard engagement with no advantage.

**The surprise turn.** If the ambush succeeds, the ambushing force gets a free first turn. The ambushed side does not roll — they are not yet assembled. At the end of the surprise turn, the ambushed troops make a Demanding morale roll: each die that shows a 1 is removed permanently. From the second turn onward, the battle continues normally.

**If the target has scouts.** Before entering suspected ground, the target general may make a **SCOUTING** roll. On success, they spot signs in time to halt, bypass, or advance in formation — removing the surprise turn. On failure, they enter unprepared.

**Cavalry in ambush.** A cavalry troop caught in an ambush in broken ground or forest loses its attack roll for the first two turns, as the terrain that concealed the ambush prevents effective mounted operation. Skirmishing cavalry in this situation also loses its mobility advantage for those turns.

> *The supply convoy was Torkel's — eight wagons, thirty guards, moving through the valley below the Kord ridge before the siege lines were established.*
>
> *Vara set the ambush on day three of the approach. She had sixty riders in the tree line and four hours of patience. The convoy's outriders passed within fifty meters of her line. She waited until the wagons were fully in the valley.*
>
> *She set it clean. No one saw them.*
>
> *The outriders had no warning. Vara's skirmishers loosed from the trees before the convoy had even begun to form. The thirty guards took two hard volleys before they could dress a line and answer.*
>
> *No wagons were captured. By the time the guards assembled and the relieving cavalry were visible on the ridge, four guards were dead, two were already riding for help, and Vara was withdrawing.*
>
> *The convoy reached Torkel's camp four hours late with twenty-six guards and four looted wagons in the rear. The loss did not decide the campaign. It changed the arithmetic of how long it could last.*

### Sieges

> *The wall had been built before anyone alive could remember. It had been built by people who understood that attackers have more choices than defenders — more directions to come from, more time to wait, more freedom to choose the moment. The people who built this wall were afraid. You could tell because they had done it right.*

Sieges are a different problem than open battle. The walls decide where the fight happens. The defenders choose their positions. The attackers choose when.

#### Units Per Troop in Sieges

Defenders on walls fight in compressed positions, maximizing their effectiveness per section. For the siege, infantry and skirmisher troops need only **10 units** to gain 1 base die, instead of the normal 20. Maximum base dice remain 5. A garrison of 50 can field five full base dice of infantry. Outside, the attacker needs 100 men to match it.

#### Order of Battle in Sieges

The battlefield is the walls, the gates, the courtyards, and the ground before them.

**Attackers deploy first.** They establish sections based on how many fronts they open against the fortress. The defenders then position troops to respond to what they see. There is no INSIGHT roll — the attacker chooses the ground, and the defender chooses the response.

Sections change as the battle progresses through the narrative — walls breach, gates fall, fighting moves inside. The Gamemaster adjudicates section changes based on what the story requires.

Cavalry and skirmisher troops have limited function against walls. They cannot assault. Most are assigned to siege engines, to the blockade line, or to interdiction of the supply route. They enter the fight only when the gates are forced and the battle moves to open ground inside.

#### Walls

Walls grant defenders the ability to **attack first** and provide advantage dice based on their height and strength.

If, in any battle turn, an attacking infantry troop achieves more successes than the defenders on a section of wall, those attackers have secured footing on the parapet. The wall's advantage dice no longer apply against that troop — the fight is now at the same level, in the same press of bodies. But soldiers on a wall cannot retreat without falling. From that point, demoralization results in automatic deaths rather than fleeing.

If the fortress has multiple walls, each wall's bonuses are tracked independently. They do not stack.

#### Breach and Gate Fighting

Once a gate is broken, or a wall section is reduced to no positional advantage and the attackers secure footing there, that section stops fighting as a wall and starts fighting as a breach.

Defenders no longer attack first from position alone on that section. Any remaining advantage must come from inner barricades, a second wall, or troops already standing in the way.

Cavalry may enter only through a broken gate or a breach wide enough for mounted passage, as the GM judges. A narrow rubble breach admits infantry first.

If the stronghold has an inner wall, keep, or barricaded court, the defenders may withdraw one surviving troop to that new line at the start of the next turn. Otherwise the fight spills into the yard and is treated as ordinary ground inside the fortress.

See **Stronghold Functions** for wall construction costs and advantage dice values.

#### Siege Engines

Siege engines are how attackers reduce the defensive advantage. Each engine requires a troop to operate and protect it. Engines that attack from distance are typically assigned to skirmisher or cavalry troops. Engines meant to directly contact the walls go with infantry.

Each engine must be **constructed on-site**. Build time is listed in the table below.

Engine progress is not secret. Defenders can see towers rise, hear mining, and judge when a ram is close enough to matter. Track that progress openly unless concealment is itself the point of the scene.

| ENGINE | EFFECTS | BUILD TIME |
|---|---|---|
| Battering Ram | Protection 3. Siege dice equal to 3. Gate breaks when cumulative siege dice successes reach 3. Cavalry may enter through a broken gate | 7 days |
| Siege Tower | Siege dice equal to 6. Allows infantry to reach the wall top directly | 30 days |
| Catapult | 4 attack dice, rolled once per day. Each success reduces wall advantage dice by 1 in one section | 14 days |
| Trebuchet | 8 attack dice, rolled once per day. Each success reduces wall advantage dice by 1 in one section | 60 days |
| Tunnel | Variable — see below | Variable |

**Battering Rams and Siege Towers.** These engines grant **siege dice** (a separate color). Each success from siege dice does not deal damage — it progresses the breach. Defender successes damage the machine: results of 1 on siege dice absorb 1 point of damage inflicted by defenders. When siege dice are reduced to zero, the engine is destroyed.

**Battering Ram completion.** Track cumulative successes from the ram's siege dice across turns. When the total reaches 3, the gate breaks. Cavalry may advance through on the following turn.

**Siege Tower completion.** Track cumulative successes from the tower's siege dice across turns. When the total equals the target wall section's original advantage dice, the tower has made contact with the parapet — the bridge or ramp is down. From that turn onward, the wall section's **attack-first** quality and all **high-ground advantage dice** no longer apply against the troop ascending the tower. The fight is at wall level: the same press, without the defenders' position advantage. A tower reduced to zero siege dice before completing its approach is destroyed and must be rebuilt.

**Catapults and Trebuchets.** Attack dice rolled once per day. Each success chips away wall advantage. Defenders who watch their cover being systematically removed must consider sorties to destroy these engines. The machines are protected by their assigned troops, which makes this costly.

**Tunnels.** A tunnel is dug toward the walls with the intent to breach, collapse, or undermine a specific target. Before digging starts, roll 2D6: the sum is the total successes required to complete the objective. The higher die is the tunnel's starting **stability**. Each day of digging, the assigned troop rolls its base dice. Each success is progress toward the objective. Each result of 1 reduces the tunnel's stability. If stability reaches zero, the tunnel collapses. The troop inside is destroyed.

The objective can be abandoned at any time. A new tunnel can begin.

**Important Character inside a collapsing tunnel.** If the tunnel collapses while an Important Character is inside, they are Broken immediately. After the battle, roll D6 using the Wounded Important Characters table. On a result of 1, they die. The tunnel collapse is equivalent to a troop defeat for the purposes of this character.

> *Holk's catapult was finished on day eleven — three days past the original estimate, because on day four two timber beams split, and on day seven a stone dropped from Krymark's south tower broke the arm assembly. Even so, Holk's hands shaved time off the work that would have taken other men longer.*
>
> *First firing at dawn took one bite from the southeast wall. The second day took the rest. Men on that stretch no longer held the height as they had before, and the wall there stopped giving them the same cruel edge.*
>
> *Vidar sent a sortie on the third night, targeting the catapult. Two of Holk's crew were killed. A third was taken and not recovered. Holk spent the next morning training replacement soldiers on equipment they could break with a wrong lever. Without him there the machine would have thrown more hope than stone. He did not leave it for the rest of the siege, which was not in his original contract and which he billed for separately.*

#### Engineers

Every siege engine requires an **engineer** to build. Without an engineer assigned to the project, construction halts. For catapults and trebuchets, an engineer must also be present at each firing — without one, their attack dice are reduced to one-quarter, rounded up (a catapult without an engineer fires 1 die; a trebuchet fires 2). Battering rams and siege towers function without engineers once built.

An engineer costs **5 silver per day**. Only a character with the Engineer talent (see below) fulfills this role.

#### Blockade

Starving a fortress into surrender is slower than assault and far safer. To prevent resupply, the besieging force must field at least **three soldiers for every one soldier** in the defending garrison. If the fortress has sea access, a naval blockade must close that route as well — an army that cannot control the water cannot control the siege.

Once the blockade holds, the defenders consume their stored reserves. They cannot restock. They can only wait and hope the besiegers run out of food first.

The besieging army faces the same arithmetic from the other side. See **Supplies** below.

If the besieging force cannot hold a full blockade, the ring leaks. Once per week, the defenders may attempt one **supply run**, **messenger run**, or **relief contact** through the weak side.

The defender rolls **SCOUTING** or **MANIPULATION**. The besieger rolls **SCOUTING** or **INSIGHT**. Ties favor the besieger.

If the defenders win, choose one:

- `D6 x 20` supply units get through.
- One messenger, engineer, healer, or Important Character gets in or out.
- One base die worth of reinforcements reaches the fortress, if friendly troops are within one campaign week's march or already in direct contact with the siege.

If the besieger wins, the run is broken. The boats are taken, the pack train is scattered, or the messenger is caught with the letter still on him.

#### Non-Combatants

A besieged fortress contains more than soldiers. Craftsmen, servants, stable hands, merchants caught inside when the gates closed, the garrison commander's household, refugees who fled inside when the army appeared — all of them eat.

**Food consumption.** Non-combatants consume supply units at half the rate of soldiers: 0.5 supply units per person per day. A garrison of 60 soldiers with 100 civilians inside is consuming 110 supply units per day, not 60. Defenders rarely know the exact count at the start of a siege.

**To determine non-combatants present:** Roll D6 × 20 at the start of the siege. This is the non-combatant population. The GM may set the number directly if the fortress has been detailed in play.

**Expulsion.** The garrison commander may drive non-combatants out through the gates to extend the food supply. The expelled people walk toward the besieging army. The besieger then decides:

- **Let them through.** They pass. They also carry information — troop count, supply state, morale, the condition of the commander — none of which they intend to give but most of which they give anyway under questioning.
- **Hold them in the field.** They cannot return and cannot leave. They consume nothing inside the fortress. They die in the open unless the siege ends or they escape. This is an atrocity. Apply the same reputation consequences as massacre: future negotiations made Demanding, besieged defenders gain 1 morale point resisting this commander's terms.
- **Drive them back to the gates.** If the garrison re-admits them, the expulsion failed and the supply burden is restored. If the garrison refuses to re-admit them, they die at the walls under both sides' observation.

All three choices have costs. The besieger's decision is about intelligence, logistics, and what the army is willing to be seen doing.

> *On day nineteen, Vidar expelled forty-three people through the east gate: the families of junior soldiers, four merchants, two household servants, and a healer who had been inside when the siege started and had no way to leave before the gates were sealed.*
>
> *They walked across the cleared ground toward Torkel's line. Torkel let them through.*
>
> *They told him what he wanted to know: how many soldiers were still capable of standing watch, how far the stores had fallen, that Vidar had cut the horses' grain ration three days prior. None of them intended to give him this information. Most of it came out of how they answered questions they thought were routine.*
>
> *One of them, the healer, refused to answer any questions at all. Torkel did not press her. She had done her work inside. He had no interest in punishing her for it.*
>
> *He gave them food and let them walk west.*

#### Surrender and Parley

A wise commander offers terms before the breach. That is when mercy is cheapest. After the wall is lost, soldiers expect payment in blood, plunder, or both.

Either side may ask parley once per siege week, and again after any major change: breached wall, failed sortie, disease outbreak, sighted relief, or emptied granary.

The attacker rolls **MANIPULATION**, **INSIGHT**, or **PERFORMANCE**. The defender does the same. Compare successes. Ties mean the talks go nowhere.

If the attacker wins, they may demand one term per extra success:

- the garrison leaves without heavy arms
- hostages are given
- named prisoners, standards, or relics are surrendered
- stores, mounts, or engines are left behind
- the garrison swears not to bear arms again in this campaign

If the defender wins, they may secure one concession per extra success:

- leave with sidearms
- carry out wounded and dead
- keep one wagon, boat, or pack train of goods
- keep one named prisoner, hostage, or relic
- receive three days of safe conduct

#### Bad Faith

If a commander murders surrendered defenders, sacks a place after sworn terms, or takes hostages under false parley, word spreads faster than armies do. For the rest of the campaign, surrender negotiations offered by that commander are **Demanding**, and any garrison that knows the deed gains 1 morale point while resisting his terms.

#### Repairing Defenses

Defenders work through every night that does not bring an assault to repair what the day's engines have broken. For each damaged wall section, roll the base dice of the defending troops (maximum 10 dice per section). Each success restores one advantage die of the wall. Repair is only possible if the wall's advantage dice have not been fully reduced to zero during the day's assault — a wall rubbled to nothing cannot be rebuilt overnight.

Repair costs in peacetime: 80 copper per advantage die, or the equivalent in stone.

#### Sorties

Defenders who wait behind walls surrender the initiative. A general with enough soldiers and nerve sends troops outside to destroy what the besiegers are building.

**Resolution.** A sortie operates as open-field combat using normal battle rules. The defending troop leaves the walls and fights the protecting troop on the ground outside. Neither side uses wall bonuses. Both sides roll their standard dice.

**Targets.** A sortie may target a siege engine, a supply depot, or a specific enemy troop. If the sortie wins the battle roll by 2 or more successes, it reaches the target.

**Engine damage.** When a sortie reaches an engine, roll the sortie troop's base dice against the engine. Each success reduces the engine's attack dice or siege dice by 1. An engine reduced to zero dice is destroyed and must be rebuilt from scratch.

**The cost.** Every soldier sent outside is a soldier off the wall. If the besieging force has a reserve troop not already engaged, it may advance on the now-understaffed wall section during the same turn the sortie is active. The defender cannot be in two places at once.

> *Vidar's second sortie came on day eighteen. The first had been turned back at the catapult. The second came not for the catapult but for the crew.*
>
> *Twenty soldiers, light armor, no shields. They moved fast. Torkel's skirmishers had been watching the south field for three days and had them in range before they crossed the cleared ground. Three volleys hit them before they reached the catapult line. They came on anyway and hit hard enough to reach the machine.*
>
> *They hacked one spar half through and smashed part of the arm assembly. The catapult never threw with the same bite again.*
>
> *They killed two engineers. One was not Holk.*
>
> *Eight of Vidar's twenty soldiers made it back through the gate.*

#### Counter-Siege Measures

Defenders are not limited to waiting, repairing, and sortying. A commander with prepared hands and the right materials may answer the engines directly.

**Fire against towers and rams.** A defending troop with ranged weapons and pitch, oil, or braziers may target a battering ram or siege tower that has reached the wall or gate. If the defenders score 2 or more uncancelled successes, the engine catches or begins to smolder. It loses 1 siege die at the start of each following turn until the operators spend a full turn extinguishing it. Heavy rain or soaked hides cancel the first such loss.

**Countermine.** If the defenders know a tunnel is being dug and have an engineer, dwarf laborers, or another plausible skilled crew, they may attempt a countermine once per day. Roll **SCOUTING** or **INSIGHT**. On success, reduce the tunnel's stability by 1. With 2 or more successes, choose instead: reduce the tunnel's stability by 2, or force the attackers to lose one day's digging progress as the tunnel is flooded, smoked, or collapsed short of the wall.

**Abandon the outer wall.** A commander may give up an outer section before it collapses in order to preserve troops for the inner line. The section is lost immediately, but one defending troop may withdraw in order to the next defensible line at the start of the following turn. Troops withdrawn this way keep their remaining morale points.

#### Relief of a Siege

A siege changes the moment a relief army arrives. The besieger must now face outward and inward at once.

When a friendly field army reaches the fortress, the besieger chooses one course before the next battle turn:

- **Hold the ring.** Keep troops facing both the garrison and the relief army. Every besieger section engaged on either side loses 1 advantage die that turn. No besieger reserve may answer both threats in the same turn.
- **Turn to meet relief.** Face the relief army as an open-field battle. The garrison may sortie on the following turn without first needing to win initiative over the siege line.
- **Raise the siege.** Abandon engines, stores too heavy to move quickly, and any works that cannot be carried. The relief army has broken the investment without a full battle.

If the besieger holds the ring and, in the same turn, a garrison sortie and the relief army engage the same besieger section, that section counts as attacked from front and rear.

The siege is broken when the garrison and the relief force open a clear path between fortress and countryside, or when the besieger withdraws. Once the siege is broken, supplies, messengers, and reinforcements may move again, and the fighting continues as field battle only if the attackers choose to stand.

### Supplies

> *Torkel's adjutant brought the numbers at dawn. Seventeen days of grain at current consumption. Eleven if the cavalry horses were fed the same ration as the men, which they would have to be once the hay ran out, which would happen before the grain did. Three days to the nearest village, maybe four with the road as it was. The village had two hundred people.*
>
> *Torkel looked at the numbers for a long time. Then he told the adjutant to have the cavalry commander report to him.*
>
> *Nobody talks about this part. The songs don't mention the numbers. But that is the part that decides most wars.*

#### Supply Units

Every army has **supply units** — the food, fodder, and water that keep it functioning. Supply units are acquired from a base of operations or gathered from the land by foraging.

Daily consumption per unit:

| TYPE | SUPPLY CONSUMPTION |
|---|---:|
| Infantry | 1 |
| Skirmishers | 1 |
| Cavalry | 2 |
| Monster | 20 |

Once per day, the troops must be fed. Reduce supply units by the total consumption of all units in the army.

#### Starving Troops

When supply units run out, commanders decide who goes hungry. Entire troops are designated unfed — not individual soldiers.

A troop that goes without food suffers demoralization on results of 1 **and 2** on its base dice, rather than only 1s. Additionally, all enemy troops fighting it gain 2 advantage dice that are not subject to the base dice cap.

Hunger is not only a crisis point. A prudent general runs the numbers before the supplies run out and reduces rations before they are gone.

#### Desertion and Death

A troop unfed for a day rolls for desertion and death. Roll the troop's base dice:

- Each result of 1 — desertion. That base die leaves the troop permanently.
- Add the number of days without food to each die result. Each result of 10 or higher — the soldiers die. That die is removed permanently.

These rolls are independent. A troop starving for three days is rolling base dice where a result of 7 already means death.

> *Day nineteen. The supply numbers said two days of grain at current consumption. Torkel designated two infantry sections unfed — not because the supplies were gone but because they would be in two days, and the cavalry could not be short-rationed and remain cavalry.*
>
> *The unfed sections went brittle at once. Men who would have held yesterday began looking over their shoulders, and any enemy pressing them found the work easier. Torkel knew that before he gave the order. It was the right decision and still a bad one.*
>
> *Day twenty: desertion. Men slipped away before dawn. The nearer section lost six.*
>
> *The supply wagon from Thornwall arrived on day twenty-one. Two men from the starving sections were dead by then — cold ground, hunger the contributing cause. They were recorded in the official account as lost to illness.*
>
> *The soldiers who had been fed watched the wagon arrive and said nothing about the two who had not made it. They understood the arithmetic. Most of them accepted it. The ones who did not were quiet about it in the way people are quiet when the moment to speak has not yet come.*

#### Buying Supplies

A single supply unit — enough for one soldier for one day — costs **2 copper** in normal conditions. War conditions, distant campaigns, and local scarcity all alter this. Desperate armies pay what they must.

#### Foraging

An ordered foraging operation sends troops into the surrounding countryside to hunt and gather. When the general orders it, the foraging troops roll their base dice. Skirmisher troops double their dice for foraging — hunters know what they are doing.

Add all die results together — every result counts, not just 6s. Multiply the total by 10. That is the total supply units gathered in one quarter day.

**Depletion.** A hex foraged by an army yields less each quarter day. For every quarter day of foraging in the same hex, reduce each die's result by 1 when totaling. This penalty recovers at 1 per week. A foraged hex eventually yields nothing. Armies that stay in one place long enough eat the countryside to the bone.

Troops sent to forage in another hex divide the army. Divided forces cannot support each other. Any battle initiated while the army is split must be fought with the forces present in that hex.

#### Supply Lines

An army in the field is connected to a **base** — its stronghold, a fortified camp, a friendly settlement capable of stockpiling and distributing provisions. As long as the supply line holds, the army receives food and reinforcements along the shortest path between base and army.

**March.** Infantry moves 1 hex per quarter day. Cavalry moves 2 hexes per quarter day.

**Forced March.** An army may march a second quarter day, but each troop that fights the same day or the next applies increased demoralization — results of 1 and 2 on base dice trigger demoralization, not only 1s. Forced marches are for emergencies, not schedules.

**Cooking.** Raw and dried food must be prepared before it can sustain fighting soldiers. An army on campaign dedicates one quarter day per day to cooking and eating. This is not waste — it is maintenance.

**Camp.** An army that does not establish a proper camp at the end of each day's march is vulnerable to night attack. Setting up and breaking camp costs one quarter day each. Armies that skip camp are exposed. Enemies who know this will exploit it.

**Sleep.** Troops need one quarter day of sleep, usually at night. Poorly rested troops suffer increased demoralization — results of 1 and 2 on base dice.

**Accumulated Demoralization.** Hunger, forced march, and poor sleep stack. A troop suffering all three at once rolls base dice where results of 1 through 4 cause demoralization. The enemy does not need to beat this troop. It is doing the work itself.

#### Cutting the Supply Line

The most effective way to weaken an army without fighting it is to sever its supply line. Any enemy force that passes through a hex on the direct route between the army and its base disrupts supplies. A small skirmish occurs and the supplies are taken. If reinforcements are also in transit, the attack may trigger a larger engagement.

Supplies are typically delivered once per week. A cut line denies an army food for however long it takes to re-establish contact.

Cavalry troops make the best raiders. They are fast enough to cut through and re-establish distance before the main army can respond.

#### Baggage, Camp, and Rear Pressure

An army brings more than fighters: wagons, cookfires, spare weapons, pack animals, engineers, and the food that keeps the rest moving. A flank broken open is not only a path to the rear of the line. It is a path to all of that.

**Guarding the rear.** A general expecting raiders may hold one troop back as baggage guard. That troop does not take part in the main battle until committed.

**Raiding baggage in battle.** A cavalry, skirmishing cavalry, or skirmisher troop that wins its section and does not pivot or pursue may strike the enemy baggage instead. If no baggage guard stands there, choose one consequence immediately:

- lose `D6 x 20` supply units
- lose one engineer, messenger chain, or `1D3` spare mounts or draught beasts
- one frontline troop loses 1 morale point on the next turn as smoke, panic, and contradiction reach the line

If a baggage guard is present, fight it as a normal section first. Only a second victory opens the baggage.

**Camp raids.** An army that camps without proper pickets or baggage guard invites night work. A successful raid on camp burns `D6 x 20` supply units, scatters animals, or prevents one troop from taking part in the first turn of the next day's battle, as the GM judges.

### Campaign Movement

Armies moving across the Ravenlands are slow, loud, and visible. The quarter day rules from Chapter 8 apply. Beyond basic travel, keep the following in mind:

- Infantry marches 1 hex per quarter day; cavalry marches 2
- Camp setup and breakdown consume quarter days
- Foraging parties split the army and leave it vulnerable
- Supply deliveries arrive weekly; plan accordingly
- The army's slowest element sets the army's pace

An army moving through allied territory can sustain itself longer. An army moving through stripped or hostile ground eats its reserves, then eats the countryside, then begins to die.

#### Ferries, Boats, and Water Operations

A ford, bridge, ferry, or landing is a battlefield in its own right. Whoever holds the bank controls more than movement. They control time.

**Ferry and landing fights.** A troop defending a ferry, bridgehead, or boat landing gains 1 advantage die on the first turn if the enemy must disembark or crowd onto narrow ground. Cavalry fighting from boats or on a narrow ferry fights as infantry until clear ground is gained.

**Crossing under fire.** Troops crossing by boats, rafts, or ferries under missile fire use half their base dice on the landing turn, rounded up, just as at a ford. If they land in disorder, they cannot claim flanking or rear advantage on that turn.

**Water relief and escape.** A fortress with river or sea access that is not fully blockaded may attempt one extra messenger run, supply run, or escape by boat each siege week. Resolve it with the same opposed roll as a weak-side blockade run. If the boats are taken, the run fails and the craft are lost.

**Assault from the water.** Troops storming a waterside gate, quay, or river wall from boats attack second on the first turn after landing. Defenders attack first if they already hold the dock, stairs, or gatehouse.

### Disease

Sieges are where disease lives. Both sides pack soldiers into tight quarters with poor water, rotting food, and no distance from their own waste. Illness does not distinguish between attacker and defender.

At the end of each week of siege, roll one D6 for every 100 soldiers in the army, rounding up. If any die results in a 1, disease has broken out. Count every 1 rolled — each one adds one **disease die**.

**Disease progression:** At outbreak, and at the end of each subsequent week, roll all active disease dice:

- Each result of 1 kills 10 soldiers.
- Each result of 6 adds 1 new disease die.
- All other results remove that die.

After a disease fully clears (all dice removed), run the outbreak check again at the end of the next week to see if another begins.

**Siege conditions multiply disease.** When disease breaks out, or when a result of 6 is rolled on disease dice, besieged defenders double their total disease dice — unless the stronghold has a **Sewer** function. The crowding inside walls is worse than the crowding outside them.

**Battlefield camps.** An army that camps on or adjacent to a major battlefield for more than 3 days — resting, besieging a remaining garrison, waiting for resupply, or unable to move — risks disease spreading from the unburied dead. After those 3 days, run a simplified outbreak check: roll one D6 per 50 dead soldiers on the field, rounding up, to a maximum of 5 dice. On a result of 1, disease breaks out and proceeds normally. The siege doubling rule does not apply to battlefield camps unless the army is also inside a fortified position.

**Burying the dead.** A troop assigned to burial clears 50 dead per quarter day of work. A troop on burial duty cannot perform any other task that quarter day. An army that buries its dead within 3 days of the battle skips the battlefield disease check entirely.

> *Day thirty-one: the camp physician found the first case in the east trench — one man, high fever, the cough that started in the chest.*
>
> *By week's end the cough had not stayed with one man. The sickness had found another hold in camp.*
>
> *The following week it bred faster than it killed. The week after that it took ten men and loosened its grip nowhere else.*
>
> *Inside Krymark, Vidar had no sewer. Every bout of sickness bred another. His garrison of sixty was packed behind walls built to withstand assault. Those walls were also keeping the illness in.*
>
> *Torkel received Vidar's messenger on day forty-three. The garrison had eleven soldiers capable of standing. Torkel had lost twenty-two men to disease and forty more to battle.*
>
> *The arithmetic was clean. There was nothing to celebrate about it.*

### Aftermath

The last body falls. The fighting ends — by rout, concession, or nightfall. What remains is the dead, the wounded who can still speak, the ones who cannot, the captives worth keeping, and whatever decisions must now be made before night.

#### Prisoners and Ransom

Important Characters and Named Men captured during battle may be held for ransom. The value of a prisoner depends on their standing. A captive may also provide information, tactical access, or political leverage beyond coin.

| PRISONER TYPE | RANSOM VALUE |
|---|---:|
| Common soldier | 10 silver |
| Sergeant or equivalent | 50 silver |
| Knight or named officer | 200 silver |
| Commander or minor noble | 500 silver |
| General or significant lord | 2,000 silver or more |

Ransoms are negotiated, not fixed. The values above are starting points. A general who broke a siege might be worth far more dead to the enemy than alive to their own side. The captors know this. So does the prisoner.

When the exact terms matter, both sides roll **MANIPULATION** or **INSIGHT**. Compare successes. Each extra success adds or strips one term: more coin, exchanged hostages, a sworn truce, the surrender of a named captive, a standard returned, or safe conduct under escort.

> *Vidar came out of the gate himself, which Torkel read as either courage or the recognition that a commander who sends a subordinate to negotiate his own ransom is worth less before the negotiation ends than when it began.*
>
> *The table value for a commander of his rank was 500 silver. He opened at 200, which was a test and an insult and functioned as both. The negotiation ran four days.*
>
> *Final terms: 400 silver, an oath of non-aggression for one year, and the surrender of Haerek — the large man who had stood in the killing ground on the second day with his white flag and his patience.*
>
> *Torkel had no tactical use for Haerek. He took him anyway because Vidar flinched slightly when Haerek's name was mentioned, and anything that caused Vidar to flinch was worth putting in the terms.*
>
> *Senna handled the formal language. She made the oath sound like something both sides could mean, which was not the same as both sides meaning it.*
>
> *Haerek was released three days later without conditions. Torkel told him to walk north. He did.*

#### Prisoners Without Ransom Value

Common soldiers taken alive after a battle have no price on their heads. They are a problem: armed men who were trying to kill you an hour ago, now disarmed and standing in your camp, eating your supplies. The decision of what to do with them is the general's alone.

**Release on oath.** The soldier swears not to take further part in the campaign. This costs nothing to give. It is worth the credibility of the soldier swearing it. Some men keep it. Some do not. A released prisoner in the next season's army is not a rare outcome.

**Forced labor.** Prisoners haul supplies, dig earthworks, work the camp. They reduce the army's supply burden slightly and represent a continuous security problem. Assign a guard or accept that some of them will eventually try something.

**Press into service.** Make a **MANIPULATION** roll opposed by the prisoner's Wits. On success, the soldier agrees to fight for their captors. They fight with reduced conviction and will desert at the first opportunity the GM judges plausible. A pressed soldier who learns that no-quarter has been declared against their former side fights with more investment than most.

**Execute.** The prisoners are killed. It ends the security problem, requires no guard, and costs no supplies. It is remembered by every soldier who hears about it, including those in armies the general has not yet fought. Executing common prisoners after a battle is not unusual in the Ravenlands — the conditions that produce campaigns also produce this decision. The soldiers who carry out the order understand the arithmetic. Most of them do not think about it long afterward. Some do.

The decision is the general's. It cannot be taken back once made.

> *After Krymark fell, there were forty-one soldiers who had survived to the surrender. Vidar was worth ransoming. Haerek was worth including in the terms. The remaining thirty-nine had nothing to offer.*
>
> *Torkel ordered them disarmed and their boots taken. He released them with a warning to be gone from the region before the army broke camp. Most were gone before morning.*
>
> *The boots were distributed to Torkel's own soldiers, several of whom had been wrapping their feet in canvas since the third river crossing.*
>
> *This was the generous outcome. Torkel knew it was the generous outcome. He also knew he had enough food to be generous, which was the part that was rarely mentioned in the stories told afterward about what kind of man Torkel was.*

#### Stripping the Dead

Stripping the dead is work. A quarter day spent on it yields D6 trade goods per 10 base dice of destroyed troops from all sides combined — weapons, armor fragments, coins, belts, boots, whatever the dead were carrying when they stopped needing it.

What comes off first: boots and footwear, because soldiers who have been walking since spring know what boots are worth. Then belts. Then anything on the hands. Then the harder work of removing armor from bodies that do not cooperate with the process. Weapons are collected last and fastest — they lie where they fell. Not everything survives intact: a helmet split by an axe is scrap iron; a sword bent hard against plate is less than it was. The men doing this work have been killing since morning and feel no particular reluctance about what comes after.

A troop assigned to this task cannot participate in any other activity during that quarter day.

#### Wounded Important Characters

An Important Character who ends the battle Broken but not explicitly killed must roll D6 after the battle resolves:

| D6 | RESULT |
|---|---|
| 1 | The wounds are mortal. The character dies before the next day |
| 2–4 | Serious injury. The character needs care for 1D3 weeks |
| 5–6 | Survived. Recover normally per the standard injury rules |

A character who receives immediate care from another Important Character with a relevant healing skill rolls D6+1 instead.

#### Storm, Sack, and Mercy

When a place is taken by breach or assault, the victors choose what kind of victory it is. That decision matters more than the last ladder did.

**Mercy.** The place is spared. Prisoners can be ransomed, hostages remain useful, and the victors take no extra plunder beyond lawful seizure and the stripping of the dead. If the troops had been promised sack and are denied it, each participating troop loses 1 morale point.

**Ordered Sack.** Officers let the troops loot for one quarter day, then pull them back into line. Gain `D6 x 20` copper and `D6 x 20` supply units for each defending troop destroyed or routed in the taking of the place. Each participating troop then makes a morale roll. On failure, it loses 1 morale point to drink, murder, fire, rape, quarrels, and straggling.

**Open Sack.** The commanders stop pretending they are in charge. Gain double the Ordered Sack reward. Then choose one: a named prisoner, hostage, engineer, healer, or other useful figure in the place is dead, carried off, or lost beyond recovery. Add 1 disease die to the next weekly disease check for the victors. For the rest of the campaign, surrender negotiations offered by commanders known for this sack are **Demanding**.

#### Occupation After Taking a Place

A place taken is not held by shouting from the gate. Leave at least one troop behind by nightfall or the place is not occupied, only plundered.

Then choose how it is held.

**Oaths and hostages.** The old authorities remain visible under guard. Take `1D3` named hostages and exact sworn terms. The first attempt at revolt, refusal, or smuggling in the next campaign week fails automatically unless the hostages are freed first.

**Garrison rule.** The victors hold the place openly with steel and routine. The garrison draws normal pay and supplies. If either fails for a week, the garrison itself begins stripping the place, and the settlement counts as suffering **Hard** burden immediately.

**Terror rule.** One or more visible leaders are hanged, maimed, or nailed up where the road can see them. The place does not revolt in the next campaign week. Then worsen Standing by 2 in every nearby settlement that hears of it, and treat future surrender offers from that commander as **Demanding** for the rest of the campaign.

### New Talents

These talents are available to characters who have spent time commanding soldiers, engineering fortifications, or leading cavalry.

#### COMMANDER

You give orders and soldiers obey them. Not because you shout louder, but because you speak with the precision of someone who knows what will happen if they do not.

- **RANK 1:** The troop you join gains morale points equal to your rank in this talent at the start of the battle. You can apply this to only one troop per engagement.
- **RANK 2:** You enforce your word. Gain +2 to **REGROUP** rolls and any other battle-related leadership rolls the Gamemaster approves.
- **RANK 3:** Your presence is an advantage die. The troop you join gains 1 additional advantage die beyond the die granted for being an Important Character. Many commanders with this rank are not significant characters outside battle — they simply know the work.

#### GENERAL

You do not lead men. You arrange them. There is a difference, and the men who survive long enough come to understand it.

- **RANK 1:** Gain a bonus to your **PERFORMANCE** rally roll equal to your rank in this talent.
- **RANK 2:** The troop you join gains an additional D8 that is not an advantage die and not subject to the base dice cap. This benefit stacks with Commander (Rank 3) if both apply.
- **RANK 3:** Moving to a new troop at the start of a turn lets you make a **PERFORMANCE** roll. Every 2 successes grants that troop 1 morale point. The morale you carry with you is part of how battles turn.

#### ENGINEER

You understand weight, leverage, and stress. Other people look at a wall and see stone. You see the places it is willing to fall.

- **RANK 1:** You can oversee construction of siege engines. Catapults and trebuchets require your presence to fire at full effectiveness. You can be assigned to one engine at a time.
- **RANK 2:** Engines you oversee are built 20% faster, rounding up to the nearest day.
- **RANK 3:** Siege engines built under your supervision gain +1 to their attack or siege dice.

#### CHIEF OF RIDERS

The cavalry does not just ride when you say ride. It hits where you point it and gets clear before the enemy can close.

- **RANK 1:** Spend 1 morale point from your cavalry troop to charge. The troop gains a D8 on its first combat turn. If the enemy creates distance, you may charge again as long as you have morale to spend.
- **RANK 2:** Combine advantage dice from size and flanking or rear attack into a single artifact die, starting at D8 and jumping directly to D12 when two qualifying dice combine — D10 is intentionally skipped. This combined die does not count toward the base dice cap.
- **RANK 3:** The charge die increases to D10. While engaged, you may attempt to disengage with a **PERFORMANCE** roll. Success means the troop withdraws in order — cohesive, able to charge again or reposition.

### Example Units

### Special Troop Types

Some armies in the Ravenlands command forces that do not fight like ordinary infantry. These units require additional rules.

#### Aerial Troops

Flying mounts — wyverns, giant eagles, summoned creatures of wing and claw — are classified as cavalry but fight on different terms.

Aerial troops cannot be engaged in melee by ground forces until they choose to descend or are driven down. Ranged troops may target them normally. Ground melee troops cannot close without the aerial unit initiating the approach.

**Aerial Charge.** When aerial troops dive to attack, the attack counts as a flanking attack — or a rear attack if they come in behind the enemy line. The Attacks First quality of polearms does not apply: spears cannot be braced at battle scale against something falling from above. The size advantage die applies if the aerial creatures are larger than their targets.

**Ranged suppression.** A ranged troop that scores 3 or more successes against an aerial troop in a single turn deals double damage for that roll. Flying creatures cannot avoid a coordinated volley the way cavalry can swing around a line.

Aerial troops require specialized care between battles. Treat their daily cost as twice that of equivalent ground cavalry.

#### Undead and Misgrown Armies

Armies raised from the dead or drawn from Misgrown hordes share qualities that change how the battle resolves.

**No morale.** Undead and Misgrown troops do not roll morale. They cannot be Demoralized. Results of 1 on their base dice have no morale effect — the dead do not flee, and Misgrown do not waver in the ordinary way. They still lose base dice from damage. They simply do not break.

**No supply requirement.** Undead troops need no food and are exempt from supply checks. Misgrown troops require GM judgment — some feed on what they kill, others need nothing at all.

**Controller dependency.** An undead army requires an animating will. If the controlling Important Character is killed or Broken, the undead troop loses half its remaining base dice immediately (round up), then loses 1 additional base die at the start of each subsequent turn as the animating force fragments. When the last base die is lost, the bodies are inert.

Misgrown acting as a genuine feral horde — not directly controlled by a spellcaster or commander — do not have this vulnerability. They also cannot receive tactical orders. They go where they go.

**Demonic presence.** An army containing Misgrown or creatures with genuine demonic origin exerts a psychological weight on those facing them. For the first 3 turns of battle, enemy troops lose 1 morale point at the start of each turn. Troops with the Fearless talent or equivalent are immune to this effect. After turn 3, the enemy has adjusted. The effect ends.

### Notes on Mounts and Size

Horses are not the only mounts in the Ravenlands. The **size advantage die** is gained by being physically larger than the enemy unit — not merely by being cavalry or mounted. A goblin on a wolf may deny a size advantage die to a human on foot (the mount is comparable in height) and gains advantage over a goblin on foot. Size must be judged honestly against the actual units involved.

**Switching weapons** mid-battle is permitted at the start of any turn. Cavalry may also dismount and fight as infantry, which changes their base dice — and removes the mobility advantage they were relying on.

### Stronghold Functions for War

> *Every stronghold is, in the end, a bet. The builder bets they can accumulate enough stone, iron, wood, and human trust to make the cost of attacking them higher than the cost of going around them. Sometimes this bet pays for generations. Sometimes it does not pay past the first winter.*

Strongholds are the foundation of any serious military campaign. They are where troops are recruited, trained, and fed between engagements. They are the locations that must be held to maintain control of a region. The campaign typically starts at one and ends at another.

This section covers only the stronghold functions relevant to warfare: those that produce what armies need or that improve defensive capability. Each function has a construction cost in copper coins, a build time in days, and an effect related to battle. #### Stockpiling Supplies

Armies need not only wages but food. The stronghold's food-producing functions supply this. Either reserve the food units produced directly as supply units, or convert all income to copper and buy supply units at the standard price — 3 copper per unit — when tallying the week's income. The latter simplifies accounting at the cost of some realism.

Prudent commanders keep one or more pantries stocked before a campaign begins. A pantry holding 5,000 units can sustain 1,000 infantry soldiers for five days without resupply — enough margin to survive a cut supply line, not enough to survive a prolonged siege alone.

#### Scaling Walls

Wall height and thickness can be increased beyond the base construction, multiplying cost and adding advantage dice. Maximum wall advantage dice is **+4**. A second wall adds another independent set of bonuses — attackers who breach the outer wall must begin again against the inner.

Wall damage from siege engines is tracked per section. Overnight repair restores lost advantage dice, as described above.

#### Village Contributions

Villages near the stronghold can be called upon to contribute to a war effort. The acceptable threshold — within a protection pact or under threat — is:

- **Supplies:** Three times the village's population, weekly
- **Coin:** Five times the village's population in copper, weekly

These are the sustainable limits. Demanding more is possible, but it kills villages.

**If excess supplies are taken (four to ten times population):** Roll a D6. The result is the percentage of the village's population that dies of starvation that week. The same applies to looting. A result of 4 against a village of two hundred people is eight dead. The village will remember.

The Ravenlands cannot spare its people, and the army that destroys the villages feeding it has solved its supply problem by eliminating the force that needed supplying.

**Coin above five times population** (up to ten times, maximum) can be extracted, but most of it will not exist — the subsistence economy of most Ravenlands settlements does not produce coin that is not already spent.

**Deterioration.** Villages forced beyond these limits suffer hunger, poverty, and growing resentment. The army that causes this returns to a poorer, angrier region.

#### Refusal, Hostages, and Example

When a village hides stores, bars its granary, or answers collection with silence, the commander must decide what kind of obedience is wanted.

**Take hostages.** Seize `1D3` named hostages — headman's kin, miller's son, shrine novice, ferryman's wife, whoever the village cannot lightly lose. The next collection or surrender negotiation involving that village takes one lower difficulty. If a hostage is maimed, violated, or dies in custody, every nearby village that hears of it worsens one step in Standing toward the army or ruler responsible.

**Quarter troops.** One troop lives in the village for a week without drawing its own supply from the army's stores. The village counts as having suffered **Hard** burden even if the formal demand was lighter. If the quartered troop is already unpaid, treat the village as having suffered **Crushing** burden instead.

**Make an example.** Hang, mutilate, or beat to death one elder, headman, priest, hidden storekeeper, or other public figure. The village yields this week without another roll. Then worsen its Standing by 2 and advance any relevant Feud by 1. Nearby settlements that learn what was done take +1 difficulty to be brought under willing protection by the same force for the rest of the campaign.

### Salaries

Controlling a large army in the Ravenlands is nearly impossible for one simple reason: the cost is extraordinary. The subsistence economy that characterizes most settlements makes maintaining an armed force large enough to dominate an entire region an overly ambitious endeavor. For this reason, most strongholds maintain only a small garrison in peacetime, and wars must, by necessity, be brief. A prolonged war can devastate a region — too many people devoted to killing each other rather than working to produce food or wealth.

The true cost of raising an army in the Ravenlands is not iron. It is food. It is continuous. It cannot be delayed past the point of hunger without the army beginning to dissolve.

#### Payment Arrangements

Payment is typically expected weekly rather than daily. Various arrangements are common:

**Weekly coin:** The standard arrangement. Soldiers trust it because they can count it. Commanders prefer it because it is clean. It requires continuous income from the stronghold.

**Plunder promise:** Warriors are promised everything seized during the campaign — a predatory arrangement highly destructive to civilian populations, which eventually ruins the regions the army passes through. It is common precisely because it requires nothing from the general until after the campaign.

**End-of-campaign payment:** Soldiers agree to receive their full pay at the campaign's conclusion, typically at the start of winter. It is not well-received. Failure to deliver on this arrangement is frequent enough that soldiers who accept it are making a calculation about credibility, not a gesture of loyalty.

Salary terms are established during recruitment. Changing them mid-campaign generates dissatisfaction and distrust. Sometimes it is the only option. The soldiers understand this. They do not like it.

#### When Payment Fails

When a section of the army realizes it will not receive its rightful payment, desertion begins. It is not dramatic — a watch post stands half-empty at dawn, six saddles are gone, or an officer wakes to find armed men at the foot of his blanket asking where the money is.

Generals who owe unpaid soldiers face demands, then threats, then the threat of the troops turning their weapons inward. Deserters without funds may resort to looting nearby villages or besieging their former commander until demands are met. This is not rare. It is the expected outcome of failed promises.

Generals may renegotiate. These promises are weighted against the credibility of previous ones. A general who has failed to pay before has less credibility. A general who has made good on contracts despite difficulty has more.

Before the unpaid troops test their patience, the general or a troop commander may attempt **PERFORMANCE** or **MANIPULATION**. Each success steadies one affected troop for that week. Each 1 costs one affected troop 1 morale point immediately.

Each affected troop then makes a morale roll.

- **Success:** The troop stays in hand, but loses 1 morale point to grumbling, suspicion, and private theft.
- **Failure:** The troop loses 1 morale point and 1 base die to desertion before the next dawn.
- **Second consecutive unpaid week, failed roll:** The troop does not merely shed men. It mutinies, loots nearby civilians, seizes an officer, or refuses orders until paid, promised open plunder, or broken by force.

#### Mercenary Renegotiation

Mercenaries operate under contract. Contracts are leverage — and leverage changes when the army's situation does. A mercenary captain whose cavalry delivered the battle-winning charge is not standing in the same position as the man who signed in the spring for three copper a day. He knows it. He expects the general to know it too.

**When mercenaries renegotiate.** Any of these conditions may prompt a demand to reopen the contract:

- The campaign has run longer than the contracted period (typically one season)
- The army has suffered a major defeat (3 or more sections destroyed in a single engagement)
- The mercenaries have delivered a decisive result — the flanking attack, the successful sortie, the charge that broke the line — and want credit for it
- The general has failed to pay for 2 or more consecutive weeks

**The demand.** The mercenary commander presents terms: additional coin, a larger share of plunder, release from a specific obligation, or a fixed end date to the contract. The general may accept, negotiate, or refuse.

**Resolution.** The general or an appointed character makes a **MANIPULATION** or **PERFORMANCE** roll:

- **2+ successes:** The original terms hold. The mercenaries are satisfied — for this campaign.
- **1 success:** A partial concession is required. The general yields on one point. The troop remains in service but no longer receives the morale bonus granted by its original contract terms.
- **0 successes:** The mercenaries walk. If owed money, they may attempt to extract it by force before leaving. If a significant portion of the army departs this way, the campaign may not survive it.

**Switching sides.** If an enemy commander learns the general's mercenaries are dissatisfied, they may send a private offer. The enemy makes a **MANIPULATION** roll against the general's **INSIGHT**. On the enemy's success, the offer reaches the mercenary commander without interception. The mercenaries weigh the offer against what they are owed, what they have been promised, and what betraying their current contract will cost them across every future campaign.

### Battle Events

> *Stran was not supposed to be on that section of wall. He had been sent to check the storage under the south tower, which was a job for a courier, and the fact that it had been given to him meant the courier was probably dead. When he arrived, the tower section was in the hands of men he did not recognize who were losing it, and he had a sword and a reasonable objection to the enemy having the wall, so he stayed.*
>
> *He stayed long enough for the shouting to go hoarse and the light to change. In the tally afterward it was less time than it felt.*

Battles are the most dramatic scenes in a campaign. They should feel like it. The dice determine outcomes, but the outcomes should become specific events — not the abstraction that generated them, but what that abstraction means to the soldiers who lived it.

**Translating abstraction:** A troop with advantages that somehow routes its inferior opponent may have broken under a flanking attack none of them saw coming, or a commander whose voice filled the line for three turns finally took an arrow. Narrate from consequence.

#### Important Characters in Battle

When the rules say "Important Character," they mean anyone in the story who has a name and a history — not only PCs. A rival commander the players have fought before. An antagonist the army recognizes on sight. A mercenary captain who switches sides. These figures carry the same rules weight as PCs when they are present in a battle.

It is also possible for a character who enters the battle as a nobody to leave it as something else. The chaos of sieges and open fields creates the conditions for this constantly. A soldier who does something extraordinary during a battle event can become an important character through that event rather than before it. The GM should not hesitate to introduce new important characters mid-battle when the story calls for them — these figures, risen from complete obscurity, are often the most memorable.

#### The Important Character Die

**These rules are optional.** Use them when the characters at the table want individual battle moments for their PCs and named NPCs. If a scene needs to move quickly, skip the event roll and narrate the die result directly without a table.

**At the start of each battle turn,** any Important Character in active combat rolls a D66 against the appropriate table based on what they are currently fighting.

Every Important Character present in a battle grants their troop 1 advantage die. This die can be tracked separately — a different color, a different size — to determine individual events.

Results of 11–36 produce no special event. The character fights without being singled out. The remaining results generate scenes.

These events occur alongside the troop's regular battle roll — resolve the troop roll first, then weave the event's outcome into the scene.

**If the character's advantage die shows a 1:** The character is struck. Roll dice equal to the character's current Strength. Each result of 1 or 2 inflicts 1 point of damage. This may render the character Broken.

**If the character is Broken:** The die continues to roll each turn, but its success no longer benefits the troop. It represents the character's attempt to survive on their own. A result of 1 on this die means the character is killed.

**If the character's troop loses its last base die to damage:** The character is automatically Broken and rolls a critical injury. They may be captured, evacuated, or killed at the enemy's discretion. Prisoners are tools. In the Ravenlands, a living noble or commander is worth far more than a dead one.

**Artifact Die.** If the character carries a powerful artifact, replace their D6 advantage die with the corresponding artifact die. The die still represents them and follows all the same rules.

**Fleeing.** Important Characters do not flee the battlefield unless the last base die of their troop flees. When that happens, they retreat alongside that last group to the rear and may then attempt to regroup their soldiers.

**Retreat.** A group from the character's troop — one base die's worth of soldiers — may carry a Broken character to safety, moving them to the rear. The character, the troop's commander, or the general may call this. Any conflicting order cancels it. A character carried off the field may not re-enter the battle in the same engagement.

#### Battle Magic

A mage who fights alongside troops can turn spells to battlefield use. Each battle turn, the mage may sacrifice their advantage die contribution for that turn to cast one spell. The troop does not gain the benefit of their presence that turn — the mage's attention is elsewhere and their usual role in the line is empty.

The mage spends WP and casts the spell normally using the rules in Chapter 7. The GM translates the result into battle terms:

**Damage spells** — fire, lightning, force: Every 2 points of effective Power Level applied to direct harm converts to 1 point of troop damage against the targeted troop. Overcharge successes count toward the effective Power Level for this calculation. Protection dice still apply. A spell reaching effective Power Level 4 deals 2 points of troop damage before protection is counted.

**Area spells** that affect multiple targets split their effective Power Level equally across affected troops, rounded down per troop. A Power Level 4 area spell hitting two enemy troops in a section deals 1 troop damage to each.

**Condition spells** — fear, weakness, blindness, compulsion: The GM applies the equivalent battle condition lasting 1D3 turns. A fear effect becomes Demoralized. A weakness becomes reduced base dice. A blinding effect denies advantage dice. Scale the severity to the Power Level spent.

**Range:** The spell's stated range from Chapter 7 applies at battle scale. Near range reaches the front rank of an engaged troop. Short range reaches a full section. Long range crosses sections or the full field.

A mage who is Broken cannot cast battle spells. They fight with ordinary means or try to survive until the field clears.

#### Commanders and Important Characters

Important Characters are not always their troop's commander. A character known for craft, subterfuge, or speed may be present in a structured army without holding rank. They still grant their troop the advantage die. They still experience battle events that make their turn on the field memorable. What they lack is the authority to give orders that soldiers are obligated to follow.

This matters at the table. A PC who is a Druid or a Rogue does not automatically lead the soldiers they fight beside. Their presence is valued. Their command is not assumed. The concepts of commander and important character align often but not always — and the gap between them is where interesting things happen.

#### Battle Events Against Monsters

| D66 | EVENT |
|---|---|
| 11–36 | No special event this turn |
| 41–43 | The monsters roar, scream, or shriek. The troop loses the Important Character die and all morale points, then makes a morale roll with only 1s applying. The character can attempt to prevent both effects: first, a Demanding INSIGHT roll to hold themselves; then, a Demanding PERFORMANCE roll to hold their companions |
| 44–46 | One monster breaks from the mass and faces the character directly, bringing 1D3+2 soldiers with it. Resolve in 3 combat rounds. If the monster is Broken, the enemy troop loses one base die |
| 51–53 | A monster attempts to isolate the character, circling and attacking from multiple angles. A successful MOVE roll prevents being surrounded. On failure, the character is isolated and takes an attack with 10 base dice |
| 54–56 | Monsters use the corpses of fallen soldiers to demoralize the living. The character can punish this: INSIGHT to overcome their own fear, then MOVE or ANIMAL HANDLING to reach the monsters, then MELEE or MARKSMANSHIP to strike. Failing any roll leaves the character Stunned; the troop loses the Important Character die and 1D6 morale points |
| 61–63 | The character identifies the figure holding the monster troop together — use an existing Important Character or create one. If the character can strike them with a Formidable roll of MIGHT, MELEE, or MARKSMANSHIP, the enemy troop loses morale points equal to the successes rolled and must make a morale roll counting only 1s |
| 64–66 | A monster stampede charges the character's position. Roll MOVE or ANIMAL HANDLING against 10 base attack dice. Each success short of the enemy's total inflicts 1 point of damage |

The events above are a framework, not a script. Modify them for the character's specific situation and the battle's current state. The table is a tool for generating drama, not a rulebook entry that must be read aloud.

If the character takes damage from their advantage die result (showing a 1) during the same turn as a battle event, resolve the event only. The two damage sources are not additive — focus on the event and let the die result set the stakes.
