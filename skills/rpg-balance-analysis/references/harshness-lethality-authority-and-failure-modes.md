## Harshness Versus Good Balance

Harshness is not balance.

A harsh rule is healthy only if:

- the danger is legible
- the decision quality is strong
- the consequence feels earned
- the table still has room to respond

### Punitive harshness

Signs:

- surprise punishment
- false or hidden risk
- no real counterplay
- heavy cost for low payoff

### Productive harshness

Signs:

- players understand the risk
- the choice is meaningful
- the consequence reinforces the game's promise

## Recovery And Attrition

Recovery systems define what a game wants to remain costly.

Questions:

- what can be recovered quickly
- what can be recovered only with time
- what cannot be recovered at all
- what recovery channels are gated by resources, social access, or special roles

Balance lesson:

- if a game is built on attrition, recovery is one of the main balance levers, not just a support subsystem

## Lethality And Retirement Pressure

These are different things.

### Dramatic danger

- characters can die
- but the path to death is legible and meaningful

### Arbitrary death

- the line between “fine” and “dead” is too sudden or too hidden

### Unplayable survival

- the character technically lives
- but loses so much agency that play functionally ends

This last category is often misnamed as survivability.

Balance lesson:

- rules should state clearly whether they produce continued play, retirement-default outcomes, or death

## Narrative Authority Balance

Some rules do not mainly change damage or odds.
They change who gets to define reality, knowledge, emotion, or scene framing.

Examples:

- illusion and fear effects
- memory manipulation
- social compulsion
- hidden-information overrides
- GM-facing interpretation rules

Balance risks:

- one player dominates scene truth
- the GM must constantly decide what “really counts”
- other players lose meaningful participation

## Psychological Failure Modes

### Resentment

One player or the GM feels forced to orbit around another rule.

### Regret

The player feels they were baited into a poor choice.

### Helplessness

The rule makes failure feel arbitrary or inescapable.

### Learned caution

The game teaches players to stop engaging with risk because payoff is too weak.

### Learned recklessness

The game teaches that downside is fake, so risk loses meaning.

### Spotlight envy

One player feels structurally overshadowed.

### Text mistrust

The table stops believing the rules describe their real incentives honestly.

## Correction Toolkit

Not every problem is solved numerically.

### Numeric fix

Use when:

- success rates
- damage values
- repeat frequency

are the real problem.

### Wording fix

Use when:

- the problem is expectation mismatch
- the text promises the wrong fantasy or hides the real cost

### Trigger fix

Use when:

- an ability goes off too often or in too many contexts

### Cost fix

Use when:

- the effect is good for the game but too cheap

### Scope fix

Use when:

- the rule is doing too many jobs at once

### Structural redesign

Use when:

- the whole incentive pattern is wrong
- the rule depends on GM mercy to be tolerable
