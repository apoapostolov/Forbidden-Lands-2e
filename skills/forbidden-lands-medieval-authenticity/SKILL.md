# Forbidden Lands Medieval Authenticity

Use practical constraints to make the world playable and inhabited. Authenticity
should sharpen choices, supply concrete detail, and explain institutions. It
must not overwrite setting canon, pretend the Ravenlands are historical Europe,
or turn ordinary competence into constant rolls.

## Establish Authority

1. Read the relevant manuscript rule or setting passage.
2. Load `forbidden-lands-lore` for region, kin, religion, chronology, and
   magical truth.
3. Load `forbidden-lands-design` when realism changes mechanics.
4. Load `forbidden-lands-writing-voice` for finished prose.
5. Treat historical patterns as a menu of plausible constraints, not automatic
   canon.

Magic, monsters, the Blood Mist, kin longevity, and setting institutions may
change real-world patterns. Trace those changes instead of ignoring them.

## Use the Constraint Chain

Turn background into play:

`material condition → habitual response → institution or custom → visible sign → player choice → consequence`

Example:

`scarce dry grain → shared granaries → measured household obligations → sealed
bins and public tallies → bargain, steal, contribute, or seek another source →
status, hunger, debt, or conflict`

This chain is more valuable than isolated trivia.

## Start with Ordinary Competence

People who survive in the setting know routine work:

- lighting and tending fires
- drying clothing and bedding
- loading animals
- preserving food
- maintaining tools
- reading local weather
- recognizing ordinary danger

Summarize competent routine. Focus detail when:

- conditions are unusually bad
- tools or materials are missing
- time creates pressure
- someone is inexperienced, injured, or deceiving others
- failure changes a meaningful choice

Do not demand rolls for every fire, meal, repair, or mile.

## Choose the Authenticity Dial

State the intended level:

- **Texture:** one or two credible details; no new procedure.
- **Constraint:** material reality changes available options or cost.
- **Procedure:** repeated play needs a short rule or resource loop.
- **Campaign engine:** logistics, law, trade, or season is central to the
  supplement.

Use the lowest level that fulfills the design promise. A vignette usually needs
texture. A winter-expedition supplement may need a campaign engine.

## Respect Setting Divergence

Ask:

- How did ten generations of Blood Mist isolation alter trade, law, kinship,
  roads, and authority?
- How do non-human kin and long lives alter inheritance and memory?
- Which crafts or institutions become possible because of magic?
- Which magical practice is rare, costly, distrusted, or controlled?
- How do monsters change land use, burial, herding, architecture, and taboo?
- What knowledge survived locally but ceased to circulate?

Do not add banks, postal systems, police, standardized courts, mass literacy, or
industrial supply chains by habit. Do not forbid every organized institution
merely because the setting is harsh.

## Write Lived Detail

Prefer details a person handles or notices:

- damp wool steaming by the hearth
- a cracked haft rebound with wet rawhide
- grain measured before witnesses
- a road remembered by drainage and grazing, not distance alone
- a wound judged by heat, smell, swelling, and function

Use one exact detail that affects the scene. Avoid catalogs of research facts,
archaic vocabulary displays, and explanations that characters would not give
one another.

## Model Institutions through Need

An institution exists because it coordinates something:

- food storage
- grazing and land use
- defense
- water
- worship and burial
- compensation and feud control
- trade and measurement
- care for dependents
- seasonal labor

Show who contributes, who decides, who records or remembers, who benefits, and
who can be excluded. Every institution needs limits and an enforcement method.

## Make Realism Playable

Good realism creates:

- preparation choices
- tradeoffs between speed, safety, warmth, secrecy, and load
- bargaining over scarce labor or goods
- obligations with several possible settlements
- evidence players can interpret
- consequences that follow from material causes

Bad realism creates:

- repetitive maintenance rolls
- unavoidable penalties without counterplay
- specialist knowledge required from the player but not the character
- permanent incapacity presented as routine fun
- social restriction that removes character agency
- “historical accuracy” used to excuse gratuitous cruelty

## Handle Social Difference Carefully

Avoid a single universal “medieval society.” Customs vary by region, settlement,
kin, wealth, faith, ecology, and recent history.

When depicting hierarchy or prejudice:

- identify the institution and interest maintaining it
- show disagreement, evasion, bargaining, and change
- preserve meaningful player choices
- distinguish setting fact from one speaker's belief
- avoid treating modern stereotypes as historical inevitability

Authenticity describes pressures; it does not dictate one moral or social order.

## Research and Claim Discipline

Classify claims:

- **Manuscript fact:** directly supported by current text.
- **Historical pattern:** plausible real-world analogue.
- **Design inference:** likely consequence of established setting conditions.
- **Authorial invention:** new choice made for play.

Label inference and invention during design. In final prose, integrate them
naturally but do not present unsupported specifics as established canon.

For exact historical claims, contested customs, or technical craft procedures,
verify with reliable primary scholarship before making them load-bearing.

## Authenticity Workflow

1. Name the scene or subsystem's practical question.
2. Set the authenticity dial.
3. Identify material conditions: season, tools, labor, time, transport, and
   storage.
4. Identify social conditions: authority, obligation, custom, witnesses, and
   enforcement.
5. Check setting divergences: Mist, magic, monsters, kin, and regional lore.
6. Choose one visible detail and one meaningful constraint.
7. Offer at least two credible responses.
8. Make consequences local and causal.
9. Remove trivia and bookkeeping that do not change play.
10. Run lore, design, and voice checks.

## Audit Questions

- Could people sustain this food, labor, transport, or building practice?
- Where do materials come from, and who controls them?
- Who performs the work, how long does it take, and what displaces it?
- How is knowledge taught, remembered, and verified?
- What happens when weather, injury, shortage, or conflict interrupts routine?
- Which relationship or institution absorbs the risk?
- What evidence would adventurers see?
- Which decision becomes more interesting because of this detail?
- Does magic solve the problem, move its cost, or create a new institution?
- Is the resulting rule worth its table time?

## Final Quality Gate

- [ ] Historical analogy is distinguished from setting canon.
- [ ] Material inputs, labor, time, and transport are plausible.
- [ ] Institutions have needs, contributors, beneficiaries, and enforcement.
- [ ] Regional and magical divergences are traced.
- [ ] Ordinary competence is compressed.
- [ ] Detail appears through action, object, body, or consequence.
- [ ] Realism creates choices rather than automatic punishment.
- [ ] Social customs vary and preserve room for human agency.
- [ ] Exact technical claims are verified when load-bearing.
- [ ] Lore, rules, and prose remain consistent.
