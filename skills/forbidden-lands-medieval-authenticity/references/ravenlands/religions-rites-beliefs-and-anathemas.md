# Religions of the Ravenlands: Rites, Beliefs, and Anathemas

<!-- markdownlint-disable MD024 -->

**Category:** Ravenlands-specific — an authenticity reference for the gods,
religious orders, kin traditions, household observances, and sacred disputes of
the Forbidden Lands.

Religion in the Ravenlands is not a list of divine concerns. It determines who
may witness an oath, whose calendar governs work, how a body is buried, which
knowledge may be kept, what a household owes after a death, and whether an
unusual child is blessed, hidden, or claimed. A rite without such consequences
is decoration.

This document develops the practices implied by the manuscript. The named gods,
orders, migrations, and kin beliefs are setting facts. Detailed prayers, local
ceremonies, and prohibitions are grounded setting inferences or local
inventions unless the text identifies them as widespread. The Blood Mist
isolated worshippers for generations. No priesthood achieved complete
uniformity.

## Authority and Religious Knowledge

### Four Kinds of Religious Statement

Keep religious information at the level where it belongs.

- **Public observance** is visible and broadly recognized: a Raven Sister tends
  births, dwarves dedicate work to Huge, goblins sacrifice to Eor by night, and
  Aslenes enlarge the festival fire for Horn.
- **Local custom** belongs to a place, household, clan, or teacher. A ford may
  require a copper pin for Flow while the next valley gives her the first milk.
- **Contested doctrine** is a claim with living opponents. Whether Raven or
  Wyrm was the divine Protector has caused exile, war, and execution.
- **Hidden truth** is withheld from ordinary believers or belongs to the GM.
  Ritual specialists may know that a useful rite works for reasons contrary to
  their sermon. Such truth should leave evidence before it receives a name.

The same act may carry several explanations. A household pours out the first
cup of water because Flow must be thanked, because the dead drink first, because
the well once carried fever, or because no one wants to be the first to stop.
Do not resolve every explanation. Continued practice can be more stable than
agreement.

### Canon, Inference, and Local Invention

The three faces of the Protector, the Raven Sisters, the Reapenters, the Rust
and Heme Church, the Older Gods, the dwarven World Expansion, the elven rubies,
the cults of Horn and Eor, wolfkin Huntress worship, and the named druidic
orders are manuscript facts.

The Feather-and-Coil theology below is an old household synthesis built from
Raven and Wyrm traditions. It does not erase the institutional schism. Some
Raven and Wyrm clergy accept it as inherited wisdom. Others condemn it as a
political compromise that blurs the true Protector.

Kin customs without a named canonical priesthood are presented as plausible
religious traditions, not universal law. Orc, saurian, ogre, and whiner beliefs
vary sharply because no single temple speaks for those kin.

### What a Religion Must Do

A durable Ravenlands religion answers ordinary questions:

- Who names a child and recognizes a household?
- Which promises bind the living, and who remembers them?
- When may work begin, cease, or change hands?
- Who tends dangerous birth, illness, weather, and death?
- What happens to property, obligation, and memory after burial?
- Which knowledge is taught, hidden, or destroyed?
- What can a person do after breaking faith?

Doctrine survives when it inhabits these decisions. A god who appears only in
songs will lose ground to a priest who keeps measures, knows wounds, settles
inheritance, or commands armed riders.

## How Religion Is Lived

### Household, Shrine, and Order

Most worship takes place at three scales. The household keeps brief customs
around hearth, bed, store, birth, and meal. The settlement shrine holds public
objects, witnesses vows, and coordinates festival work. An order or traveling
priesthood carries doctrine, specialist knowledge, distant alliances, and
political claims.

The scales need not agree. A village may pay a Rust priest, ask a Wyrm keeper
to record a marriage, send secretly for a Raven Sister during labor, and leave
the first clean water to Flow. People do not experience this as a tidy pantheon.
They know which power is approached for which danger and which observance can
be admitted before whom.

Under the Blood Mist, household rites continued when distant instruction did
not. A shrine might keep the words of a prayer and forget the date, preserve a
gesture but attach it to another god, or use the broken half of an object whose
other half lay beyond a lethal night's travel. Local error became ancestry.

### Offerings Are Transfers

An offering removes something from ordinary use and places it under a sacred
claim. Grain left at a shrine cannot feed a child without an act of taking.
Iron dedicated to Huge cannot be melted for a knife without answering the
smith who witnessed the dedication. A cord tied for an oath remains evidence
after the people who tied it disagree.

Small offerings dominate daily practice: a mouthful, a shaving of wood, the
first coal, a bent pin, a twist of wool, a spoken name, a period of labor. Large
offerings appear when danger, guilt, or political display requires them. Rank
often decides whose food, animal, blood, or life can be substituted by silver.

### Prayer Is Usually an Act

People pray while doing. A woman wets the threshold before sweeping and names
Flow. A smith touches the cold anvil before the first heat and names Huge. An
Aslene passes a hand through a horse's mane before adding wood to Horn's fire.
A goblin turns a stolen object to the moon and tells Eor where it came from.

Spoken prayer is short because the god already knows the danger. Long prayer
belongs to vows, funerals, public judgment, initiation, and attempts to make
other people hear the speaker's standing.

### Anathema and Ordinary Failure

An anathema is more than conduct a god dislikes. It places a person or act
outside the relationships a religion knows how to repair. Most faults can be
answered through restitution, changed conduct, fasting, service, public words,
or another rite. Anathema threatens the faith's basic account of the world.

One tradition may forbid what another requires. Raven oath-keepers condemn
false witness; Eor's priests may regard a lie that protects the cradle as holy.
Huge condemns the deliberate abandonment of sound work; Clay may require a
finished structure to be broken when it prevents living change. These conflicts
should create bargains and divided loyalties, not a universal moral chart.

## Raven and Wyrm: the Feather and the Coil

### The Older Duality

Before the churches made the crossing into a proof of one divine animal against
the other, many households told the story as a pair. Raven found the way.
Wyrm carried what must survive the journey. Raven flew in open air where every
direction could be judged. Wyrm remained coiled beneath the feathers, close to
warmth, hunger, and the bodies crowded on the ships.

In this theology, Raven is duty made visible. Raven governs the portion of life
that must be borne before others: a given word, an accepted office, a debt to
kin, the defense of a guest, and judgment after death. Raven does not promise
that a duty is fair. The bird asks whether it was yours and whether you carried
it.

Wyrm holds the feminine half of the pair. This is the old language of the
tradition, not a claim that only women embody it. Wyrm governs gestation,
continuity, bodily knowledge, household memory, adaptation, healing, concealed
counsel, and the preservation of life through change. Raven keeps a word in
public. Wyrm remembers the conditions under which it was given and the mouths
that depended upon it.

Most old households place Wyrm's offices in women's hands: the keys to food and
cloth, knowledge of births, the private terms of marriages, care of the sick,
and memory of which kin can be asked for help. This gives women authority over
continuity while making them answer for every failure of fertility, health, or
domestic peace. A man may carry the same offices after death, injury, aptitude,
or necessity. Some communities call him Wyrm-wise. Others use the title to
belittle him while depending on his work.

The feminine side is therefore neither gentleness nor obedience. Wyrm can hide,
constrict, poison, shed, and survive underground. Her virtues include patience,
indirection, bodily competence, remembered grievance, and refusal to let public
honor consume the next generation. Those virtues can preserve a household or
make its keeper impossible to challenge from within.

The pairing is unequal in different places. Raven churches often elevate duty
over continuity. Serpent congregations say an oath that destroys the household
it was meant to protect has eaten its own tail wrongly. Household teachers
answer with an older warning: the feather without the coil becomes cold law;
the coil without the feather becomes a secret no one can judge.

### Wyrd and the Given Word

Ravenlands oath-teachers use **wyrd** for the shape made by birth, deed,
accident, promise, and consequence. It is neither a fixed future nor private
desire. A person receives conditions they did not choose, then adds choices
that later people must live within.

A spoken promise enters wyrd because another person changes conduct in reliance
on it. The wrong in oath-breaking lies partly in the broken sentence and partly
in the work, journey, marriage, or risk undertaken because the sentence was
believed. Compensation must therefore address reliance rather than insult
alone.

Raven witnesses the word. Wyrm witnesses what grew around it.

### The Cord and Feather Oath

A widespread local oath rite uses a dark feather and a wool cord. The parties
state the promise outdoors or beneath an opening in the roof. A witness knots
the cord once for each obligation while another repeats the names of people who
will depend on it. The feather is passed through the final loop.

The promisor keeps the feather. The person relying on the promise keeps the
cord. Neither object proves the exact words alone. Together, before witnesses,
they show that a bond existed.

When the promise is fulfilled, the feather is burned and the cord untied. If it
is broken, the cord remains knotted. Some households sew such knots inside a
burial cloth so the dead carry an unsettled word to judgment.

### Marriage under Feather and Coil

The partners stand on opposite sides of a covered bowl. Public promises are
spoken with hands above the cloth: protection, labor, fidelity as locally
defined, property, or care of dependents. Private conditions are whispered
with hands beneath it, touching the same coil of cord.

The public words belong to Raven and can be judged by the settlement. The
private conditions belong to Wyrm and are known to the couple, a chosen keeper,
and anyone whose welfare depends upon them. This division recognizes that a
household cannot expose every matter without ceasing to be a household.

Abuse of the division is an old problem. Powerful people hide public injustice
under Wyrm's secrecy. Kin expose private bodily matters in Raven's court to win
property. Good officiants ask which part of a dispute must be seen and which
part must remain held.

### Birth, Naming, and Taking Duty

A newborn is first shown low, near bed or earth, and named to Wyrm as a life
still folding into itself. A surviving child is later carried outdoors and
named to Raven before the household. The delay may be a few days or a season,
depending on infant danger and local custom.

At the first adult oath, the young person chooses or accepts a duty: a share of
watch, care of an elder, maintenance of a path, service to a shrine, or a trade
obligation. The choice need not determine a profession. It announces that the
person's word can now cause others to act.

A youth who refuses is not necessarily expelled. They may remain unnamed in
public agreements, unable to witness or stand surety until some duty is taken.
The exclusion matters more than a sermon.

### Judgment of the Dead

Feather-and-Coil funerals ask two accounts. The Raven account names promises,
offices, witnesses, and unfinished public obligations. The Wyrm account names
people fed, bodies tended, knowledge preserved, necessary changes made, and
harms concealed when they should have been exposed.

No human officiant claims to decide the final judgment. The accounts determine
what the living must settle. A disputed oath may delay inheritance. A hidden
act of care may release a debtor. A cord found still knotted in the grave goods
can reopen a quarrel generations later.

The body is buried in a curve rather than a straight line where ground permits.
A black feather is set above the heart or at the mouth. Carrion birds taking it
are read as acceptance by Raven. Worms appearing quickly in cold soil are read
as Wyrm receiving the body. Both signs can be counterfeited, and families have
done so.

### Dogmas of the Pair

The oldest teachers repeat six propositions, though the wording changes:

1. A word becomes heavier when another life rests on it.
2. Duty without memory becomes cruelty.
3. Care without witness can become possession.
4. The dead are judged by burdens carried and life preserved.
5. No one sees the whole road from the air or the whole household from the
   ground.
6. What must endure may bend; what must be trusted must sometimes remain
   straight.

These are arguments as often as teachings. Raven and Wyrm partisans place a
different proposition first.

### Anathemas of Feather and Coil

The paired tradition treats the following as grave breaches:

- giving a witnessed word while secretly arranging that it cannot be kept
- using another person's oath after releasing oneself from the reciprocal duty
- exposing a protected household confidence for vanity or profit
- hiding violence, theft, or coercion under the claim that all domestic matters
  belong to Wyrm
- denying care solely to force a public promise from someone under bodily need
- falsifying the cord, feather, witnesses, or reliance attached to an oath
- burying a person with a settled cord still knotted in order to stain their
  judgment

Restitution must repair the life built around the false word. Some breaches
cannot be repaired because the dependent person is dead, displaced, or changed
beyond recovery. The offender may still serve those who inherited the loss.

## The Congregation of the Serpent

### Faith and Dogma

The Congregation teaches that Wyrm was the divine Protector and Raven the holy
carrier. Its ouroboros shows continuity through destruction: the end feeds the
beginning, memory passes through death, and a law survives by being renewed
rather than merely repeated.

Psychopomps guide transitions. They tend the dying, interpret inheritance,
record births and marriages, preserve letters, reconcile old terms with new
conditions, and conduct the dead toward whatever follows. The library at
Farhaven gives the order an authority rare in the Ravenlands: it can compare a
village memory with a text copied before the Mist.

The church values preservation, but preservation does not mean refusal to
change. A rule may shed its outer wording if its purpose is kept. This doctrine
makes Wyrm clergy patient negotiators and dangerous editors. Opponents accuse
them of calling any convenient alteration continuity.

### Household Practice

Wyrm households coil cord inside storage lids, bed frames, and cradle handles.
The cord counts continuities: a marriage, a surviving child, land held through
famine, a debt carried rather than denied. Strangers do not see it.

At the evening meal, the last portion is returned to the pot rather than eaten.
It belongs to tomorrow. During scarcity the portion may be tiny, but consuming
it deliberately means admitting that continuity has failed. A starving family
may eat it after a Psychopomp unknots the household cord and names the crisis
before witnesses.

Written words receive bodily care. Pages are aired, wax warmed, ink kept from
frost, and damaged texts copied before ornament. A literate keeper who lets a
book rot through laziness commits a religious fault as well as waste.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**The Shedding** settles a life change that cannot honestly be called temporary.
The person removes an old garment, title cord, household token, or lock of hair,
states what has ended, and circles the shrine once without it. A witness then
names what obligations survived the change. Divorce, exile, recovery from long
illness, release from service, and adoption can all require a Shedding.

**The Closed Circle** is used for death. The dying person's unresolved names
are spoken inside a cord laid as an open coil. After death, kin settle what they
can. At burial the Psychopomp closes the coil. A name deliberately omitted may
become a restless claim.

**The Long Reading** occurs when a text affects several households. The whole
document is read aloud, including repeated formulas and inconvenient clauses.
Listeners may interrupt only to ask the meaning of a word. Judgment comes the
next day, after everyone has slept beneath the same terms.

### Anathemas

The Congregation condemns:

- destroying a unique record to free oneself from its claim
- severing a child, dependent, or dead person from their known name for profit
- presenting alteration as continuity without naming what was lost
- closing an inheritance or funeral account while a known claimant remains
  deliberately unheard
- killing a messenger or psychopomp under protection of passage
- wasting seed, medicine, or preserved knowledge to make a display of purity

The hardest cases concern dangerous knowledge. Some Wyrm keepers preserve texts
the Rust Brothers would burn. Others seal such texts and deny them to ordinary
readers, arguing that continuity sometimes requires restraint. The argument has
no settled end.

## The Raven Church

### Faith and Dogma

The Raven Church teaches that Raven was the divine Protector who led humankind
across the sea. The serpent was a fertile creature carried for the life of the
new land. Raven sees roads, boundaries, battle, carrion, and the pattern of a
life from above. This sight gives judgment its authority.

Among Ailanders, duty is tempered by deliberation. A given word binds because
the community heard it, but a spokesperson cannot manufacture consent by
speaking first. Raven's law demands that responsibility be visible. Secret
power attracts suspicion even when used toward good ends.

Raven Sisters hold a less legal and more life-affirming tradition. Healing,
birth, harvest, hunting, love, shapeshifting, and sight make the church useful
inside ordinary households. Their long persecution has also made secrecy a
discipline. They must reconcile public duty with hidden survival every day.

### Daily Practice

A Raven household airs bedding and opens shutters at first useful light, even
in cold weather, so concealed sickness can be seen. A feather, black thread, or
forked twig may be kept above the door. The object matters less than who renews
it and what promise was spoken there.

Before travel, a person names the intended road and return before someone who
can report an absence. The rite turns disappearance into an obligation. If the
traveler does not return, the witness must raise search, settle a debt, or at
least tell the household where the road began.

Raven Sisters often take payment in future acts: shelter for another Sister,
care of a patient, seed passed to a poor household, or a message carried. This
creates a dispersed church that can survive without a treasury. It also creates
debts no village elder can fully count.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**The First Flight** recognizes a person able to carry their own word. The
candidate climbs to a roof, hill, tree platform, or gate walk and names a duty
within hearing of the settlement. They descend by another path if one exists.
Falling is an accident, not automatically an omen, though few families believe
that in the moment.

**The Open Hand** marks healing. The patient, healer, and one household witness
state what was done, what remains uncertain, and what payment or service follows.
The rite protects healers from blame for every death and patients from hidden
claims invented afterward.

**The Black Wing Vigil** accompanies the dead. Someone remains outdoors or at
an opening until dawn, watching which birds approach and speaking the dead
person's unsettled promises. Food is kept from carrion birds during the vigil;
afterward, birds may feed from a separate offering.

**Raven's Judgment** is a public accounting used when oath, office, or communal
trust is disputed. Testimony is heard in the open. Silence is permitted, but a
person who refuses to answer cannot later claim that the assembly heard their
case. The judgment concerns standing and restitution before punishment.

### Anathemas

The church condemns false witness, abandonment of a sworn dependent, secret
exercise of public office, refusal to account for communal stores, deliberate
misdirection of a traveler under guest protection, and preventing the dead
from being named so their property can be seized.

Raven Sisters add grave prohibitions against withholding ordinary healing to
force conversion, using shapeshifting to violate household confidence, and
claiming a natural sign guarantees guilt. They know too well how a feather,
birthmark, or animal shape can be turned into an accusation.

Persecution complicates these teachings. A Sister may lie about her name or
route to survive. Most Raven theologians distinguish a word given freely from
an answer extorted by someone who has already broken guest law. Rust priests
call the distinction proof that Raven worship excuses deceit.

## The Reapenters

### The Corax Doctrine

The Reapenters worship Raven as Corax, the carrion face awakened by human
violation of the Shift. They teach that the lifted Mist is a final examination,
not mercy. Human presence itself is the unpaid offense, and no ordinary act can
settle it while humans remain in the land.

Their doctrine takes Raven's public duty and removes every reciprocal limit.
The cult member owes truth and protection to the chosen remnant, nothing to the
people marked for removal. Murder becomes migration enforced upon the unwilling.

Reapenters can therefore live quietly among intended victims. The next death is
weighed against access to a later gathering, road, store, or household. Their
patience is religious, not merciful.

### Rites and Practices

The response **Nevermore** follows a recital of human offenses. New members add
one local offense to the list, often the destruction of a grove, betrayal of an
oath, collaboration with Rust, or occupation of land claimed by older kin.
Specific wrongs are used to support an unlimited sentence.

In **the Empty Roost**, members leave one place unoccupied at a meal for the
last human who will die. No one claims to know whose place it is. A member who
sits there by accident must fast, leave the cell, or undertake a killing.

Bodies exposed for carrion enact Corax's reclamation. Render the practice
through the closed road, missing kin, feathers caught in a tree, and the labor
of those who must recover what remains. Description of dismemberment adds
little to play.

### Anathemas

The cult condemns permanent peace with human settlement, childbirth celebrated
as an unqualified good, burial that denies carrion, protection of a human life
at the cost of the purge, and any teaching that the Shift can be repaired
without human departure or death.

Mercy toward one person can be tolerated only as a means to later killing. A
member who discovers that attachment has become the true reason for mercy is
judged corrupted. Defection begins at that recognition, though the cult calls
it cowardice.

## Rust and Heme

Rust and Heme receive a full treatment in [Rust Brothers: Fear, Doctrine, and
Control](rust-brothers-fear-doctrine-and-control.md). The church worships iron
and wood as the Protector's divine gifts, treats humanity as material to be
worked, joins fertility to disease and sacrifice, collects tribute, disciplines
knowledge, and governs through selective terror.

Its central rites include the Censer Circuit, the Great Measure, the Joining of
Iron and Wood, the Last Weighing, the Burning of the Copy, ordination under
demonic exposure, and human sacrifice entered as tribute. Its anathemas protect
the church's monopoly over sacred material, dangerous knowledge, allegiance,
and rank.

The church offers real services: healing, armed protection, removal of restless
dead, road control, record keeping, and expertise with demonic danger. These
services carry inspection, debt, recruitment, confiscation, or obedience. The
competence is part of the horror.

## The Vampyr Heresy

### Blood as the First Substance

Vampyrs claim that they preserve the true rite of Rust and that the Rust
Brothers corrupted it through demonic power. They call blood the divine
substance and themselves humankind's firstborn. In their teaching, iron is holy
because it answers to what blood already contains, not because a priest leaves
it to corrode.

The charge against the Rust church can be accurate without making the heresy
humane. Vampyr doctrine replaces one monopoly with another. Knowledge belongs
to the firstborn, ordinary humans exist under inherited obligation, and feeding
can be described as tribute owed by lesser branches of the same stock.

### Faith and Practice

Vampyr houses keep descent more carefully than marriage. A blood ledger records
who fed whom, which household received protection, which child showed signs of
the first blood, and which obligation passed through death. The record makes
predation look ancestral.

Blood taken under ordinary feeding is not necessarily consumed at once. A
small portion may be dried on cloth, wood, or iron and stored with the giver's
name. Possession lets the house claim that the person entered a sacred relation
even when the taking was coerced.

Some human clients accept the arrangement for protection, medicine, or status.
Their consent binds no one else, but vampyr teachers may extend it to spouses,
children, tenants, and descendants.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**The Red Witness** binds an agreement. Each party stains a separate strip of
cloth, and a vampyr elder knots the strips together around dull iron. The vampyr
keeps the knot. Human participants often keep no independent record unless they
insist before the rite.

**Keeping the Line** recognizes succession. The heir names every living source
from which the house may lawfully feed according to its own account. Challenges
concern protection given, consent, substitution, and whether an obligation died
with the person who made it.

**The First Cup** receives a human client into protected service. The patron
drinks before the client eats. Food, shelter, or medicine follows. The order of
the exchange teaches that continued human life descends from firstborn appetite.

### Dogmas and Anathemas

The heresy teaches that blood remembers descent, the firstborn carry authority
that later humans lost, and divine substance must circulate through a rightful
order. A patron who feeds without keeping the dependent alive has wasted both
tribute and claim.

Anathemas include surrendering firstborn knowledge to the Rust church, allowing
a protected source to be consumed to death through greed, falsifying descent,
destroying a blood ledger to free dependents, and accepting that an ordinary
human can judge a vampyr without firstborn witness.

Defectors most often reject the inherited reach of consent. A person may have
agreed to feed a protector during famine. Their grandchild did not. The heresy
survives by making that distinction sinful.

## Huge and the Dwarven Work

### The World under Construction

Huge made the dwarves to fill the void with matter. The world is a sphere still
under construction, the sun is Huge's Hearth, and the stars are Outer Forges
that await the builders after they reach him. Dwarven labor is therefore
cosmological. A retaining wall, mine stair, road bed, and apparently useless
foundation can all extend the made world against emptiness.

Huge judges skill, knowledge, and duty more readily than inward virtue. The
dead rest and train in a parallel workshop until the god reshapes them for
another shift. A new body may reward service or correct a badly used talent.
This belief dignifies ordinary work and permits brutal judgments about those
declared idle, incompetent, or misplaced.

Dwarven sects disagree over the next layer. Belderranians emphasize royal
direction and stone-song. Meromannians say lost works must be recovered before
new ones begin. Canides defend the surface where growth and weather attack
stone. Crombes keep and guard what others would expand. Each faction can accuse
another of building in the wrong order.

### Faith in Daily Work

A dwarf greets a task by touching raw material, tool, and finished neighboring
work in that order. The gesture places today between what the world supplied
and what earlier hands completed. If no finished work is present, a tool once
used by a teacher may stand in its place.

Measurements carry moral weight. A false measure wastes matter and endangers
the work of people not yet born. Apprentices learn established dimensions with
the names of failures attached: the span that sagged, the brace that split, the
king whose ornamental face weakened a gate.

Scrap is sorted rather than discarded. Stone chips fill voids. Bent iron is
reworked. Ash enters mortar or midden. Material too spoiled for use is carried
to a marked crack and named before disposal, lest waste be hidden as clean
work.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**Opening the Shift** begins a major work. The master strikes the intended
surface once with an unhafted tool, names who ordered the labor, who supplies
it, and who will maintain it. Workers answer with the part they personally
accept. Anyone omitted may later deny liability for failure.

**The Buried Tool** dedicates a foundation. A worn but serviceable tool is laid
where the first course will cover it. Burying a useless tool insults Huge;
dedication must cost future work. Poor crews often bury a good chisel and spend
the next year cursing their piety.

**The Reckoning of Hands** follows collapse or fatal failure. Surviving workers
reconstruct the sequence without regard to rank. Tool marks, fractures,
measurements, and orders are examined. The aim is repair, but blame determines
compensation, office, and whose name is cut from the next stone.

**The Last Shift** prepares the dead. A dwarf is laid beside one unfinished
object and one sound object they helped make. The unfinished piece states that
more work awaits. The sound piece proves what skill should be carried into the
next body. A liar may place another dwarf's work beside the dead. Stone Singers
claim Huge hears the difference.

### Dogmas and Anathemas

Huge's faithful hold that material resists falsehood, competence outranks
boasting, every structure creates a duty of maintenance, and descendants are
real parties to present construction. Completion is holy only when the work can
bear what was promised.

Grave offenses include concealing a structural fault, claiming another hand's
work, destroying sound work for vanity, wasting rare material to display rank,
teaching a dangerous method without its limits, abandoning dependents inside a
failing work, and entering the forbidden elder layers after the clans have
withdrawn their right.

Belial the Loose-Tongued supplies the darkest warning. Secret craft taught
without duty can strengthen the world or arm its enemies. Dwarves disagree
whether the offense was disclosure, betrayal of trust, or failure to control
the result. Every guild chooses the version that protects its own authority.

## Clay and the Druidic Traditions

### The Shaper Who Does Not Rule

Clay shaped the world at the Protector's behest and holds craft, life, and the
magic of creation. Clay is sexless. Followers use titles drawn from the task at
hand: Maker at birth, Mender in healing, Potter in transformation, and Teacher
when knowledge passes between unlike beings.

Elves and Elvenspring tend to honor Clay as a master teacher rather than a
sovereign. The world was shaped, then left capable of further shaping. Life is
therefore neither untouched purity nor raw material without claims. A druid may
prune, cull, graft, heal, move, and sometimes kill, but must understand the
living relation altered by the act.

Clay doctrine holds that form carries memory. A scar shows adaptation. A path
shows repeated feet. A worked field holds human intention and the soil's answer.
The dogma can produce care or arrogance. Druids who believe they read the true
form of a place may ignore the people living there.

### Clay, Soil, and Maha

Clay worship uses unfired figures for conditions still open to change and fired
tablets for statements meant to endure. Maha pictograms do not converse. They
state something about reality or, through magic, press reality toward the
statement.

A household figure may receive a wound, seed, animal hair, or impression of a
missing tool. It is kept damp while treatment, search, or repair continues.
When the matter ends, the figure is returned to mud. Firing it would make the
temporary condition into a claim of permanence.

Druids test soil with fingers, smell, water, and plant response before ritual.
The ceremony follows diagnosis. Clay handled without knowledge is piety without
craft, which most orders distrust.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**Taking Form** marks adoption, apprenticeship, transformation, or a chosen
office. The person shapes a small vessel while witnesses name what the new form
must hold. The vessel remains deliberately imperfect. A sealed, flawless vessel
would imply no room for learning.

**Returning the Shape** accompanies death, release, and the end of a living
arrangement. An unfired likeness is softened in water and pressed into soil.
The rite does not claim that the person vanishes. It returns fixed likeness to
materials other lives can use.

**The Necessary Cut** authorizes culling. The druid names the population,
disease, hunger, or imbalance requiring death, then kills the selected animal
or plant cleanly and accounts for what will use the body. Witnesses may dispute
the diagnosis. They do not pretend that refusing every cut preserves life.

**The Maha Setting** fires a tablet only after its statement is tested aloud by
teachers and people affected by it. A mistaken mark can make error durable.
Some temples keep failed tablets in walls where students can see the cracks.

### Anathemas

Clay traditions condemn creation abandoned without provision, shaping a living
being merely to display power, fixing a temporary suffering into identity,
sterilizing useful soil without necessity, concealing a failed magical creation,
and writing Maha about a person or place one has refused to study.

Orders disagree over demonic alteration. Maidens tend to treat it as hostile
form imposed upon life. More contemplative druids ask whether altered life can
develop claims of its own. Neither position permits Zytera's coercion. Their
dispute concerns what is owed to the being that survives it.

## The Shardmaiden and the Orders

### Prophet, Division, and Shared Memory

The Shardmaiden was Clay's elven prophet. After Morander's death, she shattered
the ruby in her chest so the Maidens could carry her knowledge and power. The
act is understood as gift, wounding, multiplication, or continuing personhood,
depending on the order.

Her followers hold that knowledge may be divided without becoming ownerless.
Every bearer receives power and an obligation to the source. This gives the
orders a strong doctrine of stewardship. It also gives senior bearers a reason
to control access, travel, marriage, and dangerous inquiry.

The Order of Maidens directs the inheritance toward struggle against demonic
evil and Rust. The Golden Bough directs it toward the long health of forests,
creatures, and Stillelves. Local Elvenspring circles combine both and are
criticized by each.

### Rites and Practices

**Receiving the Shard** joins a Maiden to the order through an emerald fragment
fused into the forehead. The candidate fasts from cultivated food, states the
living things and people she accepts responsibility for, and receives the shard
before witnesses who must tend any failure of the joining.

**The Remembered Absence** honors Morander and other dead companions. A meal is
prepared with one necessary ingredient omitted. Participants name how the
missing thing changes every other taste. The rite teaches loss through the
body, not a speech.

**Walking the Bough** is a Golden Bough survey. Druids follow a set path through
forest and settlement, recording births, disease, predation, felling, water,
and fire. The same route repeated over years lets change become evidence. A
missing fox litter or new charcoal pit matters more than an omen invented at
the shrine.

**The Green Judgment** hears accusations that a druidic act injured the land or
people under care. Non-druids may testify. Elves sometimes delay judgment long
enough that mortal claimants die; Elvenspring insist this is itself a verdict
made by power.

### Dogmas and Anathemas

The Shardmaiden's followers teach that power carried from another life remains
a debt, knowledge must be placed where it can oppose corruption, and care for
nature includes its hunger and violence. They do not promise harmlessness.

Anathemas include selling a shard, using its authority for private coercion,
destroying an elven ruby to distribute power, abandoning a creature changed by
one's own magic, falsifying a survey to protect an ally, and treating mortal
communities as weeds merely because they live quickly.

Some Maidens forbid intimate partnership that competes with the order. Others
cite the Shardmaiden's love for Morander as proof that attachment created the
gift. This disagreement has divided houses without producing a final doctrine.

## Wail

### Wind, Weather, and Unheld Things

Wail is the air goddess, honored by Raven Sisters, elves, Elvenspring, sailors,
herders, millers, and any household whose roof must survive a storm. In Raven
teaching she is the Protector's wife and bears birds through the sky. Other
traditions make her older than the human gods and indifferent to their
marriages.

Wind cannot be stored, fenced, or ordered to return. Wail therefore governs
movement without ownership: weather, breath, rumor, migrating birds, scent,
song carried beyond its singer, and words sent where the speaker cannot follow.
Her faithful distrust claims that something moving can be possessed merely
because it crossed one's land.

The myth of the sea holding Wail and the toothed fish throwing her free teaches
that rescue changes rescuer and rescued. The fish received size and air. A gift
from a god is rarely a restoration of the earlier state.

### Practice and Ceremony

People give Wail things light enough to move: wool fibers, chaff, feather down,
wood shavings, breath through a horn, and strips cut from worn cloth. Good cloth
is not hung casually. A visible prayer on a pass tells travelers that someone
paid material for the wind's attention.

**The Four Breaths** precedes a journey or message. The speaker faces each
direction, breathes once, and names where the road is open, blocked, desired,
and feared. The rite forces practical orientation before divine appeal.

**Roof Binding** is held before the worst weather season. Households repair
thatch and lashings together. The oldest sound binding is cut only after the
new one holds. A priest who blesses an unrepaired roof is mocked even by the
devout.

**Wail's Answer** follows dangerous stillness or sudden storm. People stop
speech long enough to hear loose shutters, trees, animals, water, and distant
horns. The first useful sound determines the work that begins. Interpretation
belongs to experience as much as priesthood.

### Anathemas

Wail's worshippers condemn killing a protected messenger, trapping birds for
ritual display until they die, taking a traveler's news while denying them
shelter, silencing a warning because it harms trade, and claiming ownership of
the windward path or migrating flock.

Whistling into a gale is forbidden in some valleys and required in others.
Both customs descend from the claim that Wail answers challenge. One community
wants her to hear courage; the other has buried the people who tried.

## Flow

### Water with Direction

Flow governs springs, rivers, lakes, cleansing, and water that moves with a
course. She named rivers so they could hold together and have will. Marshes,
left unnamed and undirected, fall outside much of her ordinary cult. Saurians
and goblins who cross such water know that a human prayer to Flow may be a poor
substitute for a pole.

Flow is honored by elves, Elvenspring, villages, and the Raven Church. Her faith
joins practical water care to purification. Cleanliness is not mere moral
symbol. A fouled well kills. A blocked channel floods seed. A spring controlled
by one household can govern a village.

### Daily Faith

The first drawn water is inspected before it is offered. Smell, color, insects,
sediment, and the behavior of animals matter. Pouring bad water at a shrine does
not make it pure; it shows that the worshipper has failed to attend.

Households set aside different vessels for drinking, washing wounds, brewing,
and unclean work where resources permit. The distinction is preached as Flow's
order and maintained because people remember sickness.

Names of local streams are repeated to children with their dangerous seasons,
crossings, and ownership disputes. To know the sacred name without knowing the
spring flood is religious vanity.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**Flow's Delight** invokes the goddess in a well or bucket until water
overflows. Successful water is used to rinse people and structures and kept for
its healing power. The rite also reveals whether a settlement can spare water
for cleansing. During drought, performance may become a political accusation.

**Naming the Course** recognizes a new channel, reopened spring, or altered
ford. People walk as much of the water as they can, note where it enters and
leaves cultivated land, and state the rights attached to it. The name is given
only after the route is known.

**The Shared Cup** settles temporary peace at water. Enemies drink from one
vessel filled by a neutral hand. Violence before both have left the bank is an
offense against Flow and the witness. The protection ends at the stated marker,
which everyone inspects before drinking.

**Returning What Washed** disposes of water used on corpse, disease, or
dangerous magic. It is poured where it cannot return immediately to drinking
water. The officiant names what the water carried. This is sanitation given
memory.

### Anathemas

Flow's cults condemn poisoning a source, hiding upstream death or disease,
altering a channel without warning people below, denying necessary drinking
water during protected parley, and giving a false name to water so an old right
can be evaded.

Dam building creates hard cases. A dwarf may call a dam sound work, a farmer
may call it life, and a Flow keeper may call the drowned course an imprisoned
will. The dispute should be decided through water, labor, and consequence, not
by assuming one god has sole jurisdiction.

## The Nightwalker

### The God Appeased at the Edge

The Nightwalker is the oldest god in common teaching: untamed nature, night,
mystery, disaster, concealed magic, and the force that breaks ordinary harmony.
The moon is his eye. Smoke and cold fog are his spies. Most people do not love
him. They acknowledge what cannot be made safe.

His rites take place at boundaries: the last firelight, the edge of a known
path, the hour when work stops, the place where a dream recurs, or the first
night spent beyond a settlement's day range. A shrine left half-ruined may be
maintained precisely by never completing it.

Nightwalker theology resists total explanation. A priest who claims certainty
about the god has either misunderstood the cult or is selling protection.
Nevertheless, customary prohibitions are exact. People can be uncertain about
why smoke must leave by one opening and still enforce the opening.

### Rites and Practices

At dusk, some households let the hearth smoke thicken for one breath before
opening the vent. The god is shown that nothing inside pretends to escape his
sight. Children are taught not to name a concealed traveler during that breath.

**The Unfinished Shrine** receives offerings from people who survived an
unexplained danger. A stone is brought but not fitted, a board cut but not
nailed, a figure begun but given no face. Completion would claim the disorder
has been mastered.

**Walking without the Moon** is an ordeal for seers and some sorcerers. The
walker crosses a known short route on a moonless night without flame, then
reports what seemed changed. Companions secretly guard the route. A teacher who
removes those guards to make the ordeal pure commits murder under a holy name.

**The Broken Night** answers calamity. Ordinary sleeping order is reversed,
animals brought inside, tools laid outside, or adults and children exchange
places at the hearth. Controlled disorder acknowledges that the usual pattern
failed and lets the community inspect assumptions hidden by routine.

### Anathemas

The cult condemns claiming complete mastery over dream, night, or wild magic;
destroying a warning because its cause is unknown; lighting every hidden place
for the purpose of domination; using the moonless ordeal on an unwilling
person; and rebuilding a Nightwalker shrine into finished, comfortable order.

Some cults give blood to avert disaster. Others say blood offered in fear only
teaches the god where fear lives. This disagreement is old enough that each
side can name settlements lost after following the other's advice.

## Horn

### Fire That Destroys and Fertilizes

Horn is the Aslene fire god, present in the volcano of the homeland and in
every flame kindled from his first fire. His rage burns settlement and pasture;
his ash renews soil. Horses sprang from him with burning manes. Dread raptors
were made as their enemies so gift would not become untested dominion.

Horn's faithful admire force that accepts consequence. Fire must be fed,
watched, and answered for. A rider must care for the horse that grants speed. A
leader who begins a feud must carry its cost before the households that follow.
Glory without provision is smoke.

Quards, Galdanes, and other Aslene groups share the god and dispute almost every
political lesson. Settled Quards bless fortress hearths. Galdanes distrust walls
and carry coals between camps. Each calls the other's practice a forgetting of
the ancestral fire.

### Daily Practice

Fire is transferred rather than casually extinguished when a household moves.
A coal wrapped in ash may travel with the oldest reliable rider. If it dies,
the new flame is received from a host, creating obligation.

Horse care precedes personal display. At a feast, a rider who ornaments a mount
before checking hoof, sweat, water, and feed is ridiculed. Horn gave the animal;
the human contribution begins with maintenance.

Ash from important fires is kept by purpose. Hearth ash enters fields. Funeral
ash marks memory. War-fire ash may be forbidden from cultivated ground until a
settlement is made with those harmed.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**Kindling Horn** enlarges a fire at festival and gathering. Each household
adds fuel and names its source. Green stolen wood smokes badly and reveals both
poor preparation and theft.

**The Mane Oath** binds rider and companion. A cord braided with horsehair is
held between the parties while the promised road, battle, gift, or protection
is named. Cutting the cord does not end the oath unless both ends are returned.

**Ashen Return** receives people displaced by fire or war. They bring ash from
the lost place if any remains, mix it with the new hearth, and state what claim
they retain. Galdane songs carry whole landscapes through this rite.

**The Raptor Trial** sends young riders toward a dread raptor's nest. Local
forms range from observing tracks and returning unseen to stealing a feather or
surviving direct danger. Ambitious leaders make the trial deadlier to prove
their own severity. Families remember which leaders spent children for fame.

### Anathemas

Horn condemns abandoning a horse for convenience, setting destructive fire
without accepting liability, hiding behind followers after calling them to
violence, using another household's carried coal without acknowledgment, and
scattering funeral ash as mockery.

Fraudulent prophets are judged by consequence. A claimant may speak beautifully
about Horn's chosen savior, but the test is what burns, who rides, who eats, and
whose land receives the ash.

## Eor

### Moon, Deception, and the Divided Kin

Eor is the moon goddess of lies, hidden meanings, jealousy, and shame. Her blue
moonstones gave rise to the beings later divided into halflings and goblins.
She turns her face away each month. Both halves worship her: goblins openly,
halflings behind domestic respectability.

Eor's faithful do not teach that every lie is holy. A useful lie reveals which
truths are dangerous, who claims the right to know, and what the speaker will
risk to preserve. Goblins praise deception that secures freedom, prey, or kin.
Halflings often bless the lie that keeps the cradle and the household facade
apart. Penita priests call both practices symptoms of a division that must end.

The moon's change gives the cult its moral grammar. No face is complete at all
times. What is hidden still shapes what is shown. Shame can protect a boundary,
but shame fed too long becomes cruelty toward the person who exposes it.

### Cradle Faith and Daily Practice

The hidden cradles are the strongest Eor institutions. Halfling and goblin
mothers, earth mothers, and shamans cooperate where public custom insists the
half-kin remain separate. Fathers guard outside and accept children without
certainty of descent. The rite makes social parenthood stronger than proof of
blood while concealing the fact from much of daily life.

Halfling households keep harmless-looking lunar customs: round loaves cut only
after moonrise, shutters closed during certain births, blue stones hidden in
garden walls, and a cup left under the table rather than on it. Goblins turn
similar acts outward with moon-painted masks, stolen silver, and sacrifices
made where the sky is visible.

An object taken by a goblin may be shown to Eor before use. The thief names who
believed they owned it and how the taking was done. A dull theft from someone
unable to resist brings little honor. A theft that exposes hoarding, false
security, or a broken bargain becomes a story.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**The Cradle Exchange** transfers newborns to mothers of their visible half-kin.
No public record names the biological mother. Witnesses remember health,
feeding, and obligations instead. The secrecy protects the children and gives
cradle keepers immense power over identity.

**The Two Faces** occurs at full and dark moon. At full moon, a person states a
public reputation they must maintain. At dark moon, before a smaller trusted
circle, they name what the reputation conceals. The second statement does not
automatically excuse the first.

**Eor's Misrule** permits a bounded night of exchanged clothing, false names,
borrowed offices, riddling judgment, and thefts that must be returned by dawn.
The rite exposes resentment without promising safety. A powerful halfling may
learn what the village says when the speaker's face is hidden.

**Penita Seeking** sends moon-priests after the lost blue stones. Before leaving,
a seeker names which present kinship they are willing to undo for restoration.
Many cannot answer. Goblins oppose the quest because reunion defined by Penita
may erase the lives the divided kin have built.

### Anathemas

Eor worship condemns betraying a cradle to outsiders, claiming certainty of
fatherhood to seize a child, using a protected confession from the dark-moon
rite as common gossip, and telling a lie that places all risk on someone who did
not consent to carry it.

Halfling orthodoxy may treat public admission of goblin kinship as dangerous
indecency. Goblins treat denial of a halfling in mortal danger as the worse
offense. Penita condemn deliberate destruction of a moonstone or refusal even
to consider reunion. The three positions cannot all govern the same crisis.

## The Red Wanderer and Elven Memory

### The Star and the Ruby

The Red Wanderer is a red star moving across the sky. Elves say their rubies
fell from it as seeds. Dwarves dispute the claim and place the rubies within
Huge's making. Most elves treat the Wanderer as origin and direction rather
than a commanding god. It gives no law anyone can quote with confidence.

The ruby changes every religious category attached to elven life. Flesh can be
lost and grown again. Shape can change. Blood descent carries less weight than
memory, teaching, and the custody of a ruby. Final death comes when the stone is
shattered, not when a body stops breathing.

The star's wandering also prevents a simple homeland myth. Elves did not descend
from a fixed northern mountain or promised valley. They came from something
that still moves. Stillelves read this as permission to withdraw from temporary
claims. Redrunners read it as command to remain in motion and recover what was
lost.

### Ruby Practice

An elf touching another's ruby without permission crosses a boundary deeper
than touching naked flesh. Healers and restorers state each intended contact
before beginning. In emergency they may act first, but must later give a full
account to the restored elf or those who hold the claim.

Rubies recovered from jewelry, altars, laboratories, and battlefields are
wrapped so that light reaches them through a narrow opening. The carrier speaks
the place-name at each halt. Elves say the person within should not wake in
dreams believing they have been buried.

Dreams caused by a ruby worn as human ornament are treated as speech under
constraint. The dreamer may receive memory, accusation, deception, or the
confusion of a person without flesh. No reputable elf calls one dream a clear
consent.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**The First Reflection** recognizes a young elf whose ruby has grown enough to
hold a stable self. The elf chooses a shape to maintain for one season while
teachers and companions learn its details. The chosen body is neither permanent
nor trivial. It is the first form for which the young elf accepts social
recognition.

**Keeping the Stone** follows bodily death. Companions clean and inspect the
ruby, recount what happened to the body, and decide whether immediate
restoration, travel to the Stillmist, or watchful delay best honors the elf.
Custody is recorded through witnesses because affectionate possession can still
be captivity.

**The Long Rooting** accompanies an elf joining ruby to tree and becoming an
Ent. The chosen ground is watched through several seasons. Soil, water, nearby
settlement, and obligations to younger elves are settled before the joining.
A withdrawal that destroys everyone else's claim is not called wisdom merely
because it takes centuries.

**The Red Run** is kept by militant elves. A recovered ruby, flower, dried sea
thing, or red-star token is carried between known shelters without sleeping in
the same place twice. The route renews contacts and tests whether the world has
changed faster than old intelligence.

### Anathemas

Elven traditions condemn shattering a ruby, trading one as an object, denying a
restorable elf access to skilled restoration as coercion, altering a helpless
elf's body to impose identity, and withholding the location where a ruby was
found.

There is no agreement on voluntary shattering. Some call it the final freedom
of an immortal person. Others hold that the ruby contains obligations and
memories not owned by one moment's despair. The Shardmaiden's act makes any
simple prohibition impossible.

## Kin Traditions beyond the Temples

### Elvenspring: Duty within Mortal Time

Elvenspring worship Clay and the Shardmaiden, honor land gods, and often respect
Raven. Their religious problem is time. They live longer than humans and die
far sooner than elves. Every tradition offers them a different measure of when
work is finished.

An Elvenspring household may keep a three-part memorial: clay for what the dead
made, a feather for promises carried among humans, and a seed or green branch
for duties left to druids. None is a complete account. The combination states
that mixed descent creates obligations rather than spiritual impurity.

The common anathema is abandonment disguised as patience. An Elvenspring who
delays a mortal need because an elf considers a decade brief is judged to have
chosen the convenience of the longer-lived. Frailers are accused of the reverse:
burning every inheritance for power within one life. Actual people rarely fit
either sermon cleanly.

### Orcs: the Gods Who Left and the Kin Who Remained

Orcs possess no single public church. Elves and dwarves say Clay made them as
servants. Many orcs reject the story because it turns enslavement into creation
and obedience into nature. Their strongest religious memory is therefore an
absence: elder masters sent them against human invaders, withdrew, and left
them to hold a war already lost.

Some clans honor **the Unabandoned**, ancestors and living leaders who remained
when flight was possible. Their names are recited with the work, fighters, and
dependents they kept together. A glorious death that leaves the clan helpless
earns less honor than an ugly survival that carries others out.

Female clan authorities tend genealogies and living continuity even where male
rank dominates public ritual. Males duel for standing; females decide which
victories may become marriage, succession, alliance, or exile. Outsiders who
hear only the challenge songs miss the religious judgment that follows them.

**The Stone That Stayed** is a Roka rite. A monolith or smaller upright stone is
surrounded while commands from a past battle are recited. One order that should
have been disobeyed is named with the others. The lesson is uncomfortable for a
clan built on obedience: leaders must be obeyed, and leaders who spend the clan
without purpose become traitors to those who stayed.

Urhur ceremony borrows freely from human and elven courts. Once repeated, an
invented rite can become binding because people organized gifts, rank, and
marriage around it. Their priests care less whether a custom is old than
whether the emperor has performed it publicly and others have relied upon it.

Isir trials with vipers and wasps treat poison endured as kinship with a hostile
world. The trial proves body and will but also consumes many candidates.
Survivors carry religious authority in their altered saliva and split tongues.
A leader who tests only followers and never risks poison loses standing.

Viraga rites use fox tokens, holy plants, secrecy, and the hunt. Their deepest
dogma is that the kin must gain knowledge the former masters denied them. This
can justify theft, abduction, and sacrifice. It also makes destruction of useful
knowledge within the clan an anathema greater than lying to male rulers.

Across clans, grave offenses include selling orcs into elder-kin service,
abandoning dependents during retreat, using surrender law to enslave someone
whose defeat was arranged by treachery, and giving an outsider the concealed
roads by which females and young escape war. Clans disagree over whether fear
itself is shameful or only action ruled by fear.

### Wolfkin: Heme as the Huntress

Wolfkin worship Heme as the Huntress, the unadulterated life they believe humans
abandoned with fur, pack, and forest. The Blood Mist was her breath. It allowed
passage beneath trees and punished exposure on open ground. The cult remembers
this as both favor and instruction: protection belongs to the right form of
life in the right place.

Huntress worship is not the Sisters of Heme in another costume. Wolfkin reject
the Rust church's settled measures, human hierarchy, and claim to own divine
wood. They may cooperate because both moved under the Mist or desire its return.
They do not agree on who serves whom.

Season, breeding, hunger, kill, and pack formation structure the faith. Blood
shows that life fed life. Narcotic mushrooms loosen ordinary restraint. Complex
howling and drum rhythms hold the group together while individuals enter altered
states. A gathering that sounds like chaos to humans can have exact parts,
entries, and prohibitions.

**Opening the Scent** begins a communal hunt. Hunters smell a token from the
prey, name who must be fed, and state any boundary the chase may not cross. A
kill beyond that boundary belongs to the offended pack or settlement unless a
new bargain is made.

**Giving the Throat** distributes a kill. The hunter who struck first does not
automatically eat first. Cubs, breeders, injured hunters, elders, guests, and
dominant animals receive portions according to pack law. A leader who feeds
only strength may win one winter and lose the pack by spring.

Training young with wounded prey is accepted among many packs as the honest
teaching of panic, pursuit, and clean killing. Other packs condemn it as making
prey suffer to flatter the teacher. The dispute is internal, not proof that one
side has become human.

Huntress anathemas include wasting a kill, caging a pack member as correction,
wearing wolf hide as mockery, poisoning prey so its body cannot be shared,
claiming a cub without pack acknowledgment, and betraying a forest refuge to
people who will clear it.

### Saurians: the Marsh Presences

Outsiders know that saurians worship beings sighted from barges in the Gargan
Marshes. They do not know whether these are gods, ancestors, great animals,
living places, or offices held by different bodies. Do not settle the question
merely to complete a pantheon.

Observable practice centers on water level, communal feasting ponds, dams,
crocodiles, winter fat, and the strict peace of trade platforms. Saurian
religion distinguishes water where outsiders may stand under protection from
water where anything alive may be prey. The difference is marked with posts,
smell, cut reeds, and spoken warning, not an abstract border.

At a **Bank Closing**, saurians inspect a dam before winter. Each family presses
mud into one weak place and leaves a scent mark. A collapse later reveals which
section failed and who had accepted it. The rite joins engineering, territory,
and accountability.

During a **Deep Feeding**, carcasses are allowed to ferment in a chosen pond
until the group can tear and swallow them. What outsiders experience as stench
and decay is controlled food preparation, feast, and return to marsh life. A
priest or elder decides when the food is ready. Premature eating insults the
group and risks sickness.

Crocodiles occupy an uncertain sacred position: trained burden-bearers,
dangerous kin-neighbors, and famine food. Killing one when other food remains
may require replacement, public feeding of its keepers, or surrender of metal.
No one who has only watched from a boat knows which remedy applies.

Likely anathemas include violence on a marked trade platform, breaching a dam
during cold season, hiding disease in a feast pond, wasting metal received for
communal use, and speaking as if one had understood a marsh presence seen only
at the surface. Treat these as local inference until a saurian community shows
otherwise.

### Ogres: Thunder, Mark, and Chosen Kin

Religion matters little to many ogres in the institutional sense. No common
priesthood records a creed. Their thunder gatherings, body marks, violent music,
and chosen attachments nevertheless carry sacred force.

When thunder begins, ogres howl, strike wood and metal, and search for a rhythm
loud enough to enter the storm. The practice recognizes a world greater than
speech and small law. An ogre who cannot join may be ill, grieving, or outside
the group's chosen bond.

Brands, stretched ears, and weighted noses turn endurance into visible history.
Marks can show standing, loss, adoption, boast, or punishment. Outsiders who
assume every mark means the same thing may insult an ogre by praising a sign of
disgrace.

Ogre adoption can be sincere and possessive at once. A chosen companion is fed,
defended, marked, and prevented from leaving. Some ogres understand departure
as proof the bond was false from the beginning. Others hold a **Last Noise**, in
which the departing person strikes one object and is allowed to walk while the
sound remains audible.

Common taboos are local: breaking another's status weights without challenge,
interrupting a thunder rhythm for mockery, claiming a mark never endured, or
killing an adopted person before witnesses have declared the bond broken. These
limits do not make ogre treatment of captives safe.

### Whiners: Religion behind the Hollow

Almost everything outsiders say about whiner belief is compromised by hunting,
caging, and harvesting. A captive questioned by people who value its flesh has
no reason to reveal a shrine, queen, route, or true name.

What can be observed is collective defense of hollows, the recovery or exchange
of captive kin, stone chewed and remade through the body, and violent revenge
against harvesters. Druids claim deep colonies and an egg-laying queen. The
claim may be natural history, hostile analogy, or both.

One plausible religious principle is that body and shelter are continuous. A
whiner eats stone, changes it, and adds it to the hollow; other kin cut whiner
flesh and use it to change their own bodies. The first exchange is communal and
consensual. The second is the central violation.

Possible rites include sealing a dead whiner into fresh porous stone, carrying
a chip from a lost hollow until a new chamber is joined, and biting captors'
coins before returning them with living prisoners. Possible anathemas include
revealing a brood chamber, trading another whiner's flesh, abandoning a captive
whose location is known, and placing growth-gold beneath one's own skin for
private wealth. Keep all of these as GM inference until play establishes a
particular hollow's law.

### Misgrown and Religious Ownership of the Body

Misgrown are not one religious kin. They are humans altered through demonic
power, concentrated around authorities that benefit from the alteration. The
Rust church reads marked bodies as election when useful and contamination when
disobedient. Raven Sisters may see a patient and victim. Druids may see imposed
form with living claims. Villagers may see danger before personhood.

Misgrown religious life therefore begins with jurisdiction. Who may name the
body? Who may touch it, heal it, display it, recruit it, or decide that change
has become identity? A private rite may consist only of covering a feature the
church demands be shown, or giving an altered limb a household name rather than
a doctrinal one.

The gravest offense within such hidden fellowships is delivering another
Misgrown person to a priest in exchange for one's own acceptance. The gravest
danger is that refusal may cost every marked person in the settlement what
little protection they possess.

## Mixed Worship and Religious Conflict

### A Household Can Hold Several Truths

A woman may keep Raven's given word, ask Wyrm to preserve a difficult pregnancy,
pour water to Flow, and fear the Nightwalker's moon without imagining herself
inconsistent. The contradiction becomes urgent when institutions demand
exclusive allegiance or assign incompatible remedies.

Mixed worship is easiest around practical gods and hardest around political
claims. A Rust prior may tolerate a cup poured to Flow and punish the feather
hidden above the same well. A Raven Sister may respect Huge at a bridge and
refuse the dwarven claim that maintaining the bridge proves ownership of the
valley.

Syncretism should preserve friction. When two rites combine, ask who gained
authority over birth, oath, property, burial, or calendar. A paired symbol may
show peace, conceal persecution, or let a minority continue under a name the
ruler accepts.

### Conversion Changes Obligations

Conversion is credible when it changes witnesses, marriage recognition, burial,
care, inheritance, festival labor, allies, or access to knowledge. Private
belief may change sooner or later than any of these.

A convert may need release from old vows, return of shrine objects, a new surety,
or protection from former kin. An order that welcomes the convert but refuses
these costs is collecting symbols, not people.

Forced conversion often preserves the old practice beneath new words. A Rust
figure receives the offering once given to Heme as Huntress. Raven's feather is
called a tool for cleaning the shrine. A Wyrm cord becomes a household tally.
The object survives because the work survives.

### Religious Conflict Has Material Evidence

Doctrinal conflict should leave something adventurers can handle:

- two different dates written for the same debt
- a burial refused because the dead carried an unsettled cord
- a bridge dedicated to Huge across water named to Flow
- a child claimed by a Rust register and a hidden Raven oath
- an elven ruby held as temple property against the elf's remembered wishes
- a cradle keeper accused of protecting kin through a holy lie

Each object has believers, beneficiaries, witnesses, and a price for changing
the arrangement.

## Rendering Religion in Play

### Begin with Work

Show a person repairing the shrine roof, measuring an offering, washing a body,
walking a boundary, feeding a horse, copying a page, or keeping watch at a
cradle. The theology enters through why the work belongs to this person and
what happens if it is refused.

A religious scene needs no miracle to matter. Recognition of a marriage can
move land. A funeral account can reveal a debt. A festival date can stop a
harvest. A damaged oath cord can make a witness valuable.

### Give Every Institution a Price

For any priesthood or cult, determine:

1. What useful service does it perform?
2. What offering, labor, obedience, or secrecy pays for that service?
3. Who controls the rite and the evidence it creates?
4. Which fault can be repaired, and who benefits from the remedy?
5. Which anathema places a person outside ordinary protection?
6. What local custom contradicts official teaching?

The answers should produce a situation. They should not tell the players which
religion is correct.

### Preserve Strangeness without Vagueness

The kin do not share one human theology in different clothing. Elven rubies
alter death and bodily autonomy. Dwarven construction extends the world. Orc
faith grows around abandonment by claimed creators. Wolfkin worship joins pack,
prey, forest, and altered state. Halfling and goblin religion protects a shared
birth system both halves publicly deny. Saurian and whiner beliefs remain partly
unreadable because outsiders lack safe access.

Strangeness still needs evidence. Name the cord, ash, scent post, broken tablet,
horsehair, hidden cup, unfinished board, or wrapped ruby. Let the interpretation
remain disputed.

### Questions for AI and GM Use

Before adding a rite, belief, or prohibition, answer:

1. Which manuscript fact supports it?
2. Is it public observance, local custom, contested doctrine, or hidden truth?
3. What repeated danger or need keeps it alive?
4. What material leaves ordinary use during the rite?
5. Who witnesses, records, enforces, or disputes it?
6. What can a believer repair after failure?
7. What act cannot be repaired within this tradition, and why?
8. What choice does the practice create for adventurers or the settlement?

If a rite answers none of these, it is atmosphere. Give it work or cut it.
