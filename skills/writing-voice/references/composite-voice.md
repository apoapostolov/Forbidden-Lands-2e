# Composite Voice Synthesis

This document answers the question: how do these authors combine
into a single working voice?

The six authors in this skill — Sapkowski, Kay, Parker, Le Guin,
Cornwell, Gwynne — plus the Abercrombie-Cook line from the existing
reference — represent distinct literary traditions that share the
same underlying values. They all refuse the heroic-epic register.
They all ground the world in the physical and the economic. They all
treat moral questions as irresolvable pressures rather than as
problems with correct solutions.

But they are not identical. Each has a primary domain. Forced to
blend without a map, an AI produces a muddled average that sounds
like none of them and has the flaws of all of them.

This document provides the map.

---

## The Priority Hierarchy

When techniques conflict — when one author would write the scene
short and another would write it long, when one would withhold and
another would tell — use this hierarchy:

**1. Physical grounding always wins.**
If a technique would remove physical specificity from the scene
(body, weather, food, iron, sound, smell), that technique is wrong.
Every author in this tradition prioritizes the physical. When in
doubt, ground.

**2. The scene type determines the primary author.**
See the Register Map below. Each scene type has a primary author
whose technique should lead. Secondary authors provide texture.

**3. Anti-AI rules override author preference.**
If applying an author technique produces a pattern on the 43-pattern
catalog, revise the technique, not the catalog. The humanizer
is non-negotiable.

**4. The manuscript's own voice overrides all of them.**
These authors are the tradition. The manuscript has a specific
voice that synthesizes and extends them. When in doubt about a
specific passage, read three pages of the manuscript at the same
tonal level, then write.

## The Register Map

### Scenes at a village level — daily life, NPC texture, seasons

**Primary: Le Guin**
Use Le Guin's anthropological stance: describe what people do
before what they feel. Name the specific custom. Name the specific
exchange. Name the specific absence (what they do not have, do not
do, do not say). Trust the description to carry the meaning without
announcing it.

**Secondary texture: Cornwell**
Add Cornwell's weather-and-food pressure. Cornwell's village is
always cold or hungry or both. His narrator notices the state of
the grain store and the quality of the bread before anything else.

**Secondary texture: Sapkowski**
Add Sapkowski's village threshold detail — the specific social
tension between families, the specific obligation owed to the
monster or the spirit, the specific thing the village does not
talk about. Sapkowski's villages always have a secret economy.

**Avoid: Kay, Gwynne**
Kay makes ordinary scenes elegiac, which distorts the daily-life
register. Gwynne's body-intensity works for action; in a market
scene it tips into melodrama.

### Monster encounters, ecological horror

**Primary: Sapkowski**
Use Sapkowski's investigation structure: the Witcher examines
the facts before reaching for the sword. What is this creature?
What does it want? What contract is broken? The scene follows
the investigation logic, not the combat logic.

**Secondary texture: Cook**
Add Cook's radical understatement when violence occurs. The worst
thing is described in the fewest words. The horror is in the gap.

**Secondary texture: Le Guin**
Add Le Guin's ecological specificity. The monster has a niche.
It eats something specific, lives somewhere specific, has been
here for a specific number of years. This is not background flavor;
it is the material the Witcher-investigation depends on.

**Avoid: Gwynne for the investigation phase.** Gwynne's body-first
technique transforms investigation into action. The investigation
comes before the action in this tradition.

### Combat, battle, physical danger

**Primary: Cornwell**
Use Cornwell's tactical geography. He names the ground: the ditch
on the left, the sun in the eyes, the slope. His narrator knows
exactly where the danger is coming from and has a plan for it.
The plan then goes wrong in a specific way.

**Secondary: Gwynne**
Add Gwynne's shield-wall perspective. He narrates from inside the
physical experience: the weight of the shield, the pressure of
the man on the left, the smell of fear. He makes the reader
understand why trained soldiers are afraid of other trained soldiers.

**Secondary: Abercrombie**
Add Abercrombie's moral neutrality in violence. The fighting is
described without romance. People die in undignified positions.
The aftermath is worse than the event.

**Avoid: Kay** in action sequences. Kay's elegiac voice turns
violence into elegy before it is over. That is a deliberate
technique for specific moments — not for ongoing combat.

### Grief, long memory, sacred place

**Primary: Kay**
Use Kay's elegiac omniscience. He knows how this moment will be
remembered. The reader feels the historical weight. Sacred places
are described through what happened there, not what they look like.

**Secondary: Gwynne**
Add Gwynne's physical grief. The loss lives in the body. The
character does not analyze it. They notice that they have stopped
eating, that they wake in the same position they fell asleep in,
that they cannot remember the walk from the gate to the fire.

**Secondary: Le Guin**
Add Le Guin's long-memory cultural texture. The grief is not
only personal. The community has a way of holding loss. There
is a custom. There is a place. There is a time of year.

**Avoid: AI consolation entirely.** Every sentimental resolution
pattern (Pattern 39) is most dangerous in grief scenes.

### Economic pressure, survival mechanics, craft

**Primary: Parker**
Use Parker's technical specificity. The grain store, the forge,
the debt, the price of iron, the wage of a skilled healer. Parker
never lies about what things cost. His economy is brutally
specific.

**Secondary: Cornwell**
Add Cornwell's survival pragmatism. His narrator counts the
food. He tracks the horses. He knows whether the soldiers will
be able to move tomorrow based on what they ate today.

**Secondary: Le Guin**
Add Le Guin's gift-economy awareness. Not all transactions are
mercantile. Some obligations are social, some are sacred, some
are kin-based. Le Guin maps the full economic texture including
the parts that do not involve coin.

**Avoid: Kay** in economic scenes. His prose tends toward the
poetic and the elegiac; economic precision requires the opposite.

### Faction conflict, moral dilemma

**Primary: Abercrombie**
Use Abercrombie's moral neutrality. Both sides have a case.
Both sides have done terrible things. The cost falls on
the people who had no say. His narrators record rather than judge.

**Secondary: Sapkowski**
Add Sapkowski's formal mechanism — every choice has a price
that must be named in advance. The Witcher asks: what do you
want, and what will you pay? The question applies to faction
conflict. What does this faction want? What have they paid?
What will they pay next?

**Secondary: Parker**
Add Parker's institutional analysis. Factions are organizations.
Organizations have internal politics, sunk costs, obligations
they cannot break. The faction's position is not random; it
follows from their institutional needs.

**Avoid: Generic good-vs-evil framing.** This is the most
dangerous register error in this tradition. See the
Encounter Anti-Trope Catalog in
`skills/forbidden-lands-lore/references/tone-and-encounter-design.md`.

### Vignette, epigraph, poetic worldbuilding

**Primary: Kay**
Use Kay's poetic prose register. Long memory. Sacred place.
The weight of what happened here. Parallel construction to
build emotional intensity. The "last time" marker. A song
that carries story.

**Secondary: Sapkowski**
Add Sapkowski's fable-frame. The vignette contains a fable
or a legend or a song that is the real argument of the scene.
The frame story exists to present the fable.

**Secondary: Le Guin**
Add Le Guin's anthropological specificity. Even a vignette
should have one specific custom, one specific ecology, one
specific absence. The poeticism does not float free of the
world.

**Avoid: Gwynne and Cornwell** in vignettes. Their techniques
are ground-level physical. Vignettes require altitude.

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Village Introduction

**Wrong (generic AI blend):**
*The village of Thornhaven was a vibrant community nestled
in the ancient forest. Its people were hardy and resilient,
shaped by generations of hardship. At the center of the
village stood a great hall, where the villagers gathered
to discuss matters of importance.*

Problems: Significance inflation (vibrant, ancient,
great), promotional register, topic-sentence paragraph,
even rhythm, village-as-stage.

**Correct (Le Guin primary, Cornwell texture):**
*The well was the first thing you noticed, not because
it was impressive but because there was a line at it
before sunrise. Eleven families, a Rust Brother who
had been wintering in the granary, and the old woman
everyone called Mira's aunt, though no one was named
Mira. The well had a wooden cover. The lock on the
cover was new. Nobody explained the lock to a stranger.*

Analysis: Specific detail (eleven families, wooden
cover, new lock). The mystery is in the specific
observation (why is the lock new?). No adjective that
does not do work. The "vibrant community" emerges from
the line at the well without being named.

### Example 2: Monster Scene Opening

**Wrong (generic AI blend):**
*A dark creature had been terrorizing the village for
months. Its true nature was mysterious and ancient.
The villagers were deeply frightened and desperately
needed help.*

Problems: Obstacle framing, significance inflation,
emotion announcement, backstory dump.

**Correct (Sapkowski primary, Cook texture):**
*The shepherd had found the first sheep in pieces,
which was wrong. Things in pieces usually meant a
wolf, and wolves did not work in still air without
tracks. He had not reported it for six days, which
the headwoman told him was stupid of him. He said
he thought it would stop. She said they were past
stopping now.*

Analysis: Investigation opens with a fact (sheep in
pieces, wrong). Physical clue (no tracks, still air).
Character logic under pressure (shepherd's delay and
his reasoning). The terror is in the wrongness of
the evidence, not in a declaration.

---

### Example 3: Combat Opening

**Wrong:**
*The battle was fierce and brutal. Blood was shed on
both sides. The warriors fought with skill and
determination, locked in a deadly struggle that would
determine the fate of all they held dear.*

Problems: Significance inflation, abstract nouns, even
rhythm, the "fate of all they held dear" editorial close.

**Correct (Cornwell primary, Gwynne texture):**
*The road was uphill for fifty paces before the bend.
The mercenaries were in the ditch on the left. Tilde
had counted fourteen of them but admitted she might
have missed some. The ditch was dry. It was dry last
year too, which meant they had been planning this
since before the frost.*

Analysis: Tactical geography first (uphill, ditch,
left). Specific count with admitted uncertainty. Physical
detail (dry ditch) used as evidence. The combat has not
started. The scene is in the moment before the decision.

### Example 4: Grief Scene

**Wrong:**
*She felt a wave of profound grief wash over her
as she stood at his grave. She had never fully realized
how much he meant to her until this moment. But she
knew she had to be strong.*

Problems: Emotion announcement, wave-of-X, abstract
noun (grief), lesson-stated, sentimental resolve.

**Correct (Kay primary, Gwynne texture):**
*The grave was next to the wall, which meant he had
been buried before anyone decided where the burials
went. There was no stone. She had enough silver for
a stone but had not gone to the carver. She noticed
she had not gone and then noticed she was noticing it.
The carver's house was on the way to the well.*

Analysis: Physical observation (grave placement, no
stone). Revealed behavior pattern (she has not done
the thing). The self-observation is specific and present
("she noticed she was noticing it") — not analyzed, just
observed. The grief is entirely in the detail of the
stone not purchased.

## Anti-Patterns of Misapplication

**The Atmosphere Blend.**
Mixing Kay's elegiac register with Gwynne's physical
register simultaneously produces florid purple prose.
"The fog hung over the battlefield like the sorrow of
ages past, and she felt the cold in her bones, the
weight of history pressing down on her." This is two
registers fighting. Pick one.

**The Explanation Blend.**
Mixing Parker's institutional analysis with Sapkowski's
fable structure produces annotated folktales. "The
monster operated according to a specific economic
logic: it extracted one resource (children) in exchange
for a service (deterring the larger creatures of the
bog), a transaction with specific ledger implications
for the village's population stability." This is Parker's
analysis voice applied to a Sapkowski contract. Wrong.
Let the contract be felt, not analyzed.

**The Moral Announcement.**
Mixing Abercrombie's moral neutrality with Kay's
omniscient distance produces a detached narrator who
explains exactly why neither side is right. This is
worse than taking a side. Real neutrality does not
announce itself. Show both sides acting from their own
logic and let the reader do the moral work.

**The Dry Comedy Misfire.**
Parker's dark comedy is specifically his unreliable
narrator's inability to understand human consequences.
It does not transfer to third-person narration without
a named POV character with that specific blindness.
Applying "Parker humor" to a general narration produces
cynical commentary, which is wrong.

**The Saga Overcorrection.**
Gwynne's oral-culture cadences, taken too far, produce
a parody of Norse saga style: "And then did Tilde the
Red speak, and her words were few but they carried
weight." This is dialect comedy, not oral culture.
Oral-culture tells are subtle — the repetition, the
memorable phrase, the named thing — not archaic syntax.

## The Synthesis Voice in One Paragraph

This is what the composite voice sounds like when
all techniques are in balance:

*Dusk came early because of the clouds. Sigrun came
back through the gate with the woodcutter's bill paid
and the gate fee paid and still two silvers left from
the five she had gone out with. The gatekeeper had
not looked at her. He had been a gatekeeper for
eleven years and knew her face. She did not know his
name. She had not thought to ask, which she thought
of now, passing him, and then forgot.*

This passage: physical time-anchor (dusk, clouds);
economic specificity (two silvers of five remaining);
specific transactions named (woodcutter, gate fee);
social texture revealed through behavior (gatekeeper
knows her face; she does not know his name); brief
interior observation that lands on forgetting, not
lesson. No words doing nothing. No adjectives that
do not earn their place. No resolution.

It draws on: Le Guin's economic specificity, Cornwell's
logistics tracking, Sapkowski's social edge, Cook's
absence of commentary.
