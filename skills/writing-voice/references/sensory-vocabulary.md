# Sensory Vocabulary

## Overview

This reference specifies how sensory detail functions in the
six-author tradition this skill synthesizes, and provides the
sensory vocabulary that is appropriate for the Forbidden Lands
setting. Sensory detail is not decoration; it is information.
Each specific sensory detail either advances the scene,
characterizes the observer, or tells the reader something about
the location that matters for what follows.

The single most common AI sensory failure: generic atmosphere.
"The air was cold and dark." This tells the reader nothing
that the genre doesn't already imply. The specific detail
— "the cold was in her hands by mid-morning and in her feet
by noon, and the bread had not risen" — tells the reader
the temperature, the character's physiology, the season,
the available fuel, and the state of the supplies.

---

## The Hierarchy of Senses

The manuscript's target register prioritizes senses in a specific order:

1. **Sound** — the first diagnostic sense
2. **Smell** — the most specific environmental marker
3. **Touch/proprioception** — the body's relation to cold, weight, texture
4. **Sight** — third, not first; sight used for tactical reading
5. **Taste** — rare; reserved for food and poison

This order is opposite to most genre fantasy prose, which leads
with sight. The reason for the inversion: the characters in this
world are survivors who have learned to use sound and smell before
sight because sight tells you what you're looking at; sound and
smell tell you what is around the corner.

---

## Sound: The Diagnostic Sense

Sound is used before sight in every investigation scene, every
monster approach, every tracking sequence. The absence of sound
is a positive fact: birds that stopped means something is in the
trees. Dogs that settled their ears means humans, not animals.
The silence before the storm is tactically legible.

### Sound Categories by Environment

**Forest:**

- The bird that stopped is more important than the bird that is
  calling
- Specific birds have specific alarm calls vs. ordinary calls
- Footsteps on each substrate: frozen leaf-fall, wet leaf-fall,
  dry soil, roots, standing water
- Wind through specific tree species: birch rattles; oak moves
  as a single mass; pine doesn't rattle until the upper branches
- The specific silence that precedes snow

**Village:**

- The hours of activity: mill starts when, market assembles when,
  children in the streets at which times
- What silence at the wrong hour means
- Dogs: three dogs in a village will give different sounds to
  different things (known person, stranger, animal, something wrong)
- The mill-wheel as timing mechanism: if it stops in the middle
  of the day, something is wrong

**Road:**

- Carts on different road surfaces
- Hooves at different gaits on different surfaces
- The specific silence that means no carts, no traffic, wrong
  for the time of year
- Water sources: streams audible before visible; the specific
  sound of a spring vs. a stream

**Fortress/Ruins:**

- What is inside a ruin: birds nest in the rafters until
  something displaces them; rats are present until a cat
  or a large predator displaces them
- The echo quality of specific stone work (thick walls,
  thin walls, collapsed sections)
- Stairs: sound of a person's weight on each type of step

## Smell: The Environmental Marker

Smell is the most specific and least used sense in most fantasy
prose. It is the sense that most immediately differentiates
environments, seasons, and conditions.

### Smell Categories by Environment

**Forest by season:**

- Spring: cold water, thaw, alder sap, wet bark
- Summer: specific flowers by region (lime flower, elderflower,
  meadowsweet near water), warm pine resin, dry leaves
- Autumn: fungus, decomposing leaf material, wet earth, wood smoke
  from distant settlements
- Winter: cold absence of smell; the specific smell of snow;
  wood smoke carries further in cold air

**Village:**

- The granary (dry grain, dust, mouse, the specific smell of
  the grain type: rye smells different from wheat; barley different
  from both)
- The forge: coal smoke vs. wood smoke; hot iron; cooling water;
  specific smell of quenched steel
- The tannery: unavoidable, sharp, the reason tanneries are always
  at the edge of the village and downwind
- The latrine: also at the edge; the smell tells you the distance
- The brewery: malt, fermentation, yeast; varies with the grain
- Cooking: rendered fat, the grain type of the bread, the specific
  vegetable boiling

**Creatures by type:**

- Large predators (wolves, bears): musk, meat-smell, urine territory
  markers
- Undead: the specific smell of a body at various stages of
  decomposition; the difference between freshly dead and long-dead
- Demons and magical creatures: the absence of smell where there
  should be smell; the wrong smell for the environment; a smell
  that does not belong
- The Demon-infected: sulphur is not actually a demon smell;
  that is a modern cultural overlay. The specific smell is
  contextually wrong — winter cold where the air should be warm,
  sweetness where the air should be neutral

## Touch and Proprioception: The Survival Sense

Following Gwynne and Cornwell's lead, this manuscript tracks
the body's state as a primary narrative indicator. Cold is not
an atmosphere; it is a fact with specific physical consequences.

### Temperature Tracking

Cold advances through the body predictably:

- First: exposed extremities (fingers, nose, ears)
- Next: feet (feet are below the heart; circulation reaches them last)
- Next: hands (despite gloves, the blood withdraws from the
  extremities to protect the core)
- Finally: core warmth begins to fail (which is dangerous and
  should be marked as such in narrative)

Warmth after cold has a specific physiology: the fire warms
the face first, the front of the body, while the back remains
cold. The hands hurt when they begin to warm. This is specific
and familiar to any person who has been genuinely cold.

**Prohibited:** "She felt the cold." This tells the reader nothing.
**Correct:** "Her fingers had stopped working properly by the time
she found the door."

### Weight and Fatigue

Armour: a gambeson alone is heavy; mail over a gambeson adds
significant weight distributed across the shoulders; a full
kit in wet weather weighs more than in dry because the gambeson
absorbs water.

Loads: carried weight is felt in specific body parts depending
on how it is carried (shoulders, hips, neck, lower back). A
character carrying a pack for the fourth hour of a march feels
it differently than the first.

The specific fatigue of specific tasks: digging is felt in the
lower back and shoulders. Fighting is felt immediately and then
in the hands and forearms hours later. Rowing is the lower back
and the inside of the thighs.

### Texture as Social Register

Le Guin's observation: the texture of materials is a social
register signal. The quality of a cloth tells you about the
wearer's wealth and trade access. The roughness of a wood surface
tells you whether this house is inhabited by someone who cares
for objects or someone who has no time to.

Touch details that carry worldbuilding information:

- The smoothness of a worn tool handle (used, not new)
- The roughness of a new rope vs. the softness of a worked rope
- The temperature of stone in different weather conditions
- The specific grit of a road surface under foot

## Sight: The Tactical Sense

Used third, for a reason: when the characters in this manuscript
use sight, it is for tactical reading, not atmospheric appreciation.
Cornwell's model: arrive at a location and identify the defensible
ground, the water source, the approaches, the exit routes.

### Sight Used Tactically

**Terrain assessment:**

- Height of ground relative to approach
- Cover that matters (cover from arrows is different from cover
  from observation; distinguish)
- Water: is it accessible? defended? drinkable?
- Light: where is it coming from, how will it change in the next
  two hours, who has sun in their eyes

**Social reading:**

- Who is working and who is watching the workers
- The state of the buildings (maintained or not, and what that
  means about the economic state of the household)
- Tools present or absent (present working tools = active household;
  absent = something is wrong)
- Faces: not described for beauty; described for what they communicate
  about situation (fear, watchfulness, anger-suppressed)

**Creature and monster signs:**

- Track analysis: depth = weight; stride length = pace; specific
  shape = specific creature; age of track = time since passage
- Kill site: what was taken, what was left, the pattern of the kill
- Environmental disturbance: broken branches at specific heights,
  displaced rocks, the direction of disturbed vegetation

### Sight Used Atmospherically (Limited)

Sight is used atmospherically only for the weather-as-logistics
technique (Cornwell) and for the landscape-as-witness technique
(Gwynne/Kay). These have specific applications:

- Weather sight: describing what the sky looks like as a logistics
  predictor (this sky means rain in two hours; this frost means
  the ford will be solid; this kind of cloud means the wind
  changes tonight)
- Landscape sight: describing a location through what happened
  there (the field where the names were given; the ford that
  was named for the drowning)

---

## Taste: Reserved Uses

Taste appears in specific functional contexts only:

- Food: the quality of available food as a logistics signal
  (fresh bread vs. dry bread vs. moldy bread; fresh water vs.
  brackish vs. polluted)
- Poison or illness: a specific wrong taste that functions as
  a diagnostic signal
- Blood: the specific taste of one's own blood in combat, which
  is a real physical experience and functions as a danger marker

Taste is not atmospheric. "The air tasted of coming rain" is
an AI tell. The rain is coming: use the specific smell, the
specific cloud formation, the specific change in the animals'
behavior.

## The Specific vs. Generic Decision

Every sensory detail must pass this test: is it specific enough
to be true of this particular place at this particular time,
or is it generic enough to be true of all similar places?

Generic: "The forest was dark and quiet."
Specific: "The birches were still. No birds. The snow was old
enough that it had crusted, and each step cracked it."

The decision about specificity level should be made based on
the narrative function of the detail:

- **High specificity:** details that are load-bearing (they will
  matter later, or they characterize the observer's expertise)
- **Medium specificity:** details that establish the ecology
  (what type of forest, what season, what weather)
- **Low specificity (avoid):** details that are true of the genre
  category but not of this specific location

## Environment Reference Vocabularies

### Forest (Alder Region — wet, near water)

**Sound:** alder catkins rattle in wind; water in root systems after
rain; the specific birds: warblers in spring, silence in winter

**Smell:** cold water, root-smell (the specific smell of soil and
roots when the soil is disturbed), green alder (astringent, specific)

**Sight:** alder bark: dark and rough; alder roots: visible at water's
edge, orange in cross-section; the specific darkness of an alder
grove vs. open forest

**Touch:** wet bark under hand, root systems underfoot, the cold
of standing water at the roots

---

### Forest (Pine Region — upland, dry)

**Sound:** pine moves as a mass in wind; no rattle; silence inside
a dense pine grove that is specific and pressing

**Smell:** pine resin dominant; dry soil; in autumn, the specific
smell of pine resin warming in the sun; the absence of water-smell

**Sight:** long sight lines between trunks; pine floor: needles,
level, soft, brown; light quality specific: filtered and amber

**Touch:** rough bark; the softness of pine needle floor

---

### Village (Working, Grain Economy)

**Sound:** mill timing; specific agricultural sounds by season
(threshing in autumn, birdsong in fields in summer, silence
in winter except the mill and the livestock)

**Smell:** grain (dry grain in the granary: dusty, slightly sweet;
wet grain in the field before harvest: green, green-straw, green-earth);
livestock (specific: cattle smell different from pigs, pigs from sheep)

**Sight:** the granary is the largest building; the privy is the
furthest building; the well is at the center or near the largest
household

**Touch:** the granary door (heavy, hinged, specific to touch);
the quality of the well rope (worn = old well; new = recent work)

---

### Marsh/Fen

**Sound:** specific marsh birds (bittern boom in spring; reed bunting);
the specific sound of marsh water moving under surface vegetation;
the sucking sound of foot withdrawn from soft ground

**Smell:** methane at the wrong time (disturbed deep mud); the sweet
smell of marsh marigold in spring; the rotting smell of standing
water warming in summer

**Sight:** mirror surface tells you the depth; disturbed surface
tells you something moved recently; the absence of tracks on the
approach tells you something came a different way

**Touch:** the specific texture of bog grass (cutting edges);
the resistance of soft mud vs. solid ground; the cold of marsh
water entering a boot

---

### Fortress / Ruins

**Sound:** wind through specific apertures (arrow slits produce
a specific frequency); the absence of rodents when something
large is present; specific stone types produce specific echo
frequencies

**Smell:** old fire in the great hall (permanent carbon smell);
damp stone (specific to lime vs. granite vs. sandstone);
specific absence: a fortress with no cooking smell has no
current inhabitants

**Sight:** the state of the wall-walk tells you when the last
maintenance was; the state of the gatehouse wood tells you
the decade; the state of the well tells you whether water
was available throughout occupation

**Touch:** stone temperature: stone holds cold longer than any
other material; in a ruin, even in summer, the deep stones
are cold; in winter, the stone is colder than the outside air

---

## Sensory Sequencing

The approach to any significant location should use this order:

1. **Sound first** — what can be heard, including what is absent
2. **Smell second** — specific environmental markers
3. **Sight third** — tactical reading, then ecological reading
4. **Touch/proprioception** — what is underfoot, temperature, texture

This sequence mirrors the approach of a competent person
in a potentially dangerous environment: sound and smell travel
further than sight and give more advance warning.

The sequence can be compressed to one or two senses for minor
locations. The full sequence is for significant locations
(the village the players arrive at, the ruin that is the
encounter site, the road where the ambush happens).

## Prohibited Sensory Patterns

**The Atmospheric Sweep:**
"The mist rolled across the moors as night fell, and the darkness
swallowed the ancient stones..." Nothing specific, nothing that
matters, nothing that will be used.

**The Weather Opener:**
Every scene beginning with a weather description is an AI tell
(Pattern 41). Vary scene openers.

**The Metaphorical Sensation:**
"She felt a chill that was not from the cold." This is a tell
for supernatural foreshadowing delivered too early and too
obviously. Name the specific physical thing, not the metaphorical
interpretation.

**The Adjective Pile:**
"The cold, dark, heavy air..." More than one sensory adjective
per noun is an AI tell (Pattern 8). Pick one specific. "The cold
was specific: dry cold, not damp."

**The Wrong Sense First:**
Opening a monster scene with visual description before sound
and smell is wrong for both verisimilitude and tension.
The characters hear and smell first; they see second.
