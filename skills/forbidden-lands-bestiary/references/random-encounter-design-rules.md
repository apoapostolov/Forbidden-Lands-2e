# Random Encounter Design Rules

## Purpose

This file is the strict reference for creating new monster random encounters.

Its job is to stop encounters from collapsing into generic fantasy prompts and to force them back into the manuscript's actual strengths:

- medieval material reality
- social pressure
- physical discomfort
- sensory disgust
- moral revulsion
- witnessed ugliness
- decision discomfort
- moral cost
- specific human arrangements under strain
- meaningful choices that divide the table instead of uniting it around the obvious answer

Use this file whenever you write, audit, or expand a `#### Random Encounter:` section.

## The Core Law

A random encounter is not a fight seed.
It is a **human situation already in progress** whose pressure becomes visible the moment the adventurers arrive.

The monster matters.
But the situation must still work as a meaningful problem if the monster is temporarily removed from the frame.

If the encounter can be reduced to one of the following, it fails:

- kill the beast
- save the victim
- clear the road
- take the job
- collect the reward

A valid encounter must force the players to choose among costs.

## The Six Mandatory Components

Every serious random encounter should contain all six.

### 1. An existing local arrangement

The situation must begin before the adventurers arrive.
Something has already been normalized, hidden, tolerated, traded, ritualized, or deferred.

Examples:

- a village has been paying children, livestock, grain, or silence
- a ferry, shrine, kiln, mill, ford, or quarry still functions by feeding something
- a headman, widow, guild, priest, clan, or family has accepted an ugly bargain
- a road custom, toll custom, burial custom, or marriage custom is already sustaining the problem

### 2. A concrete physical bind

The problem must have bodily and material form.

Examples:

- a tied child
- a failing bridge
- a sealed cistern
- a trapped worker
- a body that cannot be recovered without consequence
- a winter store that cannot be opened safely
- a ford, gate, rope, cart, or coffin whose immediate handling changes the outcome

### 3. A real dilemma

The encounter must not permit an obvious clean solution.
One action must protect one value while endangering another.

Good trade-offs:

- save the person, lose the village's water
- expose the truth, destroy the settlement's political balance
- kill the monster, trigger the hunger, debt, oath, curse, or retaliation it had been containing
- preserve the custom, condemn the victim
- protect the goods, lose the witness

### 4. Social fracture

At least two human parties in the scene must want different outcomes for reasons that make sense inside the world.

Good fracture lines:

- family vs village
- priest vs elder
- widow vs headman
- guild vs laborer
- oath vs mercy
- survival vs justice
- public truth vs usable silence

### 5. Pressure of time, weather, or terrain

The party must feel that waiting is also a decision.

Use one or more:

- nightfall
- frost
- market day
- thaw
- floodwater
- rot
- next toll crossing
- the return of witnesses
- the arrival of soldiers, creditors, clergy, kin, or rival claimants

### 6. Consequence after the scene

The encounter must imply a changed local world after the adventurers act.

Questions to test:

- who remembers this choice tomorrow?
- who loses face, food, trade, kin, or authority?
- what practice breaks if the party interferes?
- what worsens if they do not?

## Medieval Authenticity Rules

The encounter must feel like it grew out of pre-industrial life, not out of adventure design.

### Start from systems of living, not from plot hooks

Ground the scene in one of these:

- water access
- food storage
- grazing rights
- childbearing and midwifery
- inheritance and dowry
- tithes and shrine obligations
- ferry, bridge, ford, or toll usage
- winter survival
- burial and grave disturbance
- labor debts
- charcoal, quarry, kiln, mine, or mill work
- herd management
- plague response
- road duty and watch duty
- market day pressure

If the encounter begins from none of these and instead begins from "there is a monster nearby," it is probably false.

### Use goods, labor, kin, and custom as stakes

In this world, people do not think first in abstract alignment terms.
They think in:

- who gets fed
- who is owed
- who marries whom
- who inherits the field, mill, or road right
- who must answer for the dead
- what the village can survive admitting
- what cannot be replaced before winter

### Favor practical cruelty over theatrical evil

The strongest encounters usually involve people doing something ugly because they believe the alternative is worse.

That is more authentic than cartoon malice.

Examples:

- the village is complicit because the arrangement works
- the elder is lying because the truth destroys the food supply
- the mother is silent because speech kills one child and silence kills another
- the ferryman keeps the curse because the crossing feeds three households

## The Discomfort Requirement

Not every encounter needs discomfort.

Random encounters in this manuscript are allowed to be:

- grim
- light-hearted
- humorous
- faux-dangerous
- socially strange
- briefly relieving before darker material returns

If an encounter is not meant to be grim, it may instead revolve around:

- mistaken danger
- embarrassment
- local eccentricity
- new people worth meeting
- a false alarm that still reveals something about the world

If an encounter **is** meant to be grim, it should usually center on
**one dominant discomfort family**.
Do not overdo it.
Do not stack half a dozen ugliness modes into one scene unless there is
a very rare, very strong reason.

Not necessarily graphically.
Not necessarily violently.
But materially, morally, socially, sensorially, reputationally, or
decisively uncomfortable.

Discomfort is a wide repertoire, not a single grim tone.
It may disgust, sicken, shame, anger, weary, corner, implicate, or force
the players to choose between two bad paths.

### Governing principle

**Show the weight, not the spectacle.**

Use ugly truth to create pressure, not empty edginess.
The point is not to spray gore over the scene.
The point is to make the players feel that something real and hard is
being asked of them.

### Mandatory discomfort families

Use any that fit the monster and local situation.
Do not limit yourself to the narrow bodily list.

Typical rule: choose **one** discomfort family as the dominant pressure
for a grim encounter.
You may add a very light secondary note, but the encounter should still
read as one focused discomfort, not a buffet of misery.

1. **Sensory disgust.** Ooze, clotted slime, flies in the milk pail,
   leeches in the trough, insect-swarms under bandages, butcher-sweet rot,
   a stench that clings to wool and beard.
2. **Dental ugliness.** Drawn teeth in a bowl, black gums, jawbone charms,
   ritual tooth-removal, children with filed or broken teeth, something
   chewing without enough mouth left to do it right.
3. **Birth and feeding horror.** A mother beast birthing in the straw,
   trampling the weak, eating its own young, or forcing others to feed on
   afterbirth, milk, or carrion.
4. **Parasites and infestation.** Fleas, lice, mites, worms, grubs, nest
   chambers under skin, vermin that make the body feel occupied.
5. **Disease and bodily failure.** Weeping sores, fever-sweat, infected
   cuts, blood in the stool, coughing fits, wasting, gangrene, or the smell
   of old sickness in bedding and rushes.
6. **Animal panic and ugly husbandry.** Livestock gnawing the dead, mares
   tearing at foals, penned beasts packed too tightly, working animals used
   until hide and bone show through.
7. **Amoral practicality.** People doing something hateful because it works:
   feeding one child to save six, paying tribute in flesh, hiding the sick,
   selling kin, or abandoning the wounded because carrying them means all die.
8. **Amoral behavior that breeds hatred.** An antagonist who lies without
   shame, hurts dependents for convenience, laughs while others suffer, or
   treats human beings as inventory, breeding stock, bait, or proof of rank.
9. **Atrocity beyond expected violence.** Punitive mutilation, deliberate
   cruelty, organized humiliation, staged executions, burning for warning,
   or violence done to make witnesses obey rather than to win a fight.
10. **Cruelty for pleasure.** Rare but valid when the antagonist truly earns
   it. If used, it must feel monstrous, not flamboyant.
11. **Sexualized menace or arousal-linked cruelty.** Use extremely rarely.
   Never for flavor, never graphically, never casually, and preferably by
   aftermath, implication, or survivor behavior rather than direct display.
12. **Public shame.** Exposure before kin, forced confession, false witness,
   ruined marriage prospects, defiled burial status, or a debt made public
   at the worst possible moment.
13. **Reputation poisoning.** The choice that saves lives now but marks a
   family, profession, cult, or bloodline for years.
14. **Burial failure.** Corpses left unburned, disturbed graves, wrong bones
   in the wrong cairn, a body too dangerous to bury, a dead child no one can
   claim, or a funeral rite interrupted halfway.
15. **Food and water contamination.** Vermin in grain, blood in the well,
   spoiled stores, tainted ale, worms in salted meat, or the knowledge that
   the whole village has been eating something foul to survive.
16. **Exhaustion and no-sleep misery.** Men nodding off on watch, mothers
   nursing through fever, guards too tired to lie well, everyone one bad hour
   from panic or stupidity.
17. **Starvation arithmetic.** Rationing, hoarding, choosing who eats,
   watching a community justify ugly choices by winter math.
18. **Dependency betrayal.** Midwife against mother, father against child,
   shepherd against flock, elder against widow, healer against the sick, or
   guardian against the body they were meant to keep safe.
19. **Ritualized normality around horror.** The awful thing is not hidden;
   it is scheduled, witnessed, tithed, blessed, or written into custom.
20. **Slavery and ownership logic.** Human beings treated as cargo, payment,
   dowry correction, labor stock, or lawful spoil.
21. **Punishment and mutilation.** Missing ears, cropped fingers, tongue-cut
   survivors, branded faces, or the visible remains of public correction.
22. **Silence after the fact.** The room that goes quiet, the washed stones,
   the child who will not speak, the villagers who explain too little because
   everyone already knows.
23. **Two hated options.** The players dislike both roads and argue because
   both reveal something about who they are willing to sacrifice, forgive,
   conceal, or become.
24. **Character-limit pressure.** The choice strikes at mercy, disgust,
   taboo, revenge, pity, class contempt, sexual threat, or fear of helpless
   suffering, exposing the limits of both characters and players.

### Preferred discomfort sources

- cold
- wetness
- rot
- bad air
- spoiled food
- vermin
- crowding
- sleeplessness
- shame
- debt
- childbirth danger
- burial failure
- livestock panic
- contaminated water
- disease exposure
- reputation cost
- ritual obligation nobody fully believes in anymore

Add to that broader repertoire whenever the scene needs it:

- disgust
- witnessed cruelty
- reproductive ugliness
- starvation logic
- burial horror
- slavery or ownership pressure
- punishment spectacle
- silence and aftermath
- public shame
- impossible choices

The discomfort should feel lived in, not decorative.

A wet bridal hem, a child with soot on the teeth, a rope worn shiny by
repeated use, a bowl of broth outside a plague door, a sack rattling with
milk teeth, a mare nosing the foal she already half-crushed, a father who
counts winter grain before naming which child stays — these are stronger
than generic gore.

### Restrictions on extreme discomfort

The harshest forms of discomfort are usable, but under discipline.

- One strong discomfort is usually enough.
- If the scene starts to feel crowded with ugliness, cut back to the one
   thing the encounter is really about.
- Do not deploy atrocity or sexualized menace as default ambience.
- Do not write them for thrill, shock value, or swagger.
- Prefer aftermath, implication, witness behavior, silence, or material
  traces over graphic dwelling.
- The ugliness must change the decision, not merely decorate it.
- If the same encounter works without the extreme element, cut the extreme
  element.

## Anti-Trope Rules

The following are forbidden unless the encounter subverts them hard enough to stop being themselves.

- villagers ask the heroes to slay the monster
- obvious lair, obvious treasure, obvious fight
- innocent victim plus evil beast, with no second layer
- obvious rescue with no cost
- purely heroic intervention rewarded by gratitude alone
- simple information-delivery NPC whose only job is to point at danger
- any scene where the best action is also the only morally tolerable action

## The Monster's Job In The Encounter

The monster should usually be doing one of five things.

### 1. Symptom

The monster reveals a deeper human arrangement.
Killing it does not solve the underlying practice.

### 2. Enforcer

The monster is part of an ongoing bargain or discipline system.
It may be fed, tolerated, signaled, or redirected.

### 3. Territory fact

The monster has changed how people use a place.
Roads, wells, shrines, quarries, burial grounds, spring caves, and market routes may all reorganize around it.

### 4. Witness pressure

The monster creates a situation in which hidden truths must surface.

### 5. Moral accelerant

The monster forces a decision that people were already postponing.

## The Argument Test

A proper encounter should make players argue about what to do.

Not because the scene is confusing.
Because two or more options are genuinely defensible.

To pass the argument test, the encounter should offer at least two of these at once:

- a human life immediately at stake
- a settlement-scale consequence
- a truth that destroys a working arrangement
- a route, bridge, ford, shrine, field, or store whose loss matters beyond the scene
- a promise, oath, or kin-obligation that one player may respect and another may reject
- a morally compromised victim or morally compromised community

## Writing Procedure

Use this order.

### Step 1: Name the local system under pressure

Do not begin with the monster.
Begin with the local thing that mattered before the monster scene opened.

Examples:

- a spring tithe
- a quarry labor arrangement
- a hidden road toll
- a plague-warning custom
- a bridge economy
- a burial debt

### Step 2: Decide what the community is hiding, tolerating, or misnaming

The most useful question is:

> What has everyone here been calling necessary that would look monstrous from outside?

### Step 3: Give the scene one unforgettable physical image

This is the epigraph seed.
It must be visible before explanation begins.

### Step 4: Insert the adventurers too late

They should arrive after somebody has already chosen, bargained, lied, tied the knot, sealed the lid, set the child on the post, opened the cairn, or accepted the tribute.

### Step 5: Remove the clean option

If there is a solution that saves everyone and preserves everything, cut it.

### Step 6: Add aftermath cost

Ask what the settlement looks like tomorrow if the party chooses each route.

## The Epigraph Rule

The epigraph is not a teaser sentence.
It is the first physical fact of the scene.

It should usually show:

- an object out of place
- a body doing something wrong for the context
- a sign of repetition or use
- a detail that implies the arrangement without naming it

The epigraph must not explain the dilemma.

## Human Authenticity Checklist

Before approving an encounter, verify all of the following.

- Does the scene arise from a believable village, road, shrine, labor, kin, or trade practice?
- Is the problem already in motion before the party arrives?
- Does at least one party in the scene have a practical reason for doing the ugly thing?
- Would the situation still matter if the monster were temporarily removed?
- Is there a genuine trade-off rather than a puzzle with one answer?
- Does the scene carry material, bodily, moral, reputational, or decision discomfort, not just danger?
- Will different players plausibly advocate different choices?
- Does the outcome leave a scar on the place?

## Fast Failure Signs

Rewrite the encounter if any of these are true.

- the monster is the only interesting thing in the scene
- nobody in the scene has a reason to defend the status quo
- the party can solve everything by fighting well
- the epigraph sounds like a GM summary instead of a visual moment
- the village acts like a quest board instead of a pressured community
- the stakes are abstract instead of bodily, economic, social, or religious

## Recommendation

When in doubt, make the scene smaller, dirtier, and more specific.

A ford, a grain loft, a birthing hut, a toll span, a spring cave, a plague bell, a wedding bed, a quarry face, a tied animal, a sealed well.

That is where Forbidden Lands encounters live.
Not in generic hero work.
