# Lore Roll Rules

## Purpose

This file is the strict reference for writing `#### Lore Roll` tables in monster entries.

A Lore Roll is not trivia.
It is the controlled release of survival-relevant knowledge in an in-world voice.

The table must do three things at once:

- preserve atmosphere
- reward knowledge gathering
- help the players survive or understand the encounter without turning into overt mechanical spoilers

## Core Structure

A Lore Roll table has three escalating rows.

| Row | Job | Measured hard minimum |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| 1 success | public knowledge, category, habit, or reputation | 12 words |
| 2 successes | partial but useful hint | 22 words |
| 3 successes | strong directional hint | 24 words |

These floors come from measured Book of Beasts material and exist because rows 2 and 3 need room to carry implication without falling into either vagueness or direct spoiler language.

## The Three Rows

### Row 1: public knowledge

Row 1 is what a traveler, hunter, ferryman, shrine-keeper, road-wife, hedge-priest, or soldier might reasonably know.

It should answer one of these:

- what sort of thing this is
- where it is found
- what it is known for
- what kind of danger it commonly poses

It must not give away the solution.

### Row 2: partial hint

Row 2 is where the real design work begins.

This row should provide a useful but incomplete hint framed through:

- old observation
- smith lore
- hunter practice
- shepherd custom
- marsh-wife saying
- road superstition with a practical core
- priestly warning
- graveyard custom

The row must be interpretable.
But the players should still need to think.

### Row 3: strong directional hint

Row 3 is the strongest non-spoiler guidance the table provides.

This row may tell the players:

- what sort of thing might work
- where the weakness lies in the body, site, or ritual arrangement
- what old practitioners did when they meant to kill or break such a creature

But it must still sound like inherited world knowledge, not a rules annotation.

## Hard Rule For Hidden Necessary Weaknesses

If a monster has a weakness that is both of the following:

- not reasonably knowable on first sight
- the only normal way to defeat the creature without catastrophic risk

then the Lore Roll table must carry that weakness in graduated form.

This is mandatory.

### Required row behavior

#### Row 2

Row 2 must contain a **non-spoiler hint** pointing toward the weakness.

This hint should:

- be indirect
- be flavorful
- require deduction
- name a pattern, sign, or old practice rather than the answer itself

Good forms:

- what old hunters watched for
- what shepherds, smiths, masons, or grave-keepers say
- what behavior changes before the creature strikes
- what body part, object, or site detail matters without spelling out the mechanic

#### Row 3

Row 3 must contain a **strong flavorful hint** about what may defeat the creature.

This hint should:

- point more directly at the answer
- still avoid direct system phrasing
- remain rooted in in-world speech, memory, or practice

The row may strongly imply:

- a vulnerable body seam
- a bound object
- a material that works
- a ritual condition that breaks the monster
- a place that must be struck, burned, broken, or denied

## Spoiler Control Rules

### Forbidden phrasing

Do not use:

- stat names
- roll names
- page references
- direct procedural language like "target the weak point"
- overt rule-summary language like "this is the only way to kill it"

### Preferred phrasing

Use:

- old sayings
- tool wisdom
- trade wisdom
- burial custom
- local taboo
- remembered hunt practice
- inherited fear or ritual habit

The text should sound like something people in the world say because they needed it, not because a designer inserted a clue.

## Deduction Rule For Row 2

The two-success row should not be a puzzle, but it should require the players to connect one fact to another.

This means the row should usually do one of the following:

- point at a body feature without naming the attack procedure
- point at a place in the lair without naming the kill switch
- point at an old custom that only makes sense if the players infer the reason
- point at what practitioners avoided, fed, broke, covered, or struck first

If the row can be translated immediately into a full answer with no thought, it is too open.

If the row can mean almost anything, it is too vague.

## Flavor Rule

Rows 2 and 3 must remain embedded in the manuscript world.

Preferred speakers and traditions:

- old women
- smiths
- road wardens
- ferrymen
- hill shepherds
- charcoal burners
- shrine keepers
- gravediggers
- plague wives
- quarrymen
- clan elders
- priestly warnings
- dwarven craft sayings

A good row feels carried by mouths across time.

## Row Writing Templates

These are patterns, not fill-in forms.

### Row 1 templates

- `[Monster] are known for [public danger] in [terrain or social setting].`
- `Travelers say [monster] haunt [place] and do [recognizable harm].`

### Row 2 templates

- `Old [profession] say [pattern or bodily clue], and act accordingly.`
- `The [local group] never [action] when [sign] appears, because they learned what follows.`
- `[Profession/custom group] say [seemingly odd practice], which points at the hidden weakness without naming it.`

### Row 3 templates

- `The old tales say [body/site/object relationship] and imply what breaks it.`
- `Those who meant to end such a thing used [material/place/body feature], though not all lived long enough to explain why.`
- `Where the creature keeps [bound object / seam / buried truth], the old hunters struck first.`

## Example Logic

### Weak-point body monster

- **Row 2:** points at a gap, seam, throat-slit, eye-line, or change in movement
- **Row 3:** points more clearly at the vulnerable place and what sort of strike matters

### Bound-object monster

- **Row 2:** hints that the creature guards, returns to, or is tethered to something
- **Row 3:** strongly implies shared life, tether, or dependency

### Material-aversion monster

- **Row 2:** says ordinary weapons fail or anger it, and notes what old practitioners carried instead
- **Row 3:** more strongly names the material or force in world terms

## Audit Questions

Before approving a Lore Roll table, ask:

- Does row 1 provide public knowledge rather than padding?
- Does row 2 point toward survival-relevant reasoning?
- Does row 3 provide stronger direction without becoming naked rules text?
- If the monster has a hidden necessary weakness, does row 2 hint and row 3 strongly hint?
- Would a player be able to act on the hint after thinking, rather than only after being told the answer?
- Do the rows sound like the world speaking, not the designer speaking?

## Recommendation

Treat the Lore Roll table as part of the monster's counterplay architecture.

If the monster can wipe the party unless its hidden truth is learned, the table must help the players learn that truth in stages.

Not cleanly.
Not clinically.
But reliably enough that smart players, listening to the world, can live.
