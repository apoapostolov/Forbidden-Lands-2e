# Forbidden Lands Bestiary

Build monsters as situations with bodies, behaviors, signs, counterplay, and
consequences. A monster is not only a combat statblock. It changes travel,
settlement behavior, local knowledge, resource value, and player decisions.

## Establish Authority

1. Read the relevant bestiary text in `01-corebook/` and any region source.
2. Search for the creature, its abilities, materials, and related legends across
   the manuscript.
3. Load `forbidden-lands-lore` for setting facts.
4. Load `forbidden-lands-writing-voice` for finished prose.
5. Load `forbidden-lands-design` and `forbidden-lands-synergy-analysis` when
   changing mechanics rather than only presentation.

Do not import assumptions from first edition or generic fantasy when this
manuscript establishes a different rule or identity.

## Define the Monster Contract

Before drafting, answer:

- **First sign:** What evidence reaches the adventurers before the monster?
- **Threat identity:** What pressure does it create that another monster does not?
- **Body logic:** How do anatomy, curse, appetite, intelligence, or habitat
  explain its rules?
- **Behavior:** What does it want when it is not fighting?
- **Escalation:** What makes the situation worse?
- **Counterplay:** What can players learn, prepare, manipulate, or sacrifice?
- **Aftermath:** What changes if it survives, dies, moves, feeds, bargains, or is
  exploited?
- **Sandbox role:** Which routes, settlements, factions, resources, or customs
  does its existence affect?

Reject designs whose identity is only higher damage, Armor Rating, or immunity.

## Mechanical Construction

### Choose one primary engine

Examples:

- relentless pursuit
- area denial
- grapple and relocation
- fear and separation
- changing terrain
- regeneration with a discoverable interruption
- host, anchor, or site dependence
- social taboo or bargain
- resource predation

Secondary mechanics should reinforce the primary engine. Avoid six unrelated
spectacles.

### Choose tactical architecture only when needed

For an ordinary monster, the primary engine and six attacks are sufficient. For
a set piece, coordinated force, or recurring enemy, select one additional
architecture:

- intent and setup across rounds
- damage-state or phase change
- linked attack tables for changing stances, bodies, or behavior states
- command, formation, or troop scale
- targetable anatomy or anchors
- objective pressure
- category-specific hunt, doctrine, manifestation, or morale logic

Use the tactical references for costs, tells, counterplay, and bookkeeping
limits. Do not stack the full allowances of several modules onto one enemy.

### Build a readable threat

Use this sequence:

`sign → approach → attack pattern → escalation → counterplay → consequence`

Players need not know exact statistics, but they must be able to learn useful
behavior. Necessary weaknesses require multiple clues and observable
confirmation.

### Test combat roles

Across six Monster Attacks, vary purpose:

- direct pressure
- movement or positioning
- multi-target danger
- condition or resource pressure
- escalation or battlefield change
- signature climax, vulnerability, or behavior

Not every attack must deal damage. Every result must change a decision.

If an attack gathers power, mark its target or geometry and let setup consume
real tempo. If an attack moves to another state table, resolve it fully before
changing the active table for the next attack. If current Strength changes
attack access, place core behavior on low results and intact-body or
formation-dependent behavior on high results.

### Preserve agency

Hard control needs a limit, warning, escape, resistance, rescue decision, or
changed role. Count how many meaningful turns a target loses. A spectacular
effect that removes a player for most of a session is usually poor design.

### Scale honestly

Compare:

- attack dice and damage
- number of targets
- Armor Rating and immunities
- action denial
- range and mobility
- regeneration and return conditions
- probability and duration of conditions
- information needed for counterplay
- party resources consumed

Area attacks should usually be lighter per target. Rare defenses require clearer
weaknesses. Large monsters should dominate space and priorities, not merely
inflate numbers.

## Sandbox Ecology

Give the monster a place in the living world:

- prey, food, or magical sustenance
- territory and migration
- breeding, spawning, binding, or creation
- signs left in weather, animals, corpses, tools, or custom
- people who fear, worship, hunt, hide, feed, or profit from it
- goods and routes it makes scarce or valuable
- what fills the vacuum if it is removed

Ecology must generate choices, not a lecture. Convert background into tracks,
prices, taboos, patrols, offerings, damaged infrastructure, and rumors.

## Encounter Construction

Create situations, not mandatory fights. Each encounter needs:

1. an existing local arrangement
2. a concrete physical bind
3. incompatible interests
4. time, terrain, weather, or social pressure
5. at least two viable interventions
6. an aftermath that matters

The monster may be threat, symptom, enforcer, witness, territory fact, or moral
accelerant. It should not automatically be the only problem or the obvious
target.

Do not protect a prepared solution. Players may fight, flee, observe, bargain,
feed, trap, redirect, expose, exploit, or leave.

## Lore and Discovery

Use three levels:

1. **Public knowledge:** identity, sign, habit, or danger.
2. **Useful hint:** partial evidence that suggests preparation or experiment.
3. **Directional lore:** stronger clue framed through witnessed behavior,
   tradition, material trace, or story.

Do not state mechanics in Lore Roll prose. If a hidden weakness is necessary for
normal victory, row 2 must hint at it and row 3 must point more strongly without
giving rules text.

Distribute essential information across lore, encounters, environmental signs,
survivors, failed attempts, and the creature's reactions. One failed roll must
not make the monster an unknowable wall.

## Resources

Harvestable resources must arise from specific physiology or supernatural
behavior. Begin with the harvest procedure, then state uses, limits, duration,
and consequences.

Prefer:

- narrow Artifact Dice
- Potency or Virulence effects
- unique tools, lures, wards, reagents, and transformations
- one-use effects that create a decision
- social, ecological, factional, or bodily costs

Avoid generic `+1` bonuses, interchangeable monster parts, unlimited repeatable
healing, and effects that erase another profession's niche. Preserve canonical
potion ingredients where the manuscript establishes them.

## Entry Structure

Use the manuscript's established order unless the target chapter differs:

1. `### Monster Name`
2. one-sentence physical vignette
3. statblock
4. `#### Monster Attacks` with six results, or an explicit no-attacks rule
5. GM-facing description
6. `#### Lore Roll` with three graduated rows
7. at least two `#### Random Encounter: Title` situations when the format calls
   for encounters
8. `RESOURCES` block
9. legend, when the creature shapes regional memory

Use `references/entry-size-calibration.md` as a diagnostic. Length is evidence of coverage,
not a substitute for quality. Do not pad an entry to meet an average.

## New Entry Workflow

1. Write the monster contract.
2. Select a structure model and one primary engine.
3. Search the taxonomy for overlap and native precedents.
4. If needed, choose one tactical architecture and state its cost, tell, and
   counterplay.
5. Draft signs, behavior, morale or appetite, and counterplay before numeric
   attacks.
6. Set attributes, Armor Rating, movement, and special abilities.
7. Draft six attacks by function, a justified wider Strength-band table, or a
   small linked network of state tables whose transitions are explicit.
8. Write Lore Roll clues that teach the same counterplay.
9. Add two encounter situations with different pressures.
10. Derive resources from the creature's body and campaign relationships.
11. Decide whether it warrants a legend.
12. Run mechanical, player-agency, lore, voice, sandbox, and handling audits.

## Rewrite Workflow

1. Identify which parts are actually in scope.
2. Preserve established identity and rules unless change is authorized.
3. List current weaknesses: repetition, opaque counterplay, generic ecology,
   scripted encounters, unusable resources, voice drift, or imbalance.
4. Revise the smallest coherent set of connected parts.
5. Recheck every clue and encounter if the weakness or behavior changed.
6. Recheck resources if physiology or abilities changed.
7. Compare before and after at scene and campaign scale.

Do not quietly alter statblocks while rewriting prose.

## Audit Matrix

Rate each **clean**, **watch**, or **revise**:

| Lens | Question |
| --- | --- |
| Identity | Is the monster recognizable through behavior and rules? |
| Native grammar | Does it use established verbs and scales? |
| Telegraphing | Can players notice danger before full punishment? |
| Counterplay | Can choices and preparation change the encounter? |
| Agency | Do control and immunity preserve participation? |
| Variety | Do attacks create different decisions? |
| Time | Do setup, phase, and cooldown rules create answerable windows? |
| State tables | Are the active state, transition timing, tells, and destination tables unambiguous? |
| Organization | Do numbers create formation, morale, or objective play rather than excess turns? |
| State | Do injury and disruption change behavior where the fiction demands it? |
| Balance | Does power match access, clarity, and frequency? |
| Ecology | Does the monster alter a real place or practice? |
| Sandbox value | Can it produce several situations and outcomes? |
| Lore | Do clues invite deduction without withholding necessities? |
| Resources | Are rewards unique, bounded, and consequential? |
| Voice | Does the text sound concrete, restrained, and setting-native? |
| Usability | Can the GM find and run the rule quickly? |

## Final Quality Gate

- [ ] The first sign and core threat are clear.
- [ ] Anatomy, curse, appetite, or habitat explains the mechanics.
- [ ] Six attacks have distinct tactical or fictional jobs.
- [ ] Any phase, troop, intent, reaction, or attrition rule has one clear state
      marker and a visible fictional cause.
- [ ] Linked attack tables change genuine capabilities, resolve transitions
      after the current attack, and never smuggle in extra actions.
- [ ] Setup attacks spend tempo, declare useful information, and have practical
      counterplay.
- [ ] Human, beast, monster, and demon behavior arises from the correct kind of
      intelligence, body, organization, appetite, or metaphysical dependency.
- [ ] The strongest control effect preserves meaningful participation.
- [ ] Necessary counterplay is discoverable through several paths.
- [ ] Encounters support more than fight or flee.
- [ ] Ecology creates routes, customs, scarcity, or faction behavior.
- [ ] Resources are unique and cannot be farmed into a broken loop.
- [ ] Removal of the monster changes the local situation.
- [ ] Lore, mechanics, encounters, resources, and legend tell the same truth.
- [ ] Terms, tables, headings, and formatting match the manuscript.
