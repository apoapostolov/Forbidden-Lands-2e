## Why This Manual Exists

Most AI-generated roleplaying prose fails not because it is ungrammatical or off-topic, but because it reads like a well-organized report about a roleplaying book rather than a page from one. It summarizes the mood instead of creating it. It describes the world instead of building it. It explains the rules instead of teaching them through use.

The Forbidden Lands manuscript was written by Tomas Härenstam, Erik Granström, and other authors who understood that a corebook is fiction, reference manual, and teaching tool simultaneously — and that the reader should never feel the seams between those functions.

This manual teaches how they achieved that, and how to continue achieving it.

## What The Original Authors Do Well

Before learning rules, understand what is being imitated and why it works.

### They think in objects, not concepts

The manuscript opens with: "These days, strange creatures haunt those valleys, beasts that should not be, for the land is cursed." The curse is not explained philosophically. It is shown through the creatures and valleys. When the combat chapter opens, a character wipes blood from his blade on a silk handkerchief while quoting poetry — and the other character reaches for a hidden dagger in his boot. The authors never write "violence is complex." They write the silk, the blood, and the boot.

### They teach through consequence, not lecture

The magic chapter does not start with "Magic is an important subsystem." It starts with a druid raising a being of pure stone from a cliff while orcs hesitate. The reader learns what magic does before learning how it works. The journey chapter does not explain that travel is dangerous — it opens with a character pointing across a forest and naming the places, the druid, the lizardfolk. You learn the world is connected and risky because you are already inside a journey.

### They trust the reader

The manuscript rarely explains why a rule exists. It defines the rule, states the consequence, and moves on. "Before you enter combat, you should always ask yourself: Is it worth it?" That line teaches lethality, player agency, and tone in nine words. An AI would write: "Combat in Forbidden Lands is designed to be lethal, which means players should carefully consider whether engaging in combat is the optimal choice given the potential consequences."

### They vary pressure, not decoration

Short sentences for hard rules. Medium sentences for explanation. Long sentences only when the scene needs accumulated physical detail. The variation is functional, not stylistic. The original authors do not write short sentences to sound punchy. They write short sentences because a rule needs to land.

### They use dark humor sparingly and earn it

The epigraphs have wit: "Like any girl ever let you close!" and "But he does know everything about a good grip on his shaft." The humor is in-character, crude, and brief. It never breaks into modern irony, self-awareness, or winking at the reader. The humor belongs to the people of the world, not to the author commenting on them.

## Voice Identity

The manuscript voice is:

- harsh without being sadistic
- clear without being clinical
- grounded in body, weather, material, and use
- practical without being flat
- myth-shadowed without being vague
- atmospheric without ornament for its own sake
- confident and unsentimental
- compressed

It is not:

- generic dark fantasy
- modern game-design commentary
- clinical instructional copy
- faux-archaic theater
- proposal or design-document language
- therapeutic or corporate

## The Six Registers

The manuscript uses six related registers. They share a voice but adjust pressure.

### 1. Fiction opener

The chapter epigraphs. Brief, image-rich, anchored in a body or object. They close on an image, never on a moral or reflection.

How the originals work: The Combat epigraph puts two characters in contrast — one quoting Shakespeare while cleaning a blade, the other reaching for a hidden weapon while dying. It teaches the reader that this world has literate people who still kill and get killed. The Gear epigraph has soldiers joking crudely around a campfire about axe maintenance. It teaches the reader that equipment is personal, unglamorous, and daily.

### 2. Flavor exposition

Tells the reader what kind of world this is and pivots quickly to what that means for adventurers. The first sentence usually names the condition. The second or third sentence turns toward play.

How the originals work: "Markets in the Ravenlands run like early medieval exchanges: local, personal, and shaped by scarcity more than coin." One sentence, and the reader knows the entire economic register of the world.

### 3. Rules exposition

Defines, explains, limits. Short paragraphs. Front-loaded definitions. Exceptions arrive after the baseline. The voice does not soften for rules — it just gets more precise.

How the originals work: "Casting a spell requires a great deal of mental exertion and requires that you spend one or more WP." No hedging, no buildup. The cost is stated plainly and the chapter proceeds.

### 4. Mixed flavor-and-rule bridge

Opens with material reality, pivots to operation. This is the register where AI drafts fail most often, because the AI either writes pure flavor (mood fog) or pure rules (clinical) and cannot hold both at once.

How the originals work: The front matter describes the Blood Mist as a demonic veil that sucked the life out of wanderers — then immediately explains that it has lifted and the characters are now free to travel. World truth becomes game state in two sentences.

### 5. Item and equipment description

Concrete noun first. Function and distinction. Little ornament. The object is described as if the writer has held it.

How the originals work: The Makeshift Tools description opens by naming every ugly item in the kit — shovel, pick, hammer, pry-iron, wedges, nails, cord, leather, knife, oil — then says "None of it is fine, and none of it is meant to last." The reader can feel the weight and the cheapness.

### 6. Example scene

Names actors, shows order, teaches through consequence. The best examples feel like moments in a campaign, not test cases.

How the originals work: The combat example with Nirmena and Tyrgar establishes that they are leaving a ruin with only cheap gems, then drops them into an orc encounter. The reader learns initiative, action economy, and damage through the scene unfolding. Every rule hook is tied to a character doing something, not to an abstract explanation.

## Fiction Craft

This is what separates good roleplaying prose from competent technical writing. The skill is not in knowing the diction — it is in knowing how to make the reader feel something while also learning something.

### Show through objects, not through statements

Do not write: "The Ravenlands are a dangerous place."
Write: "Feulenmark, where my people once ruled."

The first is a claim. The second is a character pointing at the forest below her, and the reader feels loss, ownership, danger, and history in six words.

### Use specific names for specific weight

"Tonder lies Feulenmark" has weight because it is a name. Generic statements ("the lands to the south") carry no weight. Name the place. Name the person. Name the blade.

### Anchor mood in the senses

Not "the journey was difficult." But: the cold, the mire, the frost on a pack strap, the weight of a mail shirt after three days in rain. The original authors build mood through accumulated sensory detail, never through stated mood.

### Let restraint do the emotional work

The manuscript's most powerful moments are the least decorated. "Is it worth it?" at the end of the combat introduction carries more weight than any three-sentence warning about lethality. The question marks are the emotion. The restraint is the force.

### Write characters who are distinct in two lines

The epigraphs create full characters quickly. Haggamur greases his axe and jokes about spit. You know him — his vanity, his humor, his roughness — in one sentence. Mormelan quotes literature while cleaning a blade. That single gesture tells you he is educated, dangerous, and theatrical. The skill is economy: one concrete gesture that implies a whole person.

### Use perspective to create narrative tension

"Reubenwald was in no shape to respond, clutching his bleeding chest, but his left hand reached for his grandmother's magical dagger, hidden in his boot." This is not just description — it is a reversal in progress. The reader's attention shifts from death to possibility. Narrative tension in a rulebook epigraph.

## Emotional Intelligence

The manuscript respects the reader's emotional range without manipulating it.

### How the manuscript handles death

Plainly, with consequence. Critical injuries are described in bodily terms — "ceaseless pain," "withered loins," "neck-broken remnant." The naming refuses both clinical distance and dramatic performance. The reader understands that these injuries are horrible because the words make them physical, not because the text tells the reader to feel horror.

When a wound ends an adventurer's career, the manuscript states it honestly: "Alive and lucid, but your Strength and Agility are both permanently reduced to 1. Retirement from active adventuring is the default." No mourning. No heroic framing. Just the truth of the body.

### How the manuscript handles kindness

The retirement rules for crippled characters show genuine care: "The companion has not failed. They have endured." But the kindness is stated in world-facing language, not therapy language. "They may keep the camp, tend the stores, watch the horses." The dignity is in the list of things the character can still do, not in a statement about their emotional state.

### How the manuscript handles wonder

Sparingly, through specificity. When magic is described, it is through the stone rising from the cliff, the druid's eyes gone grey as granite, her mouth gaping like a pit. The wonder is in the image, not in the narrator's awe. The manuscript never says "a wondrous display of magical power." It shows the cliff leaning in.

### How the manuscript handles humor

Through character, in dialogue, briefly. The crude jokes in the Gear epigraph work because they emerge from the situation (soldiers caring for equipment around a fire) and from established personalities. The manuscript does not inject humor through authorial wit or narrative irony. The humor belongs to the people of the world.

### The rule for AI

Do not namerop emotions. Do not state how the reader should feel. Do not use: deeply, profoundly, truly, incredibly, utterly, overwhelmingly, heartbreakingly, awe-inspiring. Build the feeling from objects, actions, and restraint. If the reader does not feel it from the materials alone, adding emotional adjectives will not fix it — the materials are wrong.

## Paragraph Architecture

The manuscript builds paragraphs as units of function, not as essay blocks.

### The four dominant shapes

**Statement then consequence.** For items, world conditions, subsystem intros. Sentence one names the thing plainly. Sentence two gives use, consequence, or cost.

**Definition then procedure then exception.** For core rules, timing, recovery, action logic. Define the term, teach the process, add the exception only if needed. The order matters — do not begin with the exception.

**Condition then pressure then response.** For journeys, weather, scarcity, camp. Establish what presses on the characters, show the danger, state what players must do.

**Description then distinction.** For weapons, materials, kin and profession intros. Describe the thing materially, then clarify what makes it different from its neighbor.

### Paragraph length

Default to one paragraph, one job. Split once a paragraph starts defining, explaining, and caveating simultaneously.

Common working range: 35 to 60 words, 2 to 4 sentences. Longer paragraphs exist in content-dense chapters (talents, adventurer creation) but even those stay linear — they do not spiral.

### Paragraph endings

End on: a hard fact, a practical warning, a cultural distinction, a use case, a note of danger. Not on: soft qualifiers, self-conscious commentary, restated abstractions, or editorial hedges.

## Sentence Craft

### Short sentences

Use for definitions, warnings, hard tonal pivots, paragraph closers. "Is it worth it?" "That is also why they fail." "Dead is dead." These carry weight because they arrive after longer sentences and because they contain only what matters.

### Medium sentences

The manuscript default. Start with the thing being described, add one clarifying clause, end on purpose or effect. This is the safest shape for new writing.

### Long sentences

Use only when accumulated physical detail earns the length. "A short shovel, a hand pick, a hammer, a pry-iron, a few wedges, nails, cord, scraps of leather, a knife, a flask of oil." This works because every item is concrete and the list builds weight. Long sentences that stack abstractions or nested clauses do not work.

### Sentence openings

Common: the object ("The mace..."), the condition ("Markets in the Ravenlands..."), the action ("Casting a spell..."), the category label.

Avoid: throat-clearing ("It is worth noting that..."), empty anticipation ("There are several factors to consider..."), meta-commentary about what the text is doing ("As we discussed earlier...").

### Sentence endings

The strongest sentences end on material nouns, bodily consequences, or hard verbs. The weakest sentences trail into abstract qualifiers, self-explanation, or mood-word padding. Sentence endings are where AI writing leaks most visibly.

## Diction

### Preferred word fields

**Body:** blood, hand, bone, teeth, breath, scar, gut, flesh, lung, wound.

**Material and craft:** iron, stone, grip, haft, cord, wood, nail, hide, tar, edge, wedge, spike, leather, cloth.

**Travel and weather:** cold, rain, frost, trail, mire, ridge, road, ash, mud, pack, ford, dusk.

**Survival and pressure:** burden, hunger, fear, rot, smoke, strain, ruin, hearth, campfire, ration.

### Preferred verbs

cut, drag, bind, haul, split, twist, break, climb, carry, cling, stalk, brace, wield, bleed, wrench, grind, hack, pull, shove, pin.

### The diction rule

If a concrete verb exists, use it. "Break the line" not "disrupt the situation." "The chill stiffens your body" not "the cold has a negative effect." "You fall prone" not "your character is considered to have fallen into a prone position."

### What to avoid in diction

- Modern therapeutic language (meaningful, impactful, wellness, self-care)
- Corporate register (stakeholders, leverage, synergy, optimize)
- Academic hedging (arguably, perhaps, it could be said, tends to)
- Blog-tutorial filler (basically, in other words, this means that)
- Inflated epic without grounding (ancient power, dark destiny, grim fate)
- Faux-archaic parody (thou, forsooth, hath, verily)

## Rules Prose

Rules prose must sound like the same book. It adjusts precision, not voice.

### The rules-prose contract

1. Define the thing early
2. State the condition plainly
3. Give the effect cleanly
4. Name exceptions only when needed
5. Use existing game terms, not synonyms
6. Keep examples brief and game-active

### Stable terminology

If the manuscript calls it "Broken," always call it Broken. Not "incapacitated," "neutralized," or "downed." If the manuscript calls it a "slow action," do not write "a major action" or "your primary action."

### Rules and flavor coexistence

Rules prose may carry atmosphere, but never at the cost of procedure. "Casting a spell requires a great deal of mental exertion and requires that you spend one or more WP." The "mental exertion" is flavor. The WP cost is the rule. Both live in one sentence. Neither drowns the other.

## Item And Equipment Prose

### How the originals sound

The best item descriptions in the manuscript read like they were written by someone who has held the object, used it in the field, and watched it break. The Makeshift Tools entry opens with a list of ugly objects — shovel, pick, hammer, pry-iron — and then says they are cheap and they fail. The description never romanticizes the item and never reduces it to a stat block.

### The item-description formula

1. Name the thing and its material nature in the first sentence
2. Explain who uses it, where, and under what conditions
3. End on function, limitation, danger, or cultural meaning
4. Keep the physical construction visible — what it is made of, how it is shaped, where it wears out
5. Distinguish it from its nearest neighbor in the gear table

### What AI gets wrong in item descriptions

- Generic fantasy catalog copy: "A fine weapon crafted by master smiths"
- Stat-block voice: "This item provides +1 to the relevant skill"
- Purple overwrites: "The blade sings with ancient power"
- Detached abstraction: "This tool serves multiple purposes in the field"

Replace all of these with material specificity. What is it made of. Who made it. Why it costs what it costs. What happens when it breaks.

## Mixed Flavor And Rule Bridges

This is the register AI fails at most, because it requires holding two frequencies at once.

### The correct pattern

Open with a world truth or material condition. Pivot into the rule. Let the rule inherit the mood without interrupting it.

Good: "The Blood Mist has lifted. You are finally free to leave your homes and travel far and wide through the Forbidden Lands. This chapter explains how these processes work."

The transition from myth (Blood Mist) to practice (this chapter) is seamless because both sentences share the same world. The manuscript never writes "Now let's look at the travel mechanics" — it writes "You are free to travel" and then teaches travel.

### The wrong pattern

Decorative lore paragraph. Decorative lore paragraph. Decorative lore paragraph. Abrupt switch to clinical instructional voice.

This is the most common AI failure mode in mixed-register writing. The AI writes setting text that goes nowhere, then abandons it for tutorial text that ignores the setting. The fix is to make every flavor line point toward the rule it serves.

## Example Scenes

### When to write one

When multiple small rules interact. When timing matters. When the rule has a likely false reading. When the reader needs to see fiction become procedure.

### When not to write one

When one sentence resolves the rule. When the example only restates the rule without adding sequence. When the example introduces more complexity than the confusion it solves.

### How to write one

Name who acts. Establish the situation. State the roll or trigger. State the result. State the consequence. Stop.

The best examples feel like a brief exchange of blows or a night camp decision, not like annotated design notes.

### Good and bad example shapes

Good: "The raider has already spent both actions, so he cannot parry when Heme swings."

Bad: "As explained above, characters normally have two actions, and because of the reactive-action structure established earlier, the raider is no longer eligible to parry."

The good version teaches through a scene. The bad version teaches through cross-referencing its own explanations.

## Anti-AI Pattern Library

These patterns are derived from the Wikipedia "Signs of AI writing" guide and adapted specifically for Forbidden Lands manuscript prose. Every pattern below has been observed in AI drafts of roleplaying text.

### Pattern 1: Inflated significance

**AI writes:** "The mace stands as a testament to the enduring craftsmanship of the Ravenlands."
**Manuscript writes:** "The mace is an ancient and effective weapon."

**Detection:** "stands as," "serves as," "is a testament," "marks a shift," "reflects broader," "underscores."
**Fix:** Replace significance claims with plain facts.

### Pattern 2: Promotional language

**AI writes:** "A stunning array of devastating magical abilities awaits the aspiring sorcerer."
**Manuscript writes:** "What most people call magic is a catch-all term for forces at work behind the veil of the world."

**Detection:** "stunning," "devastating," "incredible," "remarkable," "breathtaking," "must-have."
**Fix:** Describe the thing. Do not sell it.

### Pattern 3: Superficial -ing analysis

**AI writes:** "The talent system creates deep character customization, enabling players to craft unique builds while fostering strategic decision-making."
**Manuscript writes:** Names the talents, explains what each rank does, stops.

**Detection:** Sentences ending in participial chains: "enabling," "fostering," "highlighting," "showcasing."
**Fix:** State what the thing does. Stop.

### Pattern 4: Copula avoidance

**AI writes:** "The shield serves as both a defensive tool and a symbol of the warrior's oath."
**Manuscript writes:** "The shield is a defensive weapon."

**Detection:** "serves as," "stands as," "functions as," "marks," "represents," "boasts," "features," "offers."
**Fix:** Use "is," "are," "has" when they are the correct verb.

### Pattern 5: Elegant variation

**AI writes:** "Willpower fuels your talents. This inner reserve allows you to push beyond normal limits. Your mental fortitude determines how many abilities you can activate."
**Manuscript writes:** "WP" every time.

**Detection:** Multiple synonyms for the same game term within one passage.
**Fix:** Repeat the game term. Repetition is cleaner than variation for technical vocabulary.

### Pattern 6: Negative parallelism

**AI writes:** "This is not just a sword. It is a lifeline."
**Manuscript writes:** Nothing like this. The original authors do not use this structure.

**Detection:** "not just X, but Y," "not merely A, it's B," "more than just."
**Fix:** State the positive claim directly.

### Pattern 7: Rule-of-three padding

**AI writes:** "The wilderness is vast, dangerous, and unforgiving."
**Manuscript writes:** "The wilderness is dangerous." (If it matters.) Or does not state it at all, letting the mechanics demonstrate it.

**Detection:** Three adjectives, three nouns, or three phrases in a pattern where two or one would suffice.
**Fix:** Use the number of points the content actually needs.

### Pattern 8: Em-dash overuse

**AI writes:** "The blade — forged in the deep furnaces of the dwarven halls — cuts through armor like paper."
**Manuscript writes:** "The blade cuts through armor. It was forged in the deep furnaces."

**Detection:** More than one em dash per paragraph.
**Fix:** Use commas, periods, or sentence breaks.

### Pattern 9: Hedging stacks

**AI writes:** "It could potentially be argued that the weapon might have some utility in certain combat situations."
**Manuscript writes:** "The weapon is useful in close combat."

**Detection:** "could," "potentially," "might," "perhaps," "arguably," "in certain cases" clustered together.
**Fix:** State the fact. If you are uncertain, say "unclear" once and move on.

### Pattern 10: Generic positive conclusion

**AI writes:** "With these tools at their disposal, adventurers are well-equipped to face whatever challenges the Ravenlands may hold."
**Manuscript writes:** Nothing. The manuscript ends sections on hard facts or practical warnings, not on uplift.

**Detection:** Final sentence that could apply to any game, any chapter, any topic.
**Fix:** End on a concrete fact, a specific rule hook, or a note of danger. Or end one sentence earlier.

### Pattern 11: Filler phrases

**AI writes:** "In order to ensure that the character is able to successfully..."
**Manuscript writes:** "To..."

**Detection:** "in order to," "due to the fact that," "at this point in time," "it is important to note," "has the ability to."
**Fix:** Replace with shorter direct phrasing.

### Pattern 12: Blog-tutorial voice

**AI writes:** "Let's take a closer look at how combat works in Forbidden Lands."
**Manuscript writes:** "When a conflict begins, the first step is to determine who has the initiative."

**Detection:** "let's," "let us," "as we discussed," "as you can see," "this means that," "in other words."
**Fix:** Define the thing. Teach the process. Do not narrate your own explanation.

### Pattern 13: Sycophantic tone

**AI writes:** "The brilliant design of the push mechanic creates a wonderfully tense experience."
**Manuscript writes:** Rules text about pushing. No praise of the design.

**Detection:** "brilliant," "wonderfully," "beautifully designed," "elegantly crafted."
**Fix:** describe the mechanic. The reader will judge its quality.

### Pattern 14: Abstract atmosphere

**AI writes:** "A sense of dread permeated the ancient halls."
**Manuscript writes:** "The walls of the gorge listened and leaned in."

**Detection:** Sentences describing feelings or moods without physical anchors.
**Fix:** Replace the mood word with the object that creates the mood. What does the reader see, hear, smell, or touch?

### Pattern 15: Structural uniformity

**AI writes:** Every paragraph as topic-elaboration-conclusion. Every list as three items. Every section as introduction-body-summary.

**Detection:** Read three consecutive paragraphs. If they have the same shape, the AI is running a template.
**Fix:** Vary paragraph shape by job. One paragraph defines. The next warns. The next describes. They should have different structures because they have different purposes.

## Revision Method

### First pass: job check

For each paragraph, state its job in one word. If you cannot, the paragraph is doing too much or too little.

### Second pass: diction check

For each sentence, check: is the subject concrete? Is the verb physical? Does the sentence end on weight? Replace abstract verbs with concrete ones. Replace noun phrases with single nouns.

### Third pass: anti-AI check

Scan for the 15 patterns above. Also check for: synonym cycling on game terms, sentences that could apply to any fantasy game, paragraphs that summarize their own content, endings that uplift or reassure.

### Fourth pass: the read-aloud test

Read the draft aloud. Every sentence that sounds like a summary of a book rather than a page from one needs to be rewritten. Every sentence that sounds like a committee wrote it needs to be rewritten. Every sentence that sounds like it is explaining why the sentence before it was important needs to be cut.

### Fifth pass: the neighbor test

Place the draft beside a paragraph from the same chapter of the manuscript. If the draft draws attention to itself — too elaborate, too simple, too clinical, too decorated — adjust until it sits naturally.

## The Final Test

A paragraph is ready for the manuscript when it passes all five of these:

1. **It sounds like it was always there.** Not imported, not grafted, not translated from a design document.
2. **It does one job.** Define, describe, warn, bridge, demonstrate. One.
3. **It contains no AI tells.** None of the 15 patterns. No filler. No hedging clusters. No elegant variation.
4. **Its strongest words are at the end.** The final noun or verb carries the sentence's weight.
5. **A GM or player can use it under live-play pressure.** If it is rules text, the answer must be in the first read. If it is flavor text, it must sharpen the world rather than diffusing it.
