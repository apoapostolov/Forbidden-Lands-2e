## Purpose

This reference is for writing examples that must teach several small rules inside one short scene.

Use it when the manuscript needs a playable example rather than a static rule summary.
The goal is not only to explain what happens.
The goal is to explain it in the order the table experiences it.

## Example Pattern

The strongest multi-rule examples in this manuscript usually do five things:

1. establish who is acting and what the immediate pressure is
2. move through the action in real timing order
3. mention only the rule hooks that actually matter
4. show consequences immediately after the triggering action
5. stop as soon as the lesson is learned

They do not try to exhaust the subsystem.
They teach one live sequence.

## Combat Sequence Calibration

Primary sources:

- `corebook/05-combat-and-damage.md`
- the combat example beginning near the `SHOVE` and `SLASH` rules

### Source Sequence

The example with Tyrgar and the orcs teaches several rules at once:

- movement across range bands
- action economy with one fast and one slow action
- the effect of having already spent actions
- the value of knocking an enemy prone
- bonus dice from position
- damage overflow after the first success
- Broken as the consequence at the end of the attack

### Why It Works

The example does not pause to restate each rule in abstract terms.
It moves from:

1. enemy movement
2. Tyrgar's shove
3. Tyrgar's attack
4. the orc's inability to defend
5. the damage result
6. the next character's turn

That order matters.
The reader learns action timing by following the scene.

### What To Preserve In A New Example

If you write a new combat example that mixes several rules, keep these traits:

- start with the tactical position, not with a lecture
- name which action is fast and which is slow only when that distinction matters
- mention defensive limits only when they change the outcome
- convert extra successes into immediate consequence, not delayed explanation
- end once the interaction has become clear

### Good Shape

Good:

- “The raider has already spent both actions, so he cannot parry when Heme swings.”

Bad:

- “As explained above, characters normally have two actions, and because of the reactive-action structure established earlier, the raider is no longer eligible to parry.”

### Writing Lesson

Combat examples should read like a brief exchange of blows, not like annotated design notes.

## Journey Sequence Calibration

Primary sources:

- `corebook/08-journeys.md`
- `corebook/06-critical-injuries.md`

### Source Sequence

The `BARE GROUND` and `SLEEP` rules teach an understated but important chain:

- the party saves time by not making camp
- each traveler must roll `SURVIVAL`
- failure means no sleep
- no sleep means becoming `SLEEPY`
- no fire also exposes the group to cold
- at `HEAT 0` or lower, the cold risk gets harsher

This is not one rule.
It is a pressure chain.

### Why It Works

The text starts with the tempting shortcut.
Only then does it reveal the price.

That is a strong manuscript habit:

- choice first
- cost second
- downstream pressure third

### What To Preserve In A New Example

If you write a journey example that combines camp, sleep, weather, and condition pressure:

- start with the travel decision
- move into the roll
- state the failed comfort or shelter outcome
- then show the condition or recovery consequence

Do not begin with condition bookkeeping.
Begin with the road choice that creates the condition.

### Good Shape

Good:

- “The company pushes on into dusk and saves the Quarter Day they would have spent making camp. That night each of them must roll `SURVIVAL` to find a place to sleep. Siga fails. She gets no sleep, becomes `SLEEPY`, and because there is no fire she must also face the cold.”

Bad:

- “Remember that characters can become `SLEEPY` and `COLD` due to multiple interacting factors in the journey subsystem.”

### Writing Lesson

Journey examples should make the player feel the bargain:
save time now, pay for it in comfort, safety, or recovery.

## Recovery Sequence Calibration

Primary sources:

- `corebook/05-combat-and-damage.md`
- `corebook/06-critical-injuries.md`
- the Tyrgar critical-injury example

### Source Sequence

The Tyrgar example teaches a compact but complex chain:

- a character is already hurt
- the next hit makes him Broken
- a critical injury is rolled
- the injury creates a lethal timer
- the character may recover consciousness before the timer ends
- self-treatment is possible but risky
- failure leaves only the remaining time to find help

The healing rules elsewhere add more sequence pressure:

- a successful `HEALING` roll takes one hour
- it pauses the timer during treatment
- each patient has a daily limit
- poor conditions can penalize treatment

### Why It Works

The example is short because it does not explain every branch.
It follows only the branch Tyrgar actually walks:

1. Broken
2. injury roll
3. death clock
4. return to awareness
5. failed self-heal
6. narrowed remaining window

That is enough to teach urgency.

### What To Preserve In A New Example

If you write a recovery or first-aid example:

- establish the wound and the timer early
- keep the clock visible
- mention treatment time only if it changes the stakes
- show the difference between getting back on your feet and actually being safe
- stop before the example turns into a full rescue narrative

### Good Shape

Good:

- “Varg is conscious again after an hour, but that does not end the danger. The spear in his side will still kill him unless someone succeeds with `HEALING` before the timer runs out.”

Bad:

- “This demonstrates the distinction between recovering attribute points and resolving the underlying critical-injury state.”

### Writing Lesson

Recovery examples should feel like dwindling time, cold ground, and bad odds.
They should never sound clinical.

## Reusable Rules For Writing Sequences

1. Use names, not generic placeholders.
2. Make the triggering choice or blow come first.
3. Teach order of operations through the scene itself.
4. Mention only the rule hooks that change the outcome.
5. Keep the consequence close to the roll that caused it.
6. If the example requires three paragraphs to explain one interaction, it is too broad.
7. End on the practical result: the enemy is Broken, the traveler is `SLEEPY`, the wounded ally is still dying.
