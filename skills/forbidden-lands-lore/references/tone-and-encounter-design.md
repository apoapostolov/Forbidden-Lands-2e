# Tone, Voice Classification, and Encounter Design

This is the lore-side authority for **what the world feels like** and
**what kinds of situations are authentic to it**. The companion skill
`forbidden-lands-writing` owns sentence-level prose technique. This
file owns the setting-grounded answer to: _is this scenario in the
right register for Forbidden Lands?_

For monster-encounter mechanics specifically, also consult
`skills/forbidden-lands-bestiary/SKILL.md`.

## Genre Classification

Forbidden Lands 2E is, in this manuscript:

**Re-feudal medieval survival fantasy with a Swedish tone, the
realistic authenticity of village life, and a soft Tolkienesque
poetic worldbuilding.**

Each clause is a constraint. Read them in order.

### "Re-feudal"

The world is not feudal in the high-medieval sense. It is _re-feudal_:
ten generations of Blood Mist destroyed travel, trade, and central
authority. Old roads are reopening. Old claims are being relitigated.
Old kingdoms exist as rumor, as tax-collectors, as pretenders, as
ruined walls. Power is local: a clan elder, a Rust priory, an oath-lord,
a merchant who controls the mill. Courts and banks do not exist.
Nobles exist but their writ stops at the next hill.

What this means in prose:
- Authority is personal, not institutional.
- Coin is silver, scarce, and often "old Alder War coin."
- Travel between villages is an event, not a routine.
- A stranger on the road is news.

### "Medieval Survival"

Not the medieval of cathedrals and tournaments. The medieval of
**food, fire, and walls.** Grain is tracked in four steps —
Bare, Thin, Adequate, Full — and most villages start at Thin.
Killing the elderly when they can no longer contribute is "viewed
as unavoidable but sad." A village of a hundred is a large village.
A bad winter forces eating the seed grain. A levy from a clan
warlord can starve a stead.

Survival is the floor of every scene. The question "did they eat
today" is always present even when not asked.

### "Swedish Tone"

The setting is Free League's, written in Swedish, translated.
The tonal signature in English prose:

- **Concrete and quiet.** No grand declarations. Statements of
  weather, body, and bread.
- **Dry humor used sparely.** A line of black wit at the end of
  a hard scene, not throughout.
- **Melancholy as default emotional weather.** Loss is older than
  the speaker. Joy is small and earned.
- **Names ring Nordic / Slavic / Mongol-Arabic depending on the
  people.** Not Anglo-Saxon. Not Romance.
- **Landscape over architecture.** Bog, fen, hill, alder, birch,
  pine, granite, frost. Stonework is described by what time has
  done to it, not by its plan.
- **Old gods are present in place names and weather**, not on
  thrones. The land is religious before any building is.

### "Realistic Village Authenticity"

Villages are not adventure-hub sets. They are economies. Each one
has:
- A working food source (field, herd, fishery, fungus, foraging).
- A water source (well, stream, cistern, river).
- A defense (palisade, wall, cliff, marsh).
- A shrine (Raven, Rust, Wyrm, Heme, Huge, Clay, Wail, Flow,
  Horn, Nightwalker, or local).
- A burial place (separate, walled, frequently haunted by
  walkers — confused, not aggressive — until ghoulish cases arise).
- An authority (clan chief, communal speaker, noble, sacred,
  merchant council, oath-lord).
- A grudge structure (Heat and Need between three to five
  households or factions).

When writing a village, name at least three of these explicitly
in the first 200 words. Do not generalize them.

The model for novella-length village texture is **The Hollows**:
a settlement built on top of a war-graveyard, divided across a
bridge between Mrs. Pollmor's traditional faction (Dead Man's
Hand Inn) and Yawim the dwarf brewmaster's commerce faction
(Three Skulls Tavern), where ghosts are integrated into evening
custom and the Spring ritual is letting the well overflow.
See `places-villages-adventure-sites.md` for the full reference.

### "Soft Tolkienesque Poetic Worldbuilding"

"Soft" is the operative word. The setting borrows the Tolkien
inheritance — long-lived elves, lineage dwarves, half-kin, wandering
strangers, songs older than walls — but never the imperial register.

What is Tolkienesque:
- A song is older than the singer.
- A name in a story may belong to someone still alive — or to a
  ruby in a temple.
- Place is sacred. Ents walk. Stillelves drowse for a century in
  one grove. The Stillmist exists and cannot be found by looking.
- Rubies in elven chests; Huge as forge-god; Belderand under the
  mountain; the Stillmist beyond.

What is **not** Tolkienesque:
- No high kings. No prophesied returns. No clean good-vs-evil.
- No epic march of armies as the central plot mode.
- No quest as monomyth. The party is not chosen.
- No lyrical Common Tongue elevation. The narration stays close
  to mud, breath, and silver.

Use Tolkien's _texture_ (long memory, sacred place, half-kin
melancholy, sung lineage). Refuse Tolkien's _scale_ and his
imperial moral confidence.

## What Forbidden Lands Is Not

It is not D&D. It is not a heroic-fantasy campaign world. It is
not a comic adventure setting. Treat the following as failure modes
when classifying or pitching a scene:

- A "tavern with a quest board." Villages have inns; inns do not
  post jobs. Work is hired across a table by someone with a name
  and a problem.
- "The party are heroes who save the village." The party are
  outsiders. The village will accept, suspect, and likely outlast
  them.
- "Goblins ambush the road for no reason." Goblins ride wolves,
  steal, scout for orcs, and worship Eor. Their motive is always
  legible if you know goblins.
- "Find the magic sword and defeat the dark lord." Zytera is not
  a dark lord; he is a sorcerer with allies, enemies, demons, and
  a wall. Artifacts are dangerous and contested, not solutions.
- "Kill the monster, take the reward." See the Encounter
  Anti-Trope Catalog below.

## Encounter Anti-Trope Catalog

This catalog is the lore-side companion to the Encounter Design
Mandate in `skills/forbidden-lands-bestiary/SKILL.md`. The bestiary
skill defines the seven canonical encounter shapes (the Bargain,
the Unequal Witness, the Inherited Custom, the Wrong-Body Family,
the Failing Service, the Wrong Side of Mercy, the Old Pact). This
file lists the **tropes those shapes exist to refuse** and gives
the in-world reasons why those tropes are wrong.

### Anti-Tropes

**1. "The villagers ask the party to kill the monster."**

Wrong because: monsters are most often economically integrated.
The Abyss Worm under the Alderlander field has plowed the ground
through two winters when the valley starved; the village pays
in one elderly villager each spring. The dragon takes eleven sheep
a season because a grandfather signed for it. The Gray Bear takes
one child a winter because a widow believes it is her dead
children it comes for. The troll at the crossroads has been fed
weekly for nine years by a dwarven merchant who just died. **Killing
the monster always breaks the contract that was keeping something
worse at bay.**

The right shape: the village asks the party to manage the contract,
witness the next exchange, find a substitute, or kill someone _other_
than the monster.

**2. "The undead are evil and must be put down."**

Wrong because: walkers are confused dead, treated with respectful
courtesy, soothed with music. Ghosts are pinned by an unfinished
thing — purging requires answering the wrong, not destroying the
spirit. The grandmother who milks the goats at dawn has done so
for eight years; her grandson hides behind the stool to listen
to her hum. The Rust Brothers are the ones who weaponize the dead.

The right shape: the choice is whether to call the purger, whether
to mend the wrong, whether to burn the millhouse with the father
inside (the seventeen-year-old daughter's plan), whether to leave
the cold room rented.

**3. "Demons are summoned by evil cultists; defeat them."**

Wrong because: demons are bound by old contracts — the small demon
in the village oak has held back the child-fever for nineteen years.
The fisherman who paid for his son's life with the demon's right
to take him back when the boy's beard came in cannot now raise
a knife to his own son; the demon keeps his hands steady. The
contract was always the load-bearing thing.

The right shape: the party stands in the contract-breaking
moment. Cutting the oak releases the fever. Killing the son ends
the debt. Neither side is innocent.

**4. "Bandits ambush the road; kill them and take their gold."**

Wrong because: most bandits in the Forbidden Lands are starving
ex-soldiers, dispossessed villagers, or refugees from a burned
hamlet. Argam Twainbeard is a bandit chief who shares his camp
in trade for food and stories. The orcs called drifters are clan
cast-offs returning to the clan in a year. The "raiders" outside
Margelda are Galdane refugees from Aslene who lost their grass
to Zygofer's demons and dream of Ashen Vale.

The right shape: the party can fight, hire, feed, or refuse —
each choice is a position on a moral map, not a tactics question.

**5. "The orcs are mindless evil."**

Wrong because: orcs are run by the Viraga (female orcs functioning
as shadow government); the Roka clan is stoic and military and
hates magic; the Urhur clan crowns an emperor and trades for
purple shells with Saurians; Tregga is an orc bride marrying a
human named Robert. Khardnal is a Great Provoker who preaches
rage as awakening. The orcs were enslaved kin abandoned by their
elven and dwarven masters; their grievance is older than human
kingdoms here.

The right shape: orc encounters carry the same moral weight as
human ones. A single named orc is the unit, not a "band."

**6. "The Rust Brothers are the bad guys; the Raven Sisters are
the good guys."**

Wrong because: the Rust Brothers protect villages from demons,
heal under Heme, run the Iron Guard against Zytera's experiments,
and may be the only authority in a stretch of country. The Raven
Sisters murdered Alderlander missionaries during the Alder Wars
and bred the Reapenters fanatic sect that wants every human
out of Ravenland — dead or alive. The Vampyrs claim **the Rust
Brothers** are the corrupted ones and they themselves keep Rust's
true rite. Every faction is partly right.

The right shape: religious factions are political. A village pays
the Rust priory in tithes the same year a Raven Sister arrives
to purge a beloved family ghost; the party stands between, with
no clean side.

**7. "Find the artifact, win."**

Wrong because: every named artifact in the canon (see
`artifacts-and-named-relics.md`) carries a curse, a contested
provenance, or a binding that was load-bearing somewhere. Asina
cuts demons but is the smuggled fang of the worm-god the priests
threw the smuggler overboard for bringing. Tvedra's Twin Rings
shared the wounds of love until one wearer outbled the other.
Wail's Horn killed the army and choked the bard who blew it.
Scarnesbane was never used on Scarne, who is not dead, who is
imprisoned beneath Stonegarden.

The right shape: the artifact is the question, not the answer.

## Vignette and Novella Register

When writing **vignette and novella prose** for the manuscript
or its proposals, prioritize the following:

### Sensory Anchors (use first, before plot)

- **Smoke** rising from a roof, hanging low in still air, staining
  beards.
- **Bread** as substance and as gift. Bread root in Laak Varda.
  Hard bread. Stew. Broth. Beer.
- **Iron** as scarce, as prized, as taxed at gates, as old Alder
  War coin in a strongbox.
- **Wood and water** with audible weather: the mill wheel turning,
  the well rope creaking, rain on thatch.
- **Bone** in the ground: every village is built on something older.
- **Roses, alder, birch, oak, ash.** Specific trees, not "trees."
- **Crystal** as protective talisman in the eastern villages
  (Shardmaiden); rusty iron in the western villages (Rust Brothers).

### Voice Anchors

- Narration close to bodily experience. Cold hands. Wet boots.
  Salt in the food.
- Speech that is short, practical, and specific to the speaker's
  kin and trade. A miller speaks like a miller. A Quard rider
  does not speak like an Alderlander.
- A single proverb or song-fragment per scene, attributed to a
  named song or a named singer when possible (`gamemaster-tools`
  in Book of Beasts has a list).
- Time-anchors via the eight phases (Springrise → Winterwane) and
  named festivals (Awakening Day, Lushday, Harvest Day, Rotday,
  Midwinter, Midsummer). Not "spring."

### Plot Anchors (always present, not always foreground)

- **Need.** Someone needs grain, salt, iron, news, an heir, a
  burial, a witness, a body counted.
- **Heat.** Some household has been insulted, robbed, exposed,
  humiliated; the count is rising.
- **Old Pact.** Something — a demon, a beast, a ghost, a clan —
  has been kept in balance by a custom that is now breaking.
- **News from Outside.** A rider, a refugee, a Rust Brother
  collector, an Iron Guard patrol, a Galdane woman with her
  children, a half-elf scribe with a letter sealed in beeswax.

A vignette is finished when these four pressures have been
acknowledged, even if none is resolved.
