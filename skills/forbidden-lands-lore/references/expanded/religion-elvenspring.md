# Elvenspring Religions of the Ravenlands

Clay, the Shardmaiden, Wail, Flow, Raven worship, and land gods all enter
Elvenspring religious life. This reference holds theology, rites, religious
authority, sacred time, memorial practice, orders, land powers, and anathema.
Household, kinship, teaching, property, and mediation are in [Elvenspring
Culture](culture-elvenspring.md). Named powers, orders, and kin history are
manuscript facts. Detailed rites are grounded setting inference unless marked
as local practice.

---

## Clay and the Druidic Traditions

### The Shaper Who Does Not Rule

Clay shaped the world at the Protector's behest and holds craft, life, and the
magic of creation. Clay is sexless. Followers use titles drawn from the task at
hand: Maker at birth, Mender in healing, Potter in transformation, and Teacher
when knowledge passes between unlike beings.

Elves and Elvenspring tend to honor Clay as a master teacher rather than a
sovereign. The world was shaped, then left capable of further shaping. Life is
therefore neither untouched purity nor raw material without claims. A druid may
prune or cull, graft or heal, and sometimes move or kill, but must understand the
living relation altered by the act.

Clay doctrine holds that form carries memory. A scar shows adaptation. A path
shows repeated feet. A worked field holds human intention and the soil's answer.
The same dogma may make a careful steward or an arrogant one. Druids who believe
they read the true form of a place may ignore the people living there.

### Clay, Soil, and Maha

Clay worship uses unfired figures for conditions still open to change and fired
tablets for statements meant to endure. Maha pictograms do not converse. They
state something about reality or, through magic, press reality toward the
statement.

A household figure may receive a wound, seed, animal hair, or impression of a
missing tool. It is kept damp while treatment, search, or repair continues.
When the matter ends, the figure is returned to mud. Firing it would make the
temporary condition into a claim of permanence.

Druids test soil with fingers, smell, water, and plant response before ritual.
The ceremony follows diagnosis. Clay handled without knowledge is piety without
craft, which most orders distrust.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**Taking Form** marks adoption, apprenticeship, transformation, or a chosen
office. The person shapes a small vessel while witnesses name what the new form
must hold. The vessel remains deliberately imperfect. A sealed, flawless vessel
would imply no room for learning.

**Returning the Shape** accompanies death, release, and the end of a living
arrangement. An unfired likeness is softened in water and pressed into soil.
The rite returns fixed likeness to materials other lives can use without
claiming that the person vanishes.

**The Necessary Cut** authorizes culling. The druid names the population,
disease, hunger, or imbalance requiring death, then kills the selected animal
or plant cleanly and accounts for what will use the body. Witnesses may dispute
the diagnosis. They do not pretend that refusing every cut preserves life.

**The Maha Setting** fires a tablet only after its statement is tested aloud by
teachers and people affected by it. A mistaken mark can make error durable.
Some temples keep failed tablets in walls where students can see the cracks.

### Anathemas

Clay traditions condemn creation abandoned without provision, shaping a living
being merely to display power, fixing a temporary suffering into identity,
sterilizing useful soil without necessity, concealing a failed magical creation,
and writing Maha about a person or place one has refused to study.

Orders disagree over demonic alteration. Maidens tend to treat it as hostile
form imposed upon life. More contemplative druids ask whether altered life can
develop claims of its own. Neither position permits Zytera's coercion. Their
dispute concerns what is owed to the being that survives it.

## The Shardmaiden and the Orders

### Prophet, Division, and Shared Memory

The Shardmaiden was Clay's elven prophet. After Morander's death, she shattered
the ruby in her chest so the Maidens could carry her knowledge and power. The
act is understood as gift, wounding, multiplication, or continuing personhood,
depending on the order.

Her followers hold that knowledge may be divided without becoming ownerless.
Every bearer receives power and an obligation to the source. This gives the
orders a strong doctrine of stewardship. It also gives senior bearers a reason
to control access, travel, marriage, and dangerous inquiry.

The Order of Maidens directs the inheritance toward struggle against demonic
evil and Rust. The Golden Bough directs it toward the long health of forests,
creatures, and Stillelves. Local Elvenspring circles combine both and are
criticized by each.

### Rites and Practices

**Receiving the Shard** joins a Maiden to the order through an emerald fragment
fused into the forehead. The candidate fasts from cultivated food, states the
living things and people she accepts responsibility for, and receives the shard
before witnesses who must tend any failure of the joining.

**The Remembered Absence** honors Morander and other dead companions. A meal is
prepared with one necessary ingredient omitted. Participants name how the
missing thing changes every other taste. The rite teaches loss through the
body, not a speech.

**Walking the Bough** is a Golden Bough survey. Druids follow a set path through
forest and settlement. They record births and disease, predation and felling,
and changes in water or fire. The same route repeated over years lets change
become evidence. A missing fox litter or new charcoal pit matters more than an
omen invented at the shrine.

**The Green Judgment** hears accusations that a druidic act injured the land or
people under care. Non-druids may testify. Elves sometimes delay judgment long
enough that mortal claimants die; Elvenspring insist this is itself a verdict
made by power.

### Dogmas and Anathemas

The Shardmaiden's followers teach that power carried from another life remains
a debt, knowledge must be placed where it can oppose corruption, and care for
nature includes its hunger and violence. They do not promise harmlessness.

Anathemas include selling a shard, using its authority for private coercion,
destroying an elven ruby to distribute power, abandoning a creature changed by
one's own magic, falsifying a survey to protect an ally, and treating mortal
communities as weeds merely because they live quickly.

Some Maidens forbid intimate partnership that competes with the order. Others
cite the Shardmaiden's love for Morander as proof that attachment created the
gift. This disagreement has divided houses without producing a final doctrine.

## Wail

### Wind, Weather, and Unheld Things

Wail is the air goddess. Raven Sisters, elves, and Elvenspring honor her. So do
sailors and herders, millers and any household whose roof must survive a storm.
In Raven teaching she is the Protector's wife and bears birds through the sky.
Other traditions make her older than the human gods and indifferent to their
marriages.

Wind cannot be stored, fenced, or ordered to return. Wail governs weather and
breath without owning either. Rumor and migrating birds belong to the same
movement, as do scent and song carried beyond its singer. So do words sent where
the speaker cannot follow.
Her faithful distrust claims that something moving can be possessed merely
because it crossed one's land.

The myth of the sea holding Wail and the toothed fish throwing her free teaches
that rescue changes rescuer and rescued. The fish received size and air. A gift
from a god is rarely a restoration of the earlier state.

### Practice and Ceremony

People give Wail things light enough to move: wool fibers, chaff, feather down,
wood shavings, breath through a horn, and strips cut from worn cloth. Good cloth
is not hung casually. A visible prayer on a pass tells travelers that someone
paid material for the wind's attention.

**The Four Breaths** precedes a journey or message. The speaker faces each
direction, breathes once, and names where the road is open, blocked, desired,
and feared. The rite forces practical orientation before divine appeal.

**Roof Binding** is held before the worst weather season. Households repair
thatch and lashings together. The oldest sound binding is cut only after the
new one holds. A priest who blesses an unrepaired roof is mocked even by the
devout.

**Wail's Answer** follows dangerous stillness or sudden storm. People stop
speech long enough to hear loose shutters, trees, animals, water, and distant
horns. The first useful sound determines the work that begins. Interpretation
belongs to experience as much as priesthood.

### Anathemas

Wail's worshippers condemn killing a protected messenger, trapping birds for
ritual display until they die, taking a traveler's news while denying them
shelter, silencing a warning because it harms trade, and claiming ownership of
the windward path or migrating flock.

Whistling into a gale is forbidden in some valleys and required in others.
Both customs descend from the claim that Wail answers challenge. One community
wants her to hear courage; the other has buried the people who tried.

## Flow

### Water with Direction

Flow governs springs, rivers, lakes, cleansing, and water that moves with a
course. She named rivers so they could hold together and have will. Marshes,
left unnamed and undirected, fall outside much of her ordinary cult. Saurians
and goblins who cross such water know that a human prayer to Flow may be a poor
substitute for a pole.

Flow is honored by elves, Elvenspring, villages, and the Raven Church. Her faith
joins practical water care to purification. Cleanliness carries bodily
consequence as well as moral meaning. A fouled well kills. A blocked channel
floods seed. A spring controlled by one household can govern a village.

### Daily Faith

The first drawn water is inspected before it is offered. Smell, color, insects,
sediment, and the behavior of animals matter. Pouring bad water at a shrine does
not make it pure. It shows that the worshipper has failed to attend.

Households set aside different vessels for drinking, washing wounds, brewing,
and unclean work where resources permit. The distinction is preached as Flow's
order and maintained because people remember sickness.

Names of local streams are repeated to children with their dangerous seasons,
crossings, and ownership disputes. To know the sacred name without knowing the
spring flood is religious vanity.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**Flow's Delight** invokes the goddess in a well or bucket until water
overflows. Successful water is used to rinse people and structures and kept for
its healing power. The rite also reveals whether a settlement can spare water
for cleansing. During drought, performance may become a political accusation.

**Naming the Course** recognizes a new channel, reopened spring, or altered
ford. People walk as much of the water as they can, note where it enters and
leaves cultivated land, and state the rights attached to it. The name is given
only after the route is known.

**The Shared Cup** settles temporary peace at water. Enemies drink from one
vessel filled by a neutral hand. Violence before both have left the bank is an
offense against Flow and the witness. The protection ends at the stated marker,
which everyone inspects before drinking.

**Returning What Washed** disposes of water used on corpse, disease, or
dangerous magic. It is poured where it cannot return immediately to drinking
water. The officiant names what the water carried. This is sanitation given
memory.

### Anathemas

Flow's cults condemn poisoning a source, hiding upstream death or disease,
altering a channel without warning people below, denying necessary drinking
water during protected parley, and giving a false name to water so an old right
can be evaded.

Dam building creates hard cases. A dwarf may call a dam sound work, a farmer
may call it life, and a Flow keeper may call the drowned course an imprisoned
will. The dispute should be decided through water, labor, and consequence, not
by assuming one god has sole jurisdiction.

## A Religion Measured between Lifetimes

Elvenspring can remember several human generations and still know that an elf
may outlive every body of their line. Their religion cannot borrow human urgency
or elven patience without cost. Every vow is heard against those unequal
lifetimes.

A promise to guard a place “until the danger passes” may consume an Elvenspring
life and remain brief to the elf who requested it. A human office passed from
parent to child may look unstable to an Elvenspring who personally remembers
the founding bargain. These differences make Elvenspring valuable witnesses
and interested parties.

At **Setting the Term**, a vow names one human measure, one Elvenspring measure,
and one observable condition. “Ten harvests, or until the youngest keeper takes
adult standing, or until no taint appears in the spring.” The measures prevent
one kin's sense of time from owning the agreement.

## Religious Authority

Authority comes from druidic training, shard-bearing, care of a place, human
office, Raven witness, magical competence, and family relation to elves. No
single rank joins these claims.

The Order of Maidens gives disciplined office and a share of the Shardmaiden's
power. Golden Bough circles give ecological memory and relations with Stillelves.
Village druids may hold neither status and still know every birth, field, spring,
and dangerous ruin within a day's walk.

An Elvenspring can be treated as an interpreter by both sides and trusted by
neither. Humans expect access to elven decisions. Elves expect patience and
obedience from shorter-lived kin. Religious authority often begins with refusing
to make one party's demand sound natural in the other's language.

## Death and the Three-Part Memorial

Elvenspring death is final in a way elven bodily death is not. Elven relatives
may understand the fact and still behave as though restoration, delay, or a
longer search should be possible.

The three-part memorial places clay for work completed, feather for public
promises, and seed or green branch for living duties. Each part goes to a
different keeper. Concentrating them in one hand lets that person control the
dead person's whole account.

At **The Mortal Name**, an elf who knew the dead must speak one act bounded by
time: the last visit, missed meeting, delayed answer, or promise made too late.
The rite prevents praise of long friendship from erasing mortal absence.

The body may be buried, burned, returned through Clay, or treated under local
human custom. The order matters less than settling magical tools, shards,
Maha tablets, patients, apprentices, and land duties before an elder claimant
absorbs them.

## Frailers

Frailers pursue Blood Magic, Death Magic, power, and political independence.
Their enemies describe them as Elvenspring impatience made corrupt. Frailers
answer that long-lived orders hoard power until mortal needs have passed.

Their religious practice often treats one life as the only honest measure.
Blood proves a cost paid now. The dead are witnesses who cannot be postponed
through elven procedure. The doctrine can expose complacency. It can also
justify using bodies, descendants, or spirits as resources.

A Frailer household may keep no unfired figures. Claims are written, scarred,
buried, or bound into durable objects. Reversibility is treated as weakness.
Defectors often leave because every mistake has been made too permanent to
repair.

## The Orders and Their Disputes

### Order of Maidens

Maidens define duty through active opposition to demons, Rust, and hostile
shaping. Their shards provide power and shared memory. Seniority can turn the
Shardmaiden's sacrifice into a claim over each bearer.

### Golden Bough

Golden Bough circles measure forest health, predation, water, birth, felling,
and fire. Their long view catches slow harm. It can miss the human household
that needs wood before winter.

### Village Druids

Village druids combine Clay, Flow, Wail, Raven custom, and local land powers.
Orders accuse them of impurity. Villagers judge whether the well remains clean,
the birth attended, the weather warning given, and the dead named.

### Independent Shard-Bearers

Some bear fragments outside formal orders. Their power may come with incomplete
memory and no institution willing to answer for its use. They can act quickly
where orders deliberate and disappear when consequences arrive.

## Land Powers and the Places between Churches

Elvenspring venerate local powers that have no order, scripture, or agreed body.
A hill that turns wind, a spring used in difficult births, a stand of trees that
survived the Mist, or an animal returning at the same season may receive duty
before anyone decides whether a god inhabits it.

At **Learning the Place**, a newcomer spends one season doing the work attached
to the site before offering interpretation. They clear the spring, watch the
flood, tend the path, or count the returning animals. A priest who names the
spirit on arrival has described their own education.

Offerings answer use. Water is carried away from a flooded path. A cut tree is
replaced where it can live. The first fruit may be left for seed or animals. A
gift that makes the place poorer in order to impress it is regarded as confused.

Local powers create narrow jurisdictions. A ford-keeper may settle passage and
have no standing over marriage. A birth spring may protect a laboring stranger
without giving its keeper a claim to the child. Human churches often attempt to
join these small offices under one priest. Druidic orders may do the same under
one survey.

When a place changes beyond recognition, worship can end. At **Releasing the
Site**, keepers name what relation has ceased, remove objects still useful to the
living, and leave one mark of the old practice. Continuing every rite after the
spring is dry turns memory into a claim on absent water.

## Sacred Time and the Three Calendars

Elvenspring households live by mortal work, druidic season, and elven return.
The first counts days until sowing or rent. The second follows growth, migration,
water, and culling. The third may wait for a star, remembered visitor, or body
restored after decades.

At **Laying the Three Measures**, the household places a perishable object, a
seasonal object, and a durable mark together. Each receives one present duty.
When the first decays, its work must be done or reassigned. The tablet cannot be
used to postpone the bread.

Orders keep long surveys. Villages keep short weather and food accounts. An
Elvenspring mediator compares them at each seasonal turn. The work catches slow
soil loss that a farmer cannot see and immediate hunger that a century map does
not show.

Birthdays carry more weight among Elvenspring than elves because aging cannot
be restored. Some orders disdain the count as human vanity. Families answer that
the body has kept a true account even if an immortal teacher finds the number
small.

The Red Wanderer's passage may be marked with elven kin. The Elvenspring adds
the names of people who died since the last observation. An elf may remember the
prior sky. The list records what changed beneath it.

## Fault, Repair, and Anathema

Repair must fit the injured party's time. Replanting a tree is insufficient if
the human claimant needed its fruit this year. Immediate silver is insufficient
if the damaged spring will sicken descendants.

Grave breaches include delaying mortal need until the claimant dies, fixing a
temporary condition into permanent form for convenience, using an elven relation
to overrule local witnesses, abandoning a being altered through one's magic,
falsifying a long survey, and spending a short-lived ally on a danger an elf
could have waited out safely.

Frailer excess adds other breaches: binding the dead without release, treating
bloodline as stored magical material, and making irreversible choices for
people whose consent was obtained under immediate need.

## Evidence at the Table

At the table, show Elvenspring belief through damp clay beside a fired tablet, a
vow carrying three different time limits, an elf's delayed answer arriving after
a funeral, or a Maiden whose demon hunt left a village druid maintaining every
ordinary duty she set aside.

Religious conflict should concern time, custody, mixed authority, the cost of
change, and who bears the interval between an immortal decision and a mortal
need.
