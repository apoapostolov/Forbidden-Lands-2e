# Ravenlands Economy, Exchange, and Stronghold Life

**Category:** Generic expanded Ravenlands lore for Apostol Apostolov's
campaign. This reference combines manuscript rules and setting facts with
grounded pre-industrial economic models and setting inference.

Food comes first: who raises or finds it, who preserves it, and who keeps the
store. Barter and old silver follow from those facts. So do the few inhabited
shells of lost towns. The later sections treat an adventurers' stronghold as a
working community. AI and Game Masters can use the reference to decide what
people do, what a place can support, why a price changes, and who controls the
store. Smell and noise reveal whether a stronghold function is in use.

The rules for prices and function costs remain in Books 01 and 02. Those books
also govern yields, staffing, construction, and stronghold battles. The
material below explains the lived world around those rules. It adds no
compulsory maintenance rolls.

## Authority and Economic Method

### Four Kinds of Claim

- **Manuscript fact:** The Blood Mist isolated settlements for roughly three
  centuries. It lifted in 1160 AS. Roads, trade, and territorial authority are
  only beginning to recover in 1165 AS. Silver and copper circulate, stronghold
  functions produce defined resources, and towns are uncommon.
- **Established campaign lore:** Ravenland has no known operating silver mine.
  Its silver is old Alder War money. Coins return to circulation worn or
  clipped. Others are hoarded, lost, and found again. Apostol Apostolov's
  campaign treats this as the ordinary source of circulating silver.
- **Historical model:** Household production and mixed subsistence answer most
  needs close to home. Witnessed measures and payment in kind support exchange.
  Reciprocal labor and seasonal markets extend it. Stores remain under personal
  authority.
- **Setting inference:** Ten generations without dependable travel make local
  measures and household memory unusually strong. Local debts and salvage
  rights depend upon both. Five years of open roads have made strangers and
  tolls disruptive. Portable coin and distant market information disturb old
  bargains as well.

The settlement tables can produce a silver mine. In Ravenland, treat that
result as an exhausted old working or false assay. A lost shaft might instead
be a discovery important enough to alter regional politics. A new producing
silver mine is an authorial decision, not ordinary scenery.

### The Economic Constraint Chain

Build economic detail through this sequence:

`land and season → work → yield → preservation → control → exchange → obligation`

A field without a mill produces grain that is slow to eat. A mill without a
road may feed one valley but cannot create a distant trade. A road without
guards brings tax collectors and raiders as readily as buyers. A full granary
whose key belongs to one household is food, authority, and a reason for three
other households to count sacks through the wall.

### Ordinary Competence

People who survived the Blood Mist mend fences and dry damp grain without
thinking themselves exceptional. They salt fish, keep firewood off the ground,
and judge whether a thin animal will live. Familiar land usually yields one more
meal. Summarize such work until weather or missing tools change a choice. Bring
it forward when labor is absent, storage has failed, or ownership is disputed.

## An Economy Measured in Winter

### The Household Is the First Treasury

Count the mouths eating from one store and the hands answerable for it. That is
the working unit: the household, not the solitary wage earner. Animals and
rights to land make those hands more productive, provided the household has
tools, seed, and stored food. Everyone who eats from the store shares its
reputation and liability. Wealth may lie in a sound roof or ploughshare, in two
daughters old enough to keep sheep, or in six sealed grain jars. The remembered
fact that the miller's father owes help at harvest belongs to the household as
well.

Very little household wealth is idle. The axe preserves next winter's heat.
The cow turns summer grass into milk and dung. Calves follow. Hide and meat come
later. A bronze
cauldron saves fuel because it hangs properly over the fire. Selling any of
these for silver may solve today's debt by cutting into next season's work.

### Stores Matter More Than Annual Yield

A good harvest can still end in hunger. Grain heats and molds if stored damp.
Mice enter through a gap that a tired household meant to close tomorrow. Salt
runs out before the autumn slaughter. A levy takes the dry sacks and leaves the
green ones. Ravenland wealth is therefore counted by usable stores:

- seed grain that no one should eat
- eating grain and flour
- salt and smoke for preservation, with sound vessels and cool space
- fodder and sheltered space for winter stock
- dry firewood, charcoal, or peat
- sound iron edges and spare handles; cord, leather, and nails
- labor owed and labor still healthy enough to answer

The four settlement steps, **Bare**, **Thin**, **Adequate**, and **Full**, have
physical signs. Bare means the bin can be swept. Thin means someone has begun
measuring portions in private. Adequate means the next season can be met if
nothing serious happens. Full leaves enough to trade or lend. It can support a
feast, attract a tax, or reward a thief.

### The Work Year

Economic time follows the eight phases rather than an abstract annual account.

- **Springrise:** Repair fences and roofs. Inspect seed. Clear drainage. Births
  among animals decide which households need help at night.
- **Springwane:** Plough and sow. Move stock onto grazing. Fish runs, greens,
  eggs, and the first milk loosen the last hunger of winter.
- **Summerrise:** Weed, hay, build, quarry, cut timber, burn charcoal, travel,
  and repair roads. Daylight is long. So is the list of work.
- **Summerwane:** Store hay and firewood. Shear, preserve, brew, and finish work
  that must dry before rain. Traders move while tracks still hold.
- **Fallrise:** Harvest grain, flax, fruit, roots, and nuts. Every able hand is
  pulled toward the fields and stores.
- **Fallwane:** Thresh, mill, salt, smoke, slaughter surplus stock, mend roofs,
  and decide what can be spared for tribute or market.
- **Winterrise:** Indoor craft takes over. Spinning, weaving, leatherwork,
  tool repair, tallying, teaching, and dispute fill the dark.
- **Winterwane:** Fodder thins. Fuel piles sink. Animals and people show which
  calculations were wrong.

Construction competes with this calendar. A wall raised during harvest costs
grain even if the stone was free, because its builders were absent from the
fields. A lord who calls labor at the wrong time may finish the wall and empty
the barns.

### Three Economic Distances

**Within call:** Households exchange small food gifts and remember value rather
than price. They share tools, watch children, and help with grazing or harvest.

**Within a day's return:** Marriage kin and neighboring settlements remain
close enough for repeated contact. A mill, smith, ferry, or shrine draws the
route between them. Seasonal pasture and fishing stations extend the same web.
Reputation can still enforce agreements.

**Beyond a safe return:** Strangers and caravans use old roads or river traffic.
Distant priests, mercenaries, and rare crafts belong to this range. Portable
silver becomes useful. Hostages or written marks support trust. Witnesses,
advance payment, and guarded cargo support it further.

The Blood Mist almost destroyed the third distance. Its return is the great
economic disturbance of the present day.

## From Foraging Ground to Ruin-Town

These forms overlap. A farmstead hunts. A fishing village keeps gardens. A
ruin-town grazes goats between fallen walls. Economic scale is a question of
how many kinds of work can be coordinated and how far food can be moved before
it spoils.

### Hunters, Gatherers, and Seasonal Camps

A trap line and a sedge bed can be held as firmly in memory as a fenced field.
A group knows where elk cross after the first frost, which sedge beds hold bread
root, when a fish weir must be repaired, where hazel bears well, and which bog
island stays dry. Knowledge of a place is property even when no fence marks it.

Large game brings feast and risk. Small game, nets, traps, fish, shellfish,
roots, berries, nuts, greens, fungi, honey, and insects provide steadier return.
The decisive crafts come after the catch: clean butchery, drying, smoking,
salting, rendering fat, scraping hides, keeping vermin out, and carrying food
home before it turns.

Seasonal camps often belong to a settled community. A household leaves for a
fish run, summer grazing, nut harvest, charcoal burn, or trapping round and
returns to its winter houses. Wolfkin packs, goblin bands, Saurian communities,
forest steads, and human fringe households may rely on wild food in different
proportions. None should be reduced to the same generic hunter culture.

What outsiders call empty forest may already be divided by traps, remembered
paths, burial trees, berry grounds, and agreements with creatures that live
there. Cutting a road through it can be an act of seizure without felling a
single inhabited house.

### The Isolated Farmstead

A farmstead is one or several related households holding enough field, pasture,
wood, and water to survive most years. It mixes grain or roots with animals,
gardens, gathering, and occasional hunting. Complete self-sufficiency is rare.
The stead still needs salt, replacement iron, breeding stock, ritual service,
marriage partners, and help when fire or illness removes several workers at
once.

The stead's economy sounds small: flails on a threshing floor, sheep against a
fold, a hand-mill after dark. Its risks are concentrated. One broken plough at
sowing or one injured adult at harvest can damage the entire year. Hospitality
is partly insurance. A stranger fed today may become the only armed witness
available tomorrow.

### Hamlet and Village

A hamlet coordinates work that one household cannot sustain. A village can
keep a mill, smith, shrine, communal store, ferry, palisade, or specialist
craft. Shared work saves labor and binds households to whoever controls it. The
mill saves days of hand grinding, so its keeper can demand a share. A common
bull improves stock, so its owner has a voice in breeding arrangements. A well
saves labor, so poisoning or monopolizing it threatens every roof.

Village exchange runs through recurring relationships. The carpenter repairs a
door now because the household will help lift his roof beam later. A widow's
field is harvested because her dead husband worked other fields. People also
cheat, delay, deny, and reinterpret these claims. The village is a coalition of
households with a long memory, not one generous family.

### The Ravenland Town

A town is unusual in 1165 AS. Most apparent towns are a few hundred people
occupying a defensible fraction of a settlement built for thousands before the
Mist. Houses lean against old public walls. A former avenue holds pens, gardens,
and dung heaps. Roof tiles are taken from three empty buildings to keep one
inhabited hall dry. The gate may be sound while two quarters behind it have
returned to scrub.

Decay itself has economic uses. Ruins are the town's quarry, timber store,
shelter, grave problem, and disputed inheritance. Salvage needs rules. One household
claims the iron clamps in a fallen hall because its grandparents guarded that
street. Another claims anything without a roof. A third says the dead beneath
the floor have never surrendered it.

Town life requires a wider food country. Grain, fish, fodder, fuel, timber,
clay, hides, and animals enter from outside the occupied walls. Crafts, market
access, protection, judgment, worship, storage, and unusual goods flow back.
Close that road or river for a season and the town begins taking its abandoned
quarters apart faster than before.

### When a Settlement Grows

Growth is visible before it is named. Tents remain after market day. A baker
buys flour instead of grain. Someone builds a second smithy. Dung is carried
farther. The graveyard wall is extended. Neighbors argue over the road edge.
Only later does a hall begin calling the place a town.

Growth also creates people who do not directly produce food: guards, scribes,
priests, merchants, specialized artisans, performers, prisoners, and rulers.
They live from surplus gathered by rent, wage, offering, tax, trade margin, or
force. A settlement that cannot explain how these mouths are fed is living on
stores, plunder, or a future promise.

## The Economic Geography of Ravenland

Each place below shows goods, labor, or authority concentrating for a different
reason. Other roads and exchanges remain beyond this list.

### Western Fortresses and Tribute Roads

**Alderstone** is the closest Ravenland comes to a large fortified town, but it
occupies the ruins of a greater city. Its economic reach depends on armed
roads, requisition, salvage, workshops, and the demands of the powers gathered
there. Its walls draw food inward and fear outward.

**Haggler's House** turns religion into a seasonal economy. Harvest feasts,
blessing, tribute, pilgrims, camps, guards, animals, and offerings concentrate
goods that villages cannot easily refuse to surrender. The road to a holy
place is also a tax road.

**Weatherstone** shows a common smaller pattern: a defensible tower with a
village economy attached. The tower lends protection and status. The fields,
herds, water, and workers below keep the tower inhabited.

**Ravenhole** and the **Stoneloom Mines** show what happens when extraction
ends. A quarry or mine leaves worked roads, spoil, chambers, tools worth
salvaging, and a space other beings can occupy. An abandoned work may remain an
economic landmark generations after its last useful stone was cut.

### Central Crossings and Mixed Markets

**The Hollows** lives from divided authority around a bridge. Its inns and
brewers serve trade moving through old graves, while goats and local crafts
support households between market days. New silver enters through Yawim's
commercial ties. Old households answer with custom, hospitality, and claims
that silver cannot settle cleanly.

**Moldena** is an old town on a trade route with dwarven presence. Such places
make natural exchange points for stonework, metal, food, lodging, animals, and
news. Their importance rises faster than their population when roads reopen.

**Margelda** and the Galdane presence place herd wealth beside settled claims.
Horses, pasture, fodder, leather, transport, and martial service can be more
important than grain or coin. A ploughed field and a grazing route may each be
necessary and still occupy the same ground.

### Eastern Stone, Horses, Learning, and Sea

**Stonegarden**, **Belderand**, and **Scarnehall** draw food and surface goods
toward dwarven craft centers and send worked stone, metalwork, judgment, and
prestige outward. Stonegarden's gathering every second year is also a great
bazaar. Work displayed there carries clan name, technical proof, religious
meaning, and a price that may be paid years later in access or marriage.

**Pelagia** joins fishing harbor, healing house, pottery, sanctuary, and coastal
traffic. Fish and clay support daily life. Healing, ritual access, safe harbor,
and druidic knowledge bring visitors whose payment may arrive as gifts,
service, rare goods, or protection.

**Farhaven** has a coastal town and library. Books need dry rooms, scribes,
patrons, lamp fuel, skins or paper, food for non-farming specialists, and some
authority capable of keeping a collection from being burned or divided.

**Agurville**, below Amber's Peak, gathers forge work, horses, fishing, bridge
traffic, and two rider populations. Its prices should react sharply to military
demand, pilgrim arrivals, grazing tension, and the safety of the bridge.

### Northern Water and Forest Exchange

**Lake Varda** supports bread-root harvest and exchange with Saurians. Its
wealth is seasonal, wet, and difficult to measure in sacks of grain. Rights to
cut root and approach the water matter as much as ownership of dry land.

**The Blush** joins mussels, salt water, river movement, Saurian platform trade,
and access to the Eastern Sea. Barges and boats can carry burdens that would
consume a line of carts, but only along water that leads somewhere useful.

**Yendra**, **Belifar**, and **Brodelmarg** belong to the sparse coastal chain.
Fish, salt, rope, boats, pottery, timber, and inland grain create reasons to
move despite storms and sea-serpents. A boat lost here can remove both a
household's food source and its transport.

**Gargan Marshes** restrict trade to known edges and platform stations. Goods
move by barge, and local permission is worth more than a map. The marsh is not
unowned because settled farmers cannot plough it.

### Places Outside Ordinary Exchange

**Stillmist** cannot be treated as an open market simply because elven goods
would command high prices. Access, relationship, memory, and permission are the
scarce goods.

**Grindbone** is a market built around coercion, theft, milling, brewing, and
captivity. People are priced there. Its trade remains coercive and morally
corrupt: every cheap product may conceal stolen labor, and every skilled
stranger has reason to fear being appraised.

## Barter, Obligation, and Old Silver

### Barter Is Rarely a Single Swap

A household gives grain now and receives help with a roof later. Most exchange
is delayed in this way. A single direct barter works only when both sides want
what the other has at the same time. A smith repairs a plough because the
farmer supplies charcoal, food, and a son to work the bellows. An innkeeper
accepts a pelt from a known hunter because the tanner will take it after market
day.

Such exchange needs memory and witnesses. People remember quantity, quality,
urgency, and conduct. A sack given in famine is not equal to a sack returned
after harvest. A day's work performed in rain is not equal to a leisurely day
promised for winter. The disagreement becomes Heat because no neutral price can
settle what the exchange meant.

### What Commonly Changes Hands

- food, seed, salt, fodder, firewood, charcoal, wax, oil, and drink
- wool, cloth, hide, leather, cord, baskets, pots, barrels, and timber
- tool use, draft animals, breeding animals, boats, carts, and storage space
- harvest, herding, watch, building, transport, healing, burial, and craft work
- hospitality, safe passage, introduction, witness, news, and silence
- future labor, marriage arrangements, fosterage, oath, and protection

These things are not interchangeable in every place. Grain may be refused by a
household with no dry storage. A cow may be ruinous to someone with no winter
fodder. A silver coin may be less useful than three days with a plough team
when Springwane is ending.

### Why Silver Matters

Silver settles with strangers. It survives travel, divides into smaller sums,
stores value without eating fodder, and lets a person carry the proceeds of one
trade toward a place where nobody knows the original debtor. That makes it
especially useful for wages, tolls, fines, mercenaries, distant craft, tribute,
and caravan goods.

Silver also conceals relationship. A meal paid in coin may create no claim on
the guest tomorrow. This can be freedom or insult. A host who expected an
obligation may hear the coin placed on the table as a refusal of kinship.

### The Old Coins

Most Ravenland silver predates the Blood Mist or descends from caches made
before and during the Alder Wars. The coins bear rulers, beasts, temples, and
inscriptions no living human administration reliably guarantees. Faces wear
flat. Edges are clipped. Some pieces have been cut to make smaller payments.
Others have plugs, solder, or suspicious color beneath the surface.

People therefore judge three things:

1. **Metal:** Does it ring, bend, cut, and weigh like accepted silver?
2. **Form:** Is the stamp known locally, and has the edge been altered?
3. **Source:** Who offers it, and who will take it afterward?

A scale, touchstone, file mark, familiar stamp, or trusted money-weigher can
matter more than the dead face on the coin. Villages without scales compare a
piece to known coins kept by the headwoman, smith, shrine, or innkeeper.

### Circulation Without a Mint

Old silver does not vanish after one purchase. A Rust tithe carries village
coins to a priory. The priory pays guards and buys iron. A guard loses silver at
an inn. The innkeeper buys salt from a coastal hawker. The hawker pays a bridge
toll and brings the same clipped piece into another valley.

Newly opened ruins release caches. Adventurers are therefore an erratic source
of liquidity. Their silver raises prices, funds repairs, pays old debts, and
attracts people who want to tax, steal, counterfeit, or sell to them. A hoard
spent in a village does not make the village permanently wealthy if the grain,
tools, animals, and labor needed for survival are absent.

### Copper and Gold

Copper handles small daily reckonings where it circulates. It is still old or
imported metal and may be counted, weighed, or bundled according to local
practice.

Gold exists in Ravenland, but a gold piece is difficult to spend in ordinary
life. Few people can make change, test it confidently, or afford to hold so much
value in one object. Gold belongs to hoards, prestige gifts, foreign bargains,
and purchases large enough to require several witnesses. A farmer offered gold
for a goat may assume the buyer is a fool, a counterfeiter, or someone who
expects the goat and the farm.

### Cultural Tendencies

**Alderlander practice** favors written tallies, named prices, tolls, fines,
measures, and explicit debt. The paper never enforces itself. Witnesses, armed
authority, and repeated access give it force.

**Aslene practice** often reckons wealth through horses, herds, riding gear,
hospitality, and obligation. A silver offer can be exact and still misunderstand
the standing carried by a gift animal.

**Ailander practice** relies more heavily on barter, household memory, and
future obligation. The account is carried in people rather than gathered into
one book.

Non-human kin apply their own measures of time, memory, craft, territory, and
honor. Dwarven work may be priced partly by proof and clan standing. Elven
exchange may concern restoration or custody over spans no human debtor can
personally answer. Goblin claims may follow possession, risk, and moon-travel.
Never make one human village custom the economy of every kin.

## Markets, Roads, and Moving Goods

### A Market Is a Time and a Guarantee

A market can be a row of stalls, a cleared yard, a bridge approach, or the open
space before a shrine. For the hours it remains open, people accept the same
peace: weapons stay bound, witnesses hear challenges to measures, stolen goods
can be identified, and outsiders know whom to approach. The authority that
keeps this peace earns fees, reputation, and enemies.

Market day gathers what is otherwise dispersed. Farmers bring a small surplus.
Craftspeople take orders. Hawkers compare shortages. Priests collect. Suitors
look. Thieves inspect knots and purses. News changes prices before all the
stalls are raised.

### Geography Makes the Price

Grain is cheap where fields yielded and dear across a marsh. Timber is heavy
and profitable only when the buyer lacks nearby trees or desperately needs a
wall. Salt holds value because it preserves other value. Iron tools command a
premium where a forge is absent. Fine pottery may be nearly worthless after a
rough mountain crossing has broken half the load.

The price includes wages, fodder, toll, spoiled goods, lost time, guard pay,
weather, breakage, and the chance that no buyer appears. A road that halves
travel difficulty can change every price along it without creating a single new
resource.

### Rivers, Roads, and Pack Trails

Pack animals reach places carts cannot. Carts move more on good ground. Wagons
need roads, bridges, turning space, repair, and fodder. Barges carry bulk loads
cheaply but cannot leave their water and may return upstream slowly or empty.

The first renewed routes are chains of personal trust. A trader knows the
ferryman, the inn yard, the spring that does not foul, the household that sells
fodder, and the settlement where armed strangers must wait outside. A line on a
map without these relations is only a proposed journey.

### The Reopening Trade

Five years after the Mist, market information is valuable and perishable.
“They need salt” may be true, may have been true last season, or may have been
spread by someone with salt to sell. A caravan that arrives second can turn a
profitable shortage into an unsold load.

Trade changes settlements even when it succeeds. Imported iron weakens a local
smith's hold over repairs. Cheap grain lets a ruler keep more guards. A road
brings marriage partners and plague. A warehouse lets one merchant wait while
hungry sellers accept low prices.

## Surplus, Storage, and Production Chains

### Surplus Is a Chain, Not a Heap

Resources become wealth only when the next link exists.

- Grain needs threshing, dry storage, milling, fuel, and baking.
- Milk needs immediate use or a dairy that turns it into longer-keeping food.
- Meat needs clean butchery, salt or smoke, vessels, cool storage, and water
  for those who eat it heavily salted.
- Wool needs washing, sorting, spinning, weaving, fulling, dye, cutting, and
  sewing.
- Hide must be cleaned before it spoils, then worked with tannin and water. The
  process demands time, skilled hands, and a foul space away from ordinary work.
- Ore needs timber supports, miners, transport, fuel, furnace, forge, and smith.
- Timber needs access, felling, hauling, seasoning or green-wood knowledge,
  sawing, joinery, and protection from damp.
- Clay needs digging, temper, shaping, drying, fuel, and a firing that may ruin
  the whole batch.

The most valuable stronghold improvement is often the missing middle. The herd
sets the milk yield. A dairy stops today's milk from becoming tonight's loss.
The harvest sets the grain yield. A granary keeps that harvest alive until it
is needed.

### Who Owns the Surplus

Separate stores create separate authority:

- **Household stores** answer first to the people under that roof.
- **Common stores** are gathered for famine, seed, defense, feast, or ritual.
- **Function stores** keep a mill, forge, inn, stable, or temple working.
- **Trade stores** wait for a buyer and may be withheld during local need.
- **The lord's store or vault** pays guards, tribute, gifts, and private plans.

Conflict begins when one category is silently treated as another. Taking seed
grain as wages, selling common salt through a private shop, or using temple
offerings to buy weapons may be practical and still break the settlement.

### Waste Returns Somewhere

Ash enriches soil or enters lye work. Dung feeds fields, dries as fuel, attracts
flies, and marks how many animals a place supports. Bones become handles, glue,
needles, gaming pieces, or grave matter. Whey feeds pigs. Bark supplies tannin.
Scrap iron is saved. Broken stone fills drains. Human filth must be moved away
from wells and work areas.

A productive settlement is loud and dirty. Cleanliness means labor has moved
the dirt somewhere, often toward poorer ground or people with less authority.

## A Stronghold Becomes a Community

### From Prize to Home

An adventurer sees an empty tower as walls, a roof, and a defensible door. A
resident sees water distance, smoke draw, sleeping space, vermin, graves,
grazing, fuel, and whether children can cross the yard without passing armed
strangers. Cleaning an old site establishes shelter. Repeated work makes it a
home.

The first dependable fire, well, store, privy arrangement, and locked door
matter more to daily life than the first monument. The first winter decides
whether the settlement was founded or merely occupied.

### Hirelings and Residents

A hireling sells work for coin or agreed goods. The relationship can end when
pay ends. A resident moves a household, places tools and dependents under the
stronghold's protection, and begins acquiring claims on its future. Food,
housing, judgment, worship, safety, and respect become part of the bargain even
when only wages appear on a sheet.

Residents do not all work at one function. Children carry, glean, scare birds,
and learn. Elders mind fires, sort, mend, teach, count, and remember. Adults
lose days to sickness, birth, burial, animal trouble, roof repair, and disputes.
The rules abstract this human margin. The fiction should remember it.

### Community Work

Some work is gathered because no household can do it alone:

- raising frames and setting roof beams
- clearing and draining roads
- digging wells, ditches, graves, and foundations
- harvesting before weather turns
- repairing palisades after storm or attack
- hauling millstones, gate timbers, and forge stone
- fighting fire, flood, vermin, and sickness
- keeping watch and searching for missing people or animals

The call to common work reveals authority. Who can call it? Who provides food?
Who is exempt? Who brings tools? Who bears injury? Does labor discharge tax, or
does the lord claim both? A wall remembers these answers long after the fresh
wood has greyed.

### Accumulating Resources

A stronghold grows by keeping part of each good season from immediate use.
Wood is stacked and seasoned. Stone is sorted near the next work. Iron is
counted under lock. Seed is sealed. Hay is kept off damp ground. Coin is divided
between wages, purchase, reserve, and display.

Accumulation needs three protections:

1. **Physical protection:** Roof, floor, drainage, container, wall, lock, and
   guard.
2. **Administrative protection:** Tally, key, witness, measure, and authority to
   refuse access.
3. **Social protection:** Enough legitimacy that hungry residents do not regard
   the store as stolen from them.

The third is the least visible and the hardest to repair.

### A Weekly Stronghold Rhythm

The daily attention and weekly CRAFTING check required for upkeep cover many
small acts: clearing gutters, replacing pegs, sweeping chimneys, checking
hinges, turning stores, patching roofs, scouring vessels, carrying ash, watching
for rot, and listening for water where no water should be. A HANDYMAN does not
perform all of this alone. The handyman notices, assigns, reminds, and finishes
what everyone else hoped would wait.

When upkeep fails, use one physical sign before applying a consequence. A roof
drips onto flour. Rats leave black grains beside the candles. A gate binds in
its socket. The wall makes a sound at night that the mason hears and the lord
does not.

## Building and Maintaining the Stronghold

### Construction Is Mostly Transport

Stone in a quarry is not stone in a wall. Timber in a forest is not a roof
beam. Most building time is spent selecting, cutting, shaping, hauling, lifting,
feeding workers, maintaining tools, and waiting for material or weather. A
master builder's value lies in sequence: the right trench before the wall, the
right joint before the lifting crew arrives, the right drainage before rain.

Every project has an opportunity cost. Quarry workers on a road are absent from
the quarry. Oxen hauling stone are not ploughing. A forge making nails is not
repairing weapons. The Game Master need not count each lost hour. Show the
choice when it becomes politically or seasonally important.

### Materials Remember Their Source

Green timber shrinks and twists. Poorly selected stone splits under frost.
Bricks differ across one firing. Mortar needs the right aggregate and curing.
Iron fittings concentrate wealth in doors, gates, mills, and roofs. Tar, hide,
nails, planks, limestone, and good tools often decide whether a structure lasts
longer than its builders.

Salvage is normal. A ruin supplies dressed stone, old timber, hinges, tiles,
glass, lead, and quarrels about ownership. Reused pieces keep old cuts, scorch,
names, and sometimes old magic.

### The Scale of the Enclosing Work

**Cottage.** One or two households can raise a modest timber dwelling with
neighbor help, but the chimney, roof, floor drainage, and winter fuel decide
whether it remains habitable. Maintenance is intimate: every leak lands on
someone's bedding or stores.

**Tower.** A tower concentrates defense, status, stairs, smoke, and damp into a
small footprint. Building requires skilled lifting and sound foundation. Living
there means carrying water and fuel upward and waste downward.

**Stone house.** The walls last longer than the roof, doors, shutters, and
people. It is slow to warm, valuable in fire, and always collecting moisture
where an unskilled builder believed stone needed no breathing.

**Farm.** A farm is house, yard, barn space, tool storage, dung, water, fields,
and access routes. Maintenance follows animals and season rather than the
building alone.

**Rough palisade.** Felling and hauling dominate the work. Wet posts are driven
close, braced, and joined into a quick claim of safety. From then on the
settlement watches fire and the hidden rot where wood enters soil.

**Reinforced palisade.** More timber and earth deepen the first enclosure.
Bracing, fighting walks, and a worked gate turn it into a defensible system.
Hides may shield exposed timber. The stronger wall takes more people from
fields and woods whenever weather or attack opens it.

**Stone wall.** The wall begins at quarry and cart road long before a mason lays
its face. Foundation, drainage, rubble core, scaffolds, mortar, parapet, and
continual repointing make it a public work that may outlive the authority that
ordered it.

**Fort.** A fort joins several households and a guardable perimeter. Its daily
problem is coordination: gates, watch, fuel, sanitation, stores, and repair must
answer to one schedule.

**Fortress.** A fortress consumes food and labor from beyond its walls. Empty
rooms do not defend themselves. Each gate, stair, roof, cistern, and wall walk
adds inspection and another place where divided authority can hide neglect.

**Castle.** A castle is a settlement under one defensive design. Kitchens,
stables, housing, craft, worship, storage, waste, and command all compete for
space. Its walls advertise a claim large enough to attract siege, tribute,
petition, and inheritance dispute.

**Palace.** A palace converts surplus into ceremony, access, hospitality, and
distance from ordinary work. Maintaining it requires a large supporting
community, whether that support is called service, tax, devotion, or fear.

### Repair, Salvage, and Neglect

Repair should be cheaper and faster than rebuilding while the structure's
sound parts remain. Delay changes that arithmetic. Water enters a small roof
gap, rots a beam end, loosens a wall head, and spoils stores below. A neglected
bridge can look serviceable until a loaded cart finds the one member no one
inspected.

When repair fails beyond recovery, salvage what can be used. Stone and iron
often return to stock after labor. Sound timber returns as timber. Rotten or
badly broken wood becomes fuel. People remember who argued for the cheap repair
and who was beneath it when it fell.

## The Stronghold Functions in Lived Use

Book 01 supplies each function's requirements, materials, times, staff, yields,
and effects. The entries below retain those values. Their construction crews
are campaign estimates for imagining the work. They neither shorten the listed
time nor grant extra CRAFTING rolls. Each crew includes the builder or
responsible specialist. The other hands haul, dig, lift, and prepare.

The **keep** line names the people required for the function to remain useful.
This is separate from general stronghold upkeep. A HANDYMAN can patch a roof or
reset a hinge, but cannot serve as miller, healer, herder, smith, or priest.
Seasonal helpers need not be permanent hirelings. They are often residents
called through wages, household duty, reciprocal labor, or the promise of food.

### Food, Land, Animals, and Preservation

#### Apiary

- **Build:** One carpenter and the future beekeeper can prepare the stands,
  skeps, and shelter in the listed Quarter Day. Moving a settled swarm is two-person work done
  after dusk, when the bees have returned and the air is cool.
- **Keep:** One beekeeper tends the hives through the year. A helper is useful
  during swarming and at each of the three harvests. The keeper guards against
  damp, mice, hunger, and the temptation to take the bees' winter share.
- **Working day:** Most visits are short. The keeper watches the flight at the
  entrance, listens against the hive, repairs weather covers, and judges stores
  by weight. Honey harvest takes a long smoky afternoon and ends with wax,
  stings, and sticky cloth.
- **Ravenland:** Wax often matters as much as honey. It becomes shrine candles,
  document seals, waterproof thread, and quiet payment to a scribe or priest.
  A household with surviving bees after a hard winter is thought provident.

#### Bakery

- **Build:** A mason, a carpenter, and two laborers can raise the oven house.
  The mason shapes the hearth and flue while the others carry clay, stone,
  timber, and water. The oven must dry slowly before its first full firing.
- **Keep:** One baker operates the function. On baking days a helper splits
  fuel, carries flour, draws water, and cleans ash. The Handyman inspects the
  roof and flue, but the baker owns the oven's daily cracks and draught.
- **Working day:** Work begins in darkness. The baker fires the oven, mixes and
  proves dough by touch, rakes out the coals, bakes in falling heat, then uses
  the last warmth for coarse loaves or drying. Poor timing wastes flour already
  measured from the granary.
- **Ravenland:** The smell travels farther than a bell. It tells residents that
  grain has been issued and tells visitors whose household ate first. A baker
  soon learns more about rationing than many stewards.

#### Barn

- **Build:** A master carpenter with six to ten hands can raise the frame. Much
  of the settlement may join for the dangerous lifting, because a half-raised
  roof waits for neither rain nor an adventurer's return.
- **Keep:** One farmer can inspect roof, doors, drains, and stored fodder. During
  haymaking, six or more people may cut, turn, carry, and stack for several
  days. Winter livestock add daily feeding and dung work.
- **Working day:** In summer the doors stand open while hay is tested by twist
  and smell before it enters. In winter someone feeds at dawn, clears wet
  bedding, checks breathing and hooves, and bars the doors before dark.
- **Ravenland:** An empty barn at Fallwane is already a slaughter order. Old
  Mist-bound households learned to read a neighbor's hayrick as plainly as a
  merchant reads a purse.

#### Brewery

- **Build:** One cooper or carpenter, one mason, and three laborers can prepare
  the brewhouse. The costly work lies in sound vats, barrels, a safe hearth,
  and a water supply that does not carry stable or tannery filth.
- **Keep:** One brewer runs the function, with one helper on brewing and
  barreling days. Vessels must be scalded, hoops tightened, yeast protected,
  and fuel kept dry. Spent grain should reach animals before it sours.
- **Working day:** The brewer heats water before dawn, mashes grain, drains the
  sweet liquor, boils it, cools it, and listens to the ferment over following
  days. Much of the craft is waiting at the correct temperature without
  letting dirt or impatience enter the vat.
- **Ravenland:** Ale is food, hospitality, and a portable claim on grain. A
  disputed barrel can expose watered measures, stolen malt, or a steward who
  issued better drink to armed guests than to the people who brewed it.

#### Dairy

- **Build:** Two builders and the dairyman can fit a small cool room over the
  listed week. They need washable surfaces, drainage, shelves, presses, and a
  place where smoke, animals, and direct sun do not reach the milk.
- **Keep:** One farmer or dairyman processes the milk. A child or older helper
  can wash cloths and turn cheeses, though cleanliness must remain one person's
  responsibility. Summer heat makes every delay expensive.
- **Working day:** Milk arrives after morning milking. It is strained at once,
  then churned or set, pressed, salted, drained, and carried to cool storage.
  Cloths and vessels are scrubbed before evening milk begins the work again.
- **Ravenland:** Cheese turns a herd's brief summer plenty into winter food and
  travel provision. Whey may feed children, servants, or pigs. Who receives it
  reveals more about the household than its table manners.

#### Field

- **Build:** Four to eight people can clear, drain, stone, mark, plough, and sow
  a field over the listed month. More hands ease the heaviest days, but oxen,
  seed, suitable weather, and the pace of root removal still govern the work.
- **Keep:** One farmer can direct the crop, yet sowing and harvest call for the
  whole household and borrowed neighbors. Fences, birds, boar, weeds, and
  drainage require visits throughout the growing season.
- **Working day:** Routine work begins by walking the boundary and reading soil
  moisture. At harvest, adults cut and bind while children glean and carry.
  Everyone watches the sky because one wet week can undo a season.
- **Ravenland:** A field is a declaration that the Blood Mist will not return
  and drive everyone indoors. It also binds the stronghold to Fallrise. Leaving
  for an expedition then means hiring hands or accepting loss.

#### Garden

- **Build:** Two people can fence, trench, manure, and lay out a garden over the
  listed month. Good soil is usually carried and made rather than discovered
  beside the kitchen door.
- **Keep:** One farmer or herbalist can tend it through many short visits. At
  sowing and harvest, two or three helpers speed the work. Watering in a dry
  spell can consume more labor than weeding.
- **Working day:** The gardener checks shoots at dawn, lifts pests by hand,
  weeds before roots harden, and carries kitchen ash or dung to the beds.
  Harvest comes in handfuls for the pot and infirmary rather than one great
  wagon day.
- **Ravenland:** A garden close to the kitchen, dung heap, or infirmary shows
  who controls its produce. Seeds kept through the Mist may belong to a family
  custom and be lent only after an oath to return twice as many.

#### Granary

- **Build:** A carpenter and four laborers can raise the floor, bins, roof, and
  guarded door. A mason may be needed for footings. The structure must stand
  above damp and far enough from forge sparks to survive one careless wind.
- **Keep:** One granary keeper can inspect and issue grain. Two laborers help
  when carts arrive or bins must be turned. Cats, traps, sealed gaps, dry
  measures, and a sound roof save more grain than dramatic heroics.
- **Working day:** The keeper probes bins for heat, smells for damp, checks
  droppings, sweeps spilled kernels, and records each measure given out. Seed
  grain remains separate even when hunger makes separation unpopular.
- **Ravenland:** The person with the key holds quiet authority. In isolated
  settlements, grain was often counted before witnesses so a hard winter could
  not be blamed on rumor alone.

#### Hen House

- **Build:** One carpenter and one helper can finish the house and run in a
  day. The important details are dry perches, closable nests, small mesh or
  tight wattle, and a buried edge against foxes.
- **Keep:** One person needs less than an hour each day for a dozen hens. A
  child can gather eggs and scatter feed, but an adult should judge sickness,
  culling, and winter stores.
- **Working day:** The birds are released after dawn, watered, fed household
  scraps, and counted at dusk. Nests are checked twice. Bedding and droppings
  move to the garden rather than remaining to breed mites.
- **Ravenland:** A missing rooster wakes the yard before the keeper finds tracks.
  Eggs are small enough for children to barter and valuable enough for adults
  to remember exactly whose hen laid where.

#### Kennel

- **Build:** Two builders and the kennel master can set pens, shelter, drains,
  and a fenced exercise yard in the listed day. Dogs test weak latches sooner than
  any building inspector.
- **Keep:** One kennel master handles up to a dozen dogs, though a helper is
  needed when several are hunted, bred, or treated. Feed, water, cleaning,
  exercise, and human contact are daily obligations.
- **Working day:** The master checks paws and stools, feeds in an order that
  prevents fighting, runs or trains the dogs, cleans the sleeping space, then
  assigns animals to hunters or watches. Noise often identifies trouble first.
- **Ravenland:** Dogs extend a small settlement's senses beyond its wall. Poorly
  handled dogs consume precious meat, bite guests, and teach every thief the
  exact hour at which the kennel quiets.

#### Mill

- **Build:** One millwright, two carpenters, and six to ten laborers can build a
  small mill. Water control or a raised wind frame may demand more hands. The
  stones and main shaft are lifted by the whole crew, slowly and under one
  voice.
- **Keep:** One miller runs the mill, with one helper during heavy deliveries.
  The miller dresses stones, clears dust, oils bearings, watches belts and
  teeth, and stops the machinery when the floor's vibration changes.
- **Working day:** Grain is received and weighed at first light. The miller sets
  the gap, feeds the stones, feels the meal between finger and thumb, sacks the
  flour, and takes the agreed share. Cleaning follows before dust meets flame.
- **Ravenland:** The mill frees many hands from querns and makes its share a
  political office disguised as craft. After the Mist, a working mill can draw
  three hamlets into dependence before anyone agrees who owns the water.

#### Oast House

- **Build:** A carpenter, a mason, and three laborers can raise the kiln floor,
  drying racks, and ventilated roof. The builder must make heat climb evenly
  without letting sparks reach the crop.
- **Keep:** One grower or brewer oversees drying. Two or three helpers are
  needed when hops or tobacco arrive together. Racks, flues, thatch, and fuel
  stores need inspection before the brief harvest window.
- **Working day:** Fresh crop is spread in shallow layers. The keeper tends a
  low fire, turns the load, tests it by touch, and cools it before packing.
  Sleeping beside the kiln is common when a year's flavor can scorch in an hour.
- **Ravenland:** An oast house links a field to ale, pipes, market dues, and
  conspicuous hospitality. Its sweet, heavy smell tells tax collectors and
  thieves that a saleable crop has come in.

#### Orchard

- **Build:** Two knowledgeable planters and four laborers can lay out and plant
  an orchard over the listed week. Fencing and tree guards may take longer than the
  planting. Fruit worth counting comes years later.
- **Keep:** One farmer prunes, grafts, mulches, and watches disease. Two helpers
  are useful for pruning. Many baskets and hands are needed when fruit ripens
  together. Young trees need winter guards against deer and hare.
- **Working day:** Most days are inspection rather than harvest. The keeper
  checks bark, ties a graft, clears grass around roots, props a laden branch,
  and gathers windfalls before rot spreads.
- **Ravenland:** An orchard is a public wager on continuity. Planting one says
  that children, heirs, or strangers will still hold the ground when the first
  good cider is pressed.

#### Pasture

- **Build:** Three or four people can clear dangerous growth, mark bounds, open
  a water point, and prepare a grazing route. A fenced pasture needs a larger
  post-setting crew and repeated access to timber.
- **Keep:** One herder manages up to twelve cattle in the function's terms. A
  second hand helps at calving, treatment, and long drives. Without a fence,
  supervision is a full working day.
- **Working day:** The herd leaves after milking. The herder moves it before
  hooves ruin wet ground, watches dung and gait for sickness, guards water, and
  brings the animals home before predators begin to range.
- **Ravenland:** Grazing rights are often older than written claims. A new
  stronghold may discover that its empty meadow is remembered by a distant
  household whose cattle survived there before the Mist.

#### Pigsty

- **Build:** Two builders can set a strong pen and roof in one day. Posts must
  be deep, drains must lead away from clean water, and every gate must resist a
  snout working patiently at ground level.
- **Keep:** One swineherd can care for twelve pigs, often alongside other work.
  Forest pannage needs a full-day herder. Farrowing, sickness, and slaughter
  call for another adult.
- **Working day:** The keeper brings scraps and water, checks fences, shifts
  bedding, and watches which animal is bullied away from feed. In mast season,
  pigs are driven under oak and beech and counted twice before dark.
- **Ravenland:** Pigs turn waste and forest mast into meat with alarming speed.
  They also foul wells, root up graves, and settle boundary disputes by escaping
  into the one garden whose owner already bears a grudge.

#### Root Cellar

- **Build:** Two diggers and one builder can finish a modest cellar over the
  listed week if the ground is sound. Wet soil may require stone lining, drains,
  and twice the labor.
- **Keep:** One storekeeper inspects it every few days. During harvest, three or
  four carriers sort and stack produce. Air vents, drains, doors, and bins need
  repair before winter closes the ground.
- **Working day:** The keeper enters with a lamp, feels the air, checks roots
  for softness, removes one spoiled piece before it infects a basket, and keeps
  apples away from foods they hasten to age.
- **Ravenland:** A cellar smells of earth, old straw, and the first mold caught
  in time. Hidden cellars outlast burned houses, so an abandoned foundation may
  still conceal seed, bones, or a family's last sealed jar.

#### Salt Works

- **Build:** One mason or metalworker and four laborers can prepare pans,
  hearths, brine channels, sheds, and fuel stacks in about a week. Coastal
  ground and weather may demand heavier drainage work.
- **Keep:** One salt worker can tend a pan, with two cutters and carriers needed
  to keep fuel moving. Channels must be cleared, pans patched, and soot kept out
  of the crystals.
- **Working day:** Brine is lifted or led into the pan, then boiled under steady
  heat. The worker skims dirt, rakes crystals, dries them, and covers the result
  before sea damp takes it back.
- **Ravenland:** Salt wealth eats woodland. A productive shore can be ringed by
  stumps because the white crystals seem to appear in the pan while their true
  cost arrives cart by cart as firewood.

#### Sheepfold

- **Build:** Three people can set the enclosure and lean shelter in the listed day.
  Stone country takes longer to gather but gives a fold that needs fewer new
  posts each spring.
- **Keep:** One herder tends twelve sheep under the function's scale. Lambing
  needs a second watcher. Shearing brings four to six practiced hands for a
  short, exhausting spell.
- **Working day:** Sheep leave the fold at first light. The herder chooses
  grazing, checks eyes and feet, keeps the flock moving together, and returns
  before dusk to count animals by touch as well as sight.
- **Ravenland:** The fold protects sheep only during danger hours. Wool still
  travels through shearer, washer, spinner, dyer, and tailor before the first
  cloak exists, making a plain garment the record of many obligations.

#### Slaughterhouse

- **Build:** A carpenter, a mason, and three laborers can prepare the roof,
  drains, tables, hanging beam, and water access. It belongs downhill and
  downwind from the well, which guarantees an argument over whose house lies
  beyond it.
- **Keep:** One butcher can operate it, with two to four helpers on a slaughter
  day. Iron must remain sharp. Surfaces are scrubbed at once, drains cleared,
  and dogs kept from blood and offal.
- **Working day:** Animals arrive calm and early. The butcher kills and bleeds
  each one before skinning it, opening the carcass, and dividing the joints for
  cooling. Other hands carry away the hide and usable offal. Fat, horn, bone,
  guts, and blood must also reach their proper workers before heat spoils them.
- **Ravenland:** Fall slaughter is a settlement event, not private butchery.
  Families watch the apportionment because a missing hide or disputed liver can
  reopen a debt that began when the animal was born.

#### Smokery

- **Build:** Two builders can make the tight chamber, racks, low hearth, and
  adjustable vents over the listed week. Green wood and a poorly placed flue will teach
  the design's faults by ruining the first load.
- **Keep:** One cook or smoke keeper tends it. A helper prepares and hangs a
  large batch. Roof, hooks, vents, salt store, and chosen smoking wood must be
  kept dry and clean.
- **Working day:** Salted meat or fish is hung with space around each piece. The
  keeper feeds a smolder rather than a flame, reads color and smell, adjusts
  vents, and may sleep within sight of the smoke for several nights.
- **Ravenland:** A full smokery holds winter in concentrated form. When Need
  rises, its warm smell carries beyond the palisade and the watch often passes
  it more frequently than the steward admits.

#### Stables

- **Build:** One carpenter and four laborers can raise stalls, loft, tack room,
  drains, and a broad door. A smith fits hinges and hardware. Fire spacing and
  a clear escape matter because frightened horses will not wait for orders.
- **Keep:** One stableboy tends the function's animals, with help when many
  mounts return together. Feeding, watering, grooming, mucking, tack care, and
  hoof inspection fill every day.
- **Working day:** The keeper feeds before eating, waters in measured stages,
  mucks stalls, grooms each animal, checks girths and hooves, then prepares
  mounts. Evening repeats the work and ends with hay counted against winter.
- **Ravenland:** The stableboy often knows who rode at night, whose horse came
  back blown, and which saddle carried unfamiliar mud. Such knowledge is rarely
  entered in the town hall record.

#### Still

- **Build:** One coppersmith or skilled brewer, one mason, and two laborers can
  set the vessel, hearth, cooling tub, and safe work space. Every joint is tested
  with water before fire is trusted.
- **Keep:** One alchemist or brewer operates it. A helper brings fuel and water
  during a long run. Copper, seals, cooling flow, clean receiving vessels, and
  distance from open flame need constant attention.
- **Working day:** A fermented wash is heated slowly. The keeper watches the
  first vapors, changes vessels by smell and experience, cools the spirit, and
  cleans the apparatus before residue hardens. Leaving the fire is forbidden.
- **Ravenland:** Spirits carry medicine, hospitality, trade value, and misery in
  the same small cask. A stronghold that sells them soon develops rules about
  measures, debt, and who may drink while holding a watch spear.

### Craft, Extraction, and Learned Work

#### Forge

- **Build:** One mason, one carpenter, one smith, and four laborers can prepare
  the hearth, chimney, bellows frame, anvil seat, quench space, and fuel shed.
  The smith decides the layout because each misplaced step will be repeated for
  years beside hot iron.
- **Keep:** One smith operates the forge. A striker or apprentice doubles what
  can be handled safely and keeps bellows moving. The chimney, bellows leather,
  tongs, hammer faces, quench water, and charcoal store demand routine repair.
- **Working day:** The apprentice wakes the fire and sorts yesterday's work.
  The smith heats, judges color, hammers, reheats, and finishes while customers
  wait with hinges and tools. Evening is for dressing edges and banking coals.
- **Ravenland:** Hammering announces that a settlement can repair what it owns.
  Smiths often receive broken iron before new commissions, and must decide
  whether a ploughshare, gate hinge, or adventurer's sword gets the day's heat.

#### Kiln

- **Build:** One mason or experienced potter and four laborers can build a kiln
  over the listed two days. The chamber, firebox, flue, and outer shell must dry before
  a slow trial firing reveals cracks.
- **Keep:** One potter, charcoal burner, or other specialist runs each firing.
  Two helpers stack wares and carry fuel. The kiln is patched after cooling,
  while ash and residue are cleared before a different process begins.
- **Working day:** Loading may take a morning. The fire then climbs by stages
  under constant watch. The keeper reads color through a spy hole and controls
  draught through the night. Opening too soon can waste days of prior work.
- **Ravenland:** A failed firing makes little spectacle. It leaves warped pots,
  weak brick, or half-burned charcoal and a silent argument over an entire
  stack of fuel that cannot be put back into the forest.

#### Laboratory

- **Build:** One learned practitioner, one carpenter, and two laborers can fit
  the room. A mason makes the hearth and flue. Locks, shelves, water, washable
  benches, and ventilation matter more than impressive decoration.
- **Keep:** One alchemist or other specialist controls the space. A trusted
  assistant washes vessels, grinds ingredients, copies labels, and watches a
  flame. No general servant should tidy substances they cannot identify.
- **Working day:** The keeper measures, heats, cools, filters, and records while
  the assistant prevents interruptions. Work ends with vessels washed, poisons
  locked, flames quenched, and every jar returned to its known place.
- **Ravenland:** The laboratory gathers bottles whose value and danger most
  residents cannot judge. Its locked door breeds stories, but the strongest
  suspicion often comes from a missing kitchen pot or the well tasting strange.

#### Mine

- **Build:** A master miner and eight to twelve workers can open the entrance,
  shore the first workings, cut drainage, and prepare hauling ways. Fewer hands
  may start the hole, but they cannot safely timber, dig, and remove spoil at
  useful speed.
- **Keep:** Up to twelve miners work the function. At least one experienced
  person judges ground and air. Timber cutting, tool sharpening, hauling, lamp
  tending, drainage, and spoil work keep several hands from the face itself.
- **Working day:** The crew checks supports before entering. Miners strike and
  wedge at the face while others haul, drain, trim timbers, and listen for
  stone. Everyone counts lamps, companions, and changes in dripping water.
- **Ravenland:** Ore emerges only because timber, food, lamp fuel, and bodies go
  below. After a collapse, families count the missing before the steward counts
  the load, unless the stronghold has already taught them whom it values.

#### Quarry

- **Build:** A quarry master and six to twelve laborers can strip soil, expose a
  face, cut drainage, and make a hauling path. Teams begin smaller on a natural
  outcrop, then grow when useful blocks must be moved rather than merely split.
- **Keep:** Up to twelve workers extract stone under the function's scale. A
  smith or regular forge service is needed for edges. Roads, drainage, ropes,
  wedges, and the face itself require inspection.
- **Working day:** Workers clear loose stone, mark joints, cut channels, drive
  wedges in rhythm, and stand well away when the block speaks. The rest of the
  day may be spent trimming and hauling one successful fall.
- **Ravenland:** The easiest stone leaves first. Each later year reaches deeper,
  lifts farther, and asks more of the same road. A cheap promise of endless
  stone usually comes from someone who has never stood beneath the face.

#### Sawmill

- **Build:** One millwright, two carpenters, and six laborers can set the wheel,
  drive, blade frame, guides, log bed, and waste channel. Waterworks or a raised
  structure may call for twice that crew during lifting.
- **Keep:** One lumberjack or sawyer operates the mill, with two haulers keeping
  logs and finished boards moving. The blade, guides, bearings, watercourse, and
  waste channel need inspection every working day.
- **Working day:** Logs are rolled and dogged in place before the wheel is
  admitted. The sawyer watches the cut, clears slabs, resets the carriage, and
  stops whenever alignment changes. Wet boards are stacked for air rather than
  hurried indoors.
- **Ravenland:** The mill moves effort from sawing to felling and hauling. A
  stronghold may celebrate plentiful boards for one season and discover in the
  next that fuel, game cover, and easy timber have all retreated together.

#### Tailor Shop

- **Build:** One carpenter and the tailor can fit the shop in the listed two days.
  Broad tables, good daylight, dry chests, and a stove for pressing serve better
  than a grand frontage.
- **Keep:** One tailor runs the function. One apprentice can spin thread, baste,
  mend, and learn cuts without wasting cloth. Damp, moths, mice, rusted needles,
  and careless lamps threaten wealth that took months of work to weave.
- **Working day:** Morning light is used for measuring and cutting. Sewing fills
  the afternoon, with repairs fitted between new work. Every scrap is sorted at
  closing for patches, bindings, stuffing, or children's clothes.
- **Ravenland:** Cloth is labor stored in a form everyone wears against the
  weather. A tailor remembers who asked for hidden pockets, whose good coat has
  been turned twice, and which household can no longer afford matching sleeves.

#### Tannery

- **Build:** One tanner and four laborers can dig and line pits, set scraping
  beams, raise drying frames, and lead dirty water away. The site belongs below
  clean water and outside the most valued living ground.
- **Keep:** One tanner operates the function, with one or two helpers during
  soaking, scraping, and moving hides. Bark must be gathered and ground. Pits,
  frames, knives, drains, and shade all need attention.
- **Working day:** Fresh hides are trimmed and treated before rot begins. The
  tanner hauls wet weight from pit to beam, scrapes until the shoulders ache,
  changes solutions, then works drying leather so it does not harden uselessly.
- **Ravenland:** The smell follows the workers home and fixes their shop near
  the poorer wall. Yet every shield grip, boot, harness, bellows, and waterskin
  eventually proves how much the stronghold depends upon them.

#### Workshop

- **Build:** One carpenter and two helpers can raise and fit a general workshop
  in the listed two days. The resident craftsperson decides where light falls and
  where long work can pass without striking a wall.
- **Keep:** One bowyer or carpenter runs the function. An apprentice can prepare
  stock and maintain tools. Benches, clamps, edges, dry timber, glue, and the
  stove need daily care. Seasoned wood needs months of patience.
- **Working day:** The craftsperson selects stock before cutting, roughs work in
  good light, and leaves glue or bent wood under pressure while repairing small
  tools. Closing means sweeping shavings away from flame and restoring each
  edge to its rack.
- **Ravenland:** The shop becomes the place where broken domestic things wait
  beside new commissions. Its queue shows the stronghold's priorities more
  honestly than proclamations: cart wheel, cradle, bow, coffin, then door.

### Water, Roads, Trade, and Communication

#### Bridge

- **Build:** One master carpenter or mason and eight to twelve laborers can
  build a modest span. Foundations, abutments, and lifting need the full crew.
  A long or flood-prone crossing can absorb every available cart and rope.
- **Keep:** Two people should inspect after flood, thaw, and heavy traffic. One
  carpenter can replace deck boards, but scour beneath a pier or cracked
  masonry calls the original trade back with several laborers.
- **Working day:** Routine care means clearing floodwood, tightening rails,
  sounding timber, checking the approaches, and watching how a loaded cart
  makes the span move. Repairs are done before the river rises again.
- **Ravenland:** A bridge joins markets and divides claims. Everyone uses it,
  several powers may remember building it, and no one agrees who owes repair
  until a wheel goes through the decking.

#### Dovecote

- **Build:** One mason or carpenter with two helpers can raise the tower or loft
  in the listed day. The keeper must fit protected nesting holes and keep the birds
  separate from grain stores.
- **Keep:** One caretaker can tend a dozen pigeons in less than a Quarter Day.
  Birds need feed, water, cleaning, breeding control, and daily handling. A
  messenger must still carry them outward in cages before they can fly home.
- **Working day:** The keeper releases birds for exercise, scrapes the floor,
  checks nests, replaces water, and handles each trained bird. Arriving pigeons
  are caught quietly and their messages taken straight to the named recipient.
- **Ravenland:** A bird can outrun public news by days. The practical question
  is therefore less who can send than who is permitted to know first, and who
  controls the cages when a distant ally asks for help.

#### Inn

- **Build:** One carpenter, one mason, and six laborers can convert or raise the
  common room, kitchen, guest chambers, privy, and stable space. The innkeeper
  should decide the passage between hearth, cellar, yard, and door.
- **Keep:** One innkeeper needs a cook or server once trade becomes steady. A
  stable hand and cleaner may be shared with other functions. Linen, fleas,
  locks, waste, firewood, food, ale, and unpaid accounts require daily work.
- **Working day:** The hearth is lit before travelers wake. Beds are aired,
  porridge set, animals watered, and yesterday's cups counted. Evening brings
  serving, bargaining, watching tempers, and remembering which story came from
  which road.
- **Ravenland:** The inn converts travelers into news and news into trade. A
  keeper who survived the Mist years may trust a repeated face more than a
  silver coin, yet will know the value of both.

#### Marketplace

- **Build:** A carpenter and four laborers can set permanent stalls, drains,
  tether posts, waste pits, and a measure table. A larger crew clears and levels
  the ground before the first declared market day.
- **Keep:** One market reeve or respected trader can oversee ordinary use. Two
  guards and two cleaners are prudent on market days. Sellers staff their own
  stalls. The stronghold provides measures, order, and a place to settle fees.
- **Working day:** Before dawn, stalls are assigned and standard measures set
  out. The reeve hears disputes while guards watch tethered beasts and purses.
  After closing, spoiled food and dung must leave before they poison the well.
- **Ravenland:** Trust is the scarce good. People emerging from generations of
  isolated custom may disagree about the size of a sack, the purity of old
  silver, and whether a witnessed promise outweighs a stranger's scale.

#### Pier

- **Build:** One shipwright or master carpenter and eight laborers can drive
  piles, brace the frame, lay decking, and cut the shore approach. Work below
  the waterline is slow, cold, and dangerous even on a calm day.
- **Keep:** Two dockhands inspect and handle ordinary arrivals. A carpenter is
  needed after ice or collision. Mooring lines, steps, piles, deck boards, and
  the unseen bracing consume replacement timber every season.
- **Working day:** Dockhands clear weed and ice, sound suspect boards, receive
  lines, secure craft, carry catches or cargo, and keep the loading way open.
  Low water is used to inspect what high water hides.
- **Ravenland:** Fish, strangers, cargo, sickness, and rumor all enter on the
  same wet boards. A pier extends the stronghold's gate onto water without
  giving the gatekeeper the same control.

#### Road

- **Build:** Twelve quarry workers can complete an ordinary road across one hex
  in the listed month. Difficult ground takes two months. Local households may
  add carts and labor, but one experienced hand must govern crown, ditch, and
  culvert instead of merely clearing a track.
- **Keep:** Four to six laborers can patrol and repair a used stretch during the
  travel season. After thaw or storm, the full twelve-person crew may be needed
  for washed culverts, failed embankments, or a broken crossing.
- **Working day:** Road workers clear ditches first, because water destroys more
  than wheels. They fill ruts with graded stone, cut back growth, reset markers,
  and leave repairs crowned so the next rain runs away.
- **Ravenland:** A road invites merchants and kin, then tax collectors, armies,
  claimants, and people fleeing somewhere worse. Reopening an old route also
  reopens every argument over where it once led and who once kept it.

#### Shop

- **Build:** One carpenter and two helpers can fit a secure shop in two days.
  The shopkeeper needs dry shelves, shutters, a counter, storage, and a door
  broad enough for the actual goods.
- **Keep:** One shopkeeper or hawker staffs it. A household member can mind the
  counter during errands. Stock must be aired, counted, protected from mice and
  damp, and judged against how long the settlement can afford it to remain
  unsold.
- **Working day:** The keeper opens shutters, sweeps, brings valued goods into
  view, and spends more time bargaining than exchanging coin. Closing means
  checking credit tallies and carrying the best stock behind a locked door.
- **Ravenland:** Shelves reveal what the stronghold produces and what it
  hopes passing adventurers will buy. A fine imported item may sit for a year
  while nails, lamp oil, and salt change hands before noon.

#### Toll House

- **Build:** One carpenter and three laborers can build the shelter, barrier,
  hearth, and secure chest in two days. A smith supplies chain, lock, and
  fittings. The approach must make evasion visible without trapping ordinary
  traffic.
- **Keep:** One toll keeper staffs quiet hours. Two guards are safer when armed
  groups travel. Relief is needed if the crossing remains open through the
  night, because exhaustion makes poor arithmetic and worse courage.
- **Working day:** The keeper raises the barrier, judges load and exemption,
  takes payment or records obligation, then lowers it behind the traveler.
  Coin, goods, and tallies are reconciled at each change of watch.
- **Ravenland:** Building the house is easier than proving the right to collect.
  Every priest, old family, Rust Brother, returning lord, and heavily armed band
  may remember a different exemption.

#### Warehouse

- **Build:** One carpenter and six laborers can raise the broad roof, lifted
  floor, doors, lanes, and loading platform. A mason provides dry footings. The
  keeper should plan stacks before the first cart arrives.
- **Keep:** One warehouse keeper controls access and inventory. Two porters are
  useful whenever trade is active, and guards become necessary once stored
  value exceeds what neighbors can pretend not to notice.
- **Working day:** The keeper checks roof stains, pallets, seals, pests, and air
  before receiving goods. Porters weigh or count each load and stack it so the
  oldest can leave first without moving everything else.
- **Ravenland:** Storage lets one owner wait while another person is hungry now.
  A locked warehouse therefore accumulates resentment along with grain, cloth,
  ore, or salt.

#### Well

- **Build:** One experienced well digger and four to six laborers sink and line
  a well. Two remain above whenever anyone works below. Deep shafts, loose soil,
  or heavy stone lining may require a larger crew and lifting frame.
- **Keep:** One appointed household can inspect the curb, cover, rope, bucket,
  and drain each day. Serious cleaning needs three people: one below, one on the
  rope, and one watching the work and the air.
- **Working day:** The first drawer notices water level, taste, smell, rope wear,
  and any dead thing beneath the cover. Spilled water is led away. Animals,
  washing, tanning, and privies remain outside a commonly enforced boundary.
- **Ravenland:** Everyone tastes a well every day, yet contamination is often
  recognized after several households sicken. Rules around it become some of a
  new stronghold's earliest common law.

### Defense, Confinement, and Authority

#### Arena

- **Build:** One carpenter and eight laborers can level the ground, raise
  barriers, hang gates, spread sand, and make simple seating. Stone tiers or
  secure animal pens demand masons and a larger crew.
- **Keep:** No permanent staff is needed between contests. On an event day, one
  master of games needs four to eight guards, two gate hands, and several
  cleaners or animal handlers, depending on what enters the ring.
- **Working day:** Before a contest, rails and gates are tested and the sand is
  raked. Entrants are separated, weapons checked, and exits kept clear. After
  the crowd leaves, blood, dung, broken boards, and public grudges remain.
- **Ravenland:** Orcs may value the arena's open proof of strength, but any kin
  can use it to price honor before witnesses. A ruler who rigs one bout may
  discover that spectacle makes resentment communal.

#### Ditch

- **Build:** Eight to twelve diggers can cut a defensive ditch over the listed
  two weeks. Spoil should be thrown inward to strengthen the defense. One
  experienced builder sets the slope and drainage so the sides do not bury the
  crew's work after the first rain.
- **Keep:** Two laborers can walk and clear the circuit regularly. After storm,
  thaw, or collapse, four to six may be needed. Vegetation, silt, animal burrows,
  and rubbish slowly turn a defense into level ground.
- **Working day:** Maintenance begins at the lowest point, opening drains before
  cutting growth or reshaping a bank. Every bridge and daily crossing is checked
  because attackers will study the same convenience.
- **Ravenland:** A ditch appears humble beside a wall, but it records whether a
  settlement still acts together. The first section allowed to fill often lies
  beside land whose household no longer accepts the stronghold's authority.

#### Drawbridge

- **Build:** One master carpenter, one smith, and eight laborers can build the
  leaf, pivots, frame, counterweight, and lifting gear. Hanging the bridge
  requires the whole crew to move under one command.
- **Keep:** Two trained gatekeepers can raise and lower it. A third person gives
  safe relief and watches the approach. Ropes or chains, pivots, timber ends,
  stops, and balance need daily inspection.
- **Working day:** The morning watch lowers the bridge under control, listens
  for rubbing, and tests the lifting gear before traffic begins. At dusk, the
  approach is cleared and the bridge raised while another guard observes.
- **Ravenland:** A bridge jammed open defeats the moat. One jammed closed turns
  the stronghold into its own prison, which is why residents judge a gatekeeper
  by uneventful mornings rather than battlefield courage.

#### Dungeon

- **Build:** One mason, one smith, and four laborers can prepare a secure cell
  block for the function's four prisoners. The drain and barred door deserve as
  much attention as thick walls.
- **Keep:** One jailer staffs the dungeon, with a guard relieving sleep and
  escorting prisoners. Every day consumes food and water, while waste and
  sickness demand attention. Lamp fuel, sound restraints, and controlled keys
  cost money even when no sentence is being carried out.
- **Working day:** The jailer counts prisoners and keys, removes waste, brings
  water and food, checks bars and restraints, and listens for illness or digging.
  Prisoners observe the routine with equal care.
- **Ravenland:** A dungeon creates obligation rather than solving danger.
  Captives bargain, sicken, shout through walls, and acquire kin who want them
  returned. Residents learn what their rulers intend by whom they confine.

#### Fence

- **Build:** Four people can fence a field or pasture over the listed one or two days if posts
  and rails are ready. Stone boundaries take more hauling but less replacement.
  Gates consume the best timber and the most argument.
- **Keep:** One person walks the line every few days. A Handyman can reset posts
  and mend rails, while two helpers handle fallen trees or long storm damage.
  Animals identify the weakest place before people do.
- **Working day:** The keeper follows the boundary with mallet, wedges, spare
  lashings, and a shovel. Sagging gates are lifted, earth packed around posts,
  and tracks read to learn what passed through.
- **Ravenland:** A fence saves herding labor and makes exclusion visible. Where
  old grazing custom crosses a new enclosure, each repaired post can become a
  statement of ownership.

#### Gallows

- **Build:** One carpenter and three laborers can raise the frame and platform
  in a day. A smith may fit brackets. The approach and viewing ground are part
  of the design because the structure exists to be seen.
- **Keep:** No full-time staff is required. One executioner maintains rope and
  mechanism. Two guards keep the site on the day of sentence. Continued upkeep
  means choosing to leave the gallows visible when unused.
- **Working day:** Before an execution, the beam, knot, drop, platform, and route
  are tested in private. Afterward, the body and crowd must be dealt with, and
  someone washes boards that were built to make washing futile.
- **Ravenland:** The gallows declares that authority means to punish before
  witnesses. It may deter some crimes while teaching every resident which
  deaths their rulers consider lawful enough to display.

#### Guard Tower

- **Build:** One carpenter or mason and six laborers can raise a modest tower.
  Foundations, stairs, roof, shutters, signal gear, and a clear field of view
  must all be completed before height becomes useful.
- **Keep:** A tower needs at least three guards to cover day and night without
  immediate exhaustion. Four allow proper relief and sickness. The Handyman
  tends structure, but the guard captain maintains the watch schedule.
- **Working day:** Each watch receives the horizon, gate state, signal method,
  and recent sightings from the last. Guards clear shutters, test horn or fire,
  scan known approaches, and record changes rather than merely staring outward.
- **Ravenland:** Wind and rain attack the tower while repetition attacks the
  guard. The captain's harder task is keeping an alarm credible after a hundred
  quiet nights and one joke sounded at the wrong hour.

#### Moat

- **Build:** Twelve to twenty diggers can complete a moat over the listed month,
  guided by one builder who understands water and soil. Carts remove or place
  spoil while carpenters protect the intended crossing.
- **Keep:** Two laborers inspect banks and water each week. Four to eight are
  needed for seasonal dredging, weed cutting, ice damage, or a failed sluice.
  Mosquitoes and rot make neglect visible before the defense fails.
- **Working day:** Workers clear inflow and outflow, cut reeds, pull rubbish,
  inspect banks for burrows, and measure where silt is rising. Dry moats need
  reshaping. Wet ones need moving water rather than a stagnant skin.
- **Ravenland:** A neglected moat becomes either a sickness ditch or an easier
  road. Children, pigs, and desperate gardeners will also find uses for its
  edges unless the stronghold agrees what the water is for.

#### Palisade

- **Build:** One master carpenter and twelve to twenty laborers can fell, haul,
  shape, trench, set, brace, and bind a palisade within the listed week only by
  working as a settlement effort. Carts and draft animals matter as much as axes.
- **Keep:** Two people inspect a section each week. A repair party of four to
  six replaces suspect posts. Ground-line rot, loose wall walks, cleared fire
  lanes, and vegetation outside the wall require constant notice.
- **Working day:** Inspectors sound posts near the earth, look for fresh soil,
  tighten braces, and clear dead growth. Replacement begins before a post leans,
  because removing one forces the neighboring wall to carry it.
- **Ravenland:** A palisade gives rapid safety and a yearly hunger for timber.
  Its gate separates resident from outsider, yet its repair rota shows which
  households are accepted as part of the defense.

#### Portcullis

- **Build:** One smith, one mason, one carpenter, and eight laborers can prepare
  the grille, guides, gate chamber, lifting gear, and counterweight. Fitting it
  under full weight is slow work with little room for error.
- **Keep:** Two trained gatekeepers operate it. A smith and Handyman inspect it
  regularly. Guides must be swept, rope or chain checked, iron oiled, and the
  counterweight kept free of stored rubbish.
- **Working day:** The gate crew raises and lowers the grille before opening,
  listening for uneven travel. Grit is cleared from the sill, the locking pawl
  tested, and no visitor stands beneath it during practice.
- **Ravenland:** The impressive iron barrier is useless if it has not moved
  since the last lord inspected it. Gate crews value a dull daily test more than
  the story of how quickly it fell once in battle.

#### Ramparts

- **Build:** A master mason, two skilled masons, and twenty to forty laborers can
  undertake the listed month of construction only with continuous quarrying,
  hauling, mortar mixing, lifting, and scaffolding. Large walls may occupy a
  community far longer than the function's abstract build.
- **Keep:** Two masons and four laborers should inspect and repair in fair
  seasons. After frost, siege, or settlement, a larger crew is needed. Drains,
  wall walks, stairs, coping, mortar joints, and growing roots all matter.
- **Working day:** Masons start where water entered. They tap loose stone,
  remove failed mortar, reset coping, clear drains, and mark cracks for later
  comparison. Small repair prevents the collapse that songs remember.
- **Ravenland:** Stone walls outlive their builders and tempt each new power to
  claim the old labor as proof of rightful rule. Ruin stone may carry mason
  marks belonging to a community whose descendants now live outside the gate.

#### Secret Tunnel

- **Build:** Secrecy limits the crew. One miner or mason and three trusted
  laborers can excavate, shore, drain, ventilate, and disguise a short passage.
  Extra carriers may handle spoil without being shown where it came from.
- **Keep:** One keeper and one trusted companion inspect monthly. Supports,
  water, vermin, air, grate, lock, and the concealed exit require care. No one
  should inspect alone where a collapse can silence the only witness.
- **Working day:** Maintenance is done at quiet hours. The pair checks markers,
  clears drains, tests the exit from both sides, replaces disturbed concealment,
  and removes tracks or spoil before daylight.
- **Ravenland:** A tunnel known by nobody fills with water. One known by many
  ceases to be secret. Some strongholds divide the route between two keepers so
  neither can betray the whole passage without the other.

#### Shooting Range

- **Build:** Two carpenters and four laborers can clear lanes, raise a backstop,
  mark distances, and set targets in the listed day. The ground beyond must be walked
  before anyone trusts the bank.
- **Keep:** No permanent staff is required. One range master should control
  practice. Two helpers replace targets and recover arrows after a busy day.
  Lanes must remain clear and no one retrieves while shooting continues.
- **Working day:** The master inspects the backstop, assigns a line, calls each
  release and cease, then sends everyone forward together. Arrows are counted,
  shafts repaired, and new scars in the ground examined.
- **Ravenland:** The range's rhythm carries beyond the wall. Neighbors know
  whether the stronghold trains weekly or merely raises targets when visitors
  arrive.

#### Training Grounds

- **Build:** Two carpenters and six laborers can drain and level the ground, set
  posts and dummies, and prepare racks over the listed two days. Good footing prevents
  more injuries than elaborate equipment.
- **Keep:** No permanent function staff is required, though one trainer makes
  practice safer and more useful. Two residents can repair dummies, rake ruts,
  and inspect blunted weapons after each session.
- **Working day:** Practice begins with stance and movement before weapons.
  Groups drill signals, spacing, and withdrawal, then spar under supervision.
  The last hands collect splinters, straighten the ground, and bind bruises.
- **Ravenland:** Regular drill teaches who obeys whom when frightened. It can
  unite mixed kin through common movement, or expose that one household expects
  others to stand in front.

#### Trophy Hall

- **Build:** One carpenter, one tanner or preparer, and three laborers can fit a
  dry hall with strong mounts, guarded doors, and controlled light. Very large
  remains need a lifting crew and reinforcement in the roof.
- **Keep:** One keeper dusts, checks pests and damp, and controls access. A
  tanner or specialist returns when skin, horn, bone, or stitching begins to
  fail. Guards are needed if trophies are valuable beyond their story.
- **Working day:** The keeper airs the room in dry weather, inspects mounts for
  insects, renews preservatives, and rehearses the names and circumstances
  visitors will ask to hear.
- **Ravenland:** A trophy must survive both decay and doubt. The hall may deter
  ordinary enemies while advertising the stronghold to the monster, kin, or
  cult whose dead it has displayed.

#### Vault

- **Build:** One mason, one smith, and six laborers can build the enclosure,
  iron-bound door, dry floor, and difficult approach. Fewer people should know
  the final lock and key arrangement than know the stonework.
- **Keep:** One steward controls inventory and keys. Two guards provide watch
  when the vault holds serious wealth. A second witness should attend every
  opening. Hinges, damp, seals, locks, and divided access need regular checks.
- **Working day:** Most days the vault remains shut. On an opening day, witnesses
  inspect seals, count what enters or leaves, record it twice, lock together,
  and separate the keys again.
- **Ravenland:** Thick walls protect wealth while announcing where it rests.
  The deepest vulnerability is usually a person with a key, a debt, and a
  relative outside the wall.

### Care, Worship, Knowledge, and Public Life

#### Fireplace

- **Build:** One mason and one helper can set a safe hearth and flue in a day.
  Timber clearances must be judged before the first fire, and a new chimney
  should be tested with a small smoky flame before the room depends on it.
- **Keep:** One household member tends it, or the Handyman supplies the
  function's abstract care. Ash is removed daily. The chimney, hearthstone,
  fire tools, spark screen, and dry fuel need regular attention.
- **Working day:** The first person awake coaxes yesterday's coal, warms water,
  opens or closes the draught, and adds dry wood sparingly. Before sleep, flame
  is reduced to a banked ember and every loose spark is found.
- **Ravenland:** Whoever controls the morning fire quietly controls warmth,
  porridge, hot water, and the order in which damp people become useful. Wet
  fuel and a neglected flue can humble the finest hall.

#### Graveyard

- **Build:** Two diggers and two helpers can mark bounds, clear paths, fence the
  ground, and prepare the first graves in the listed day. A priest, elder, or family
  witness chooses ground safely distant from wells.
- **Keep:** One caretaker visits weekly. A burial needs two to four diggers,
  bearers from the household, and whoever performs the rite. Drains, markers,
  fence, paths, and signs of disturbed earth need inspection.
- **Working day:** The caretaker cuts growth, resets names, walks the boundary,
  and notes settling soil. Before burial, the grave is measured and witnessed.
  Afterward, earth is mounded because it will sink.
- **Ravenland:** Burial also maintains peace with walkers and the wronged dead.
  A graveyard is an archive held in names, kin memory, and soil. Moving it
  without testimony can turn old uncertainty into present fear.

#### Infirmary

- **Build:** One carpenter, one healer, and four laborers can prepare a warm,
  light room with washable beds, clean water access, a waste route, and secure
  stores. The healer chooses the layout to keep fever away from wounds.
- **Keep:** One physician operates the function. One orderly is needed when
  more than one patient cannot rise. Serious outbreaks may consume four or more
  attendants. Laundry, fuel, broth, water, herbs, waste, and night watch never
  appear in the healer's hands by themselves.
- **Working day:** The healer observes before touching, then cleans wounds,
  changes dressings, measures drink and food, and compares heat, swelling,
  breath, stool, sleep, and function with yesterday. The orderly boils linen
  and keeps the room quieter than the hall.
- **Ravenland:** Recovery is remembered by households, especially when beds are
  scarce. Who received clean linen, night watching, or the last useful herb can
  become gratitude, obligation, or a grievance that survives the patient.

#### Library

- **Build:** One carpenter, one scribe, and three laborers can fit dry shelves,
  chests, reading boards, shutters, and a secure door over the listed week. A mason
  checks hearth and damp. The scribe arranges the collection before it grows.
- **Keep:** One scribe or librarian can care for the room, with a copyist added
  if books are used heavily. The keeper guards against dust and vermin, then
  checks continually for damp or fire. Loose bindings need repair. Loans and
  unauthorized rearrangement threaten the collection in quieter ways.
- **Working day:** The keeper opens shutters only in fair light, checks for
  moisture and gnawing, returns books to known places, supervises readers, and
  copies a fading title or index before darkness makes lamps necessary.
- **Ravenland:** Books survived the Mist unevenly. A local copy may be the only
  witness to an old road, claim, demon, or cure, which makes permission to read
  it a form of authority rather than mere hospitality.

#### Monument

- **Build:** One sculptor or mason, two skilled assistants, and eight to twelve
  laborers can lay foundation, lift material, carve, and clear the approach over
  the listed month. Bronze work also requires a smith and careful casting crew.
- **Keep:** One caretaker can wash and clear growth. A mason returns each year
  to check joints and foundation. Public damage requires guards only when the
  stronghold chooses to treat it as crime rather than argument.
- **Working day:** Ordinary care is quiet: sweeping the base, clearing weeds,
  renewing painted letters, and watching cracks. Ceremony days bring flowers,
  speeches, guards, and people who remember the subject differently.
- **Ravenland:** The name cut into stone matters less than who supplied the
  labor and whose dead were omitted. Scratched words may be neglect, revenge,
  or a rival history written with a nail.

#### Scriptorium

- **Build:** One carpenter and the scribe can fit the room over the listed week. Desks
  must meet daylight without glare. Parchment and ink need dry storage. The
  stove and lamps must remain far from loose sheets.
- **Keep:** One scribe operates the function. An apprentice prepares quills,
  ink, ruling, and skins. In winter, heat must prevent frozen ink without
  warping pages or tempting tired workers to move lamps too close.
- **Working day:** The scribe sharpens a quill, rules the page, copies a few
  lines, compares them with the exemplar, and scrapes errors before they spread.
  Hands are rested before fatigue turns one wrong word into a doctrine.
- **Ravenland:** Copying turns weeks of food, lamp oil, trained sight, and
  silence into one more book. A patron often wants permanence. The scribe knows
  that every copy also creates a chance for alteration.

#### Shelter (Barracks)

- **Build:** One carpenter and six laborers can raise a simple barracks with
  bunks, chests, stove, drains, washing place, and latrine access. The residents
  should help fit it, since they will discover every poor peg and narrow aisle.
- **Keep:** The housed hirelings do most daily cleaning under one appointed
  barracks elder or sergeant. The Handyman repairs the building. A cook or
  shared kitchen is needed if meals are part of service.
- **Working day:** Bedding is aired after dawn, the stove cleared, floor swept,
  gear checked, and damp clothing hung where it will not scorch. Evening brings
  meals, mending, watch assignments, noise, and disputes over stolen space.
- **Ravenland:** Shelter tells hirelings they belong to the defense rather than
  lying like tools in the yard. Bunks also make rank visible through distance
  from smoke, door, latrine, and the sergeant's ear.

#### Shrine

- **Build:** Two builders and a priest, elder, or custodian can raise a modest
  shrine over the listed week. The sacred object or place governs the work.
  Some traditions need a roof and altar, while others need a marked tree,
  spring, or standing stone left largely untouched.
- **Keep:** One caretaker visits daily or before each customary rite. No
  permanent priest is required by the function. Flame, offering place, path,
  paint, cloth, living plant, or carved sign is maintained according to faith.
- **Working day:** The keeper sweeps, removes spoiled offerings, renews water or
  flame, and watches for damage that may be weather, insult, or omen. Departures,
  returns, oaths, births, and deaths bring short bursts of communal use.
- **Ravenland:** A shared shrine reveals whether mixed residents can place
  offerings beside one another without claiming the whole sacred place. The
  order in which gifts are removed can matter as much as the order of prayer.

#### Temple

- **Build:** One master builder, one priest, and twelve to twenty laborers can
  raise an ordinary temple over the listed month. Masons, carpenters, smiths,
  painters, and weavers enter at different stages rather than working as one
  crowded crew.
- **Keep:** One priest fulfills the function's rule. A functioning community
  usually adds one or two attendants for cleaning, lamps, guests, stores, and
  records. Great rites borrow cooks, guards, singers, and labor from households.
- **Working day:** The priest opens the sacred place, tends the prescribed sign
  or flame, receives petitioners, teaches or judges, records gifts, and prepares
  the next rite. Attendants clean what worshippers leave behind and keep sacred
  stores from becoming private property.
- **Ravenland:** A temple changes whom residents trust and which obligations
  they call holy. Raven, Heme, kin tradition, and local practice may share a
  settlement without agreeing who can witness an oath or bury a stranger.

#### Theatre

- **Build:** One carpenter and eight laborers can prepare stage, seating,
  shelter, access, and stores. Performers should test sight and sound before the
  final boards are fixed. Cloth scenery and lamps add work for tailor and smith.
- **Keep:** A troupe of four to eight can operate a modest theatre. One keeper
  watches building and stores between shows. Costumes must stay dry, flame must
  be controlled, and damaged boards repaired before the next crowd enters.
- **Working day:** Performers rehearse while others mend clothes, reset props,
  sweep seating, and advertise the evening. After the show, lamps are counted,
  coin gathered, and every joke that drew the wrong silence discussed.
- **Ravenland:** The theatre gives a stronghold public memory that can praise a
  ruler, preserve a local grief, or mock authority within hearing. A familiar
  tale changes when one returning traveler recognizes the villain as himself.

#### Torture Chambers

- **Build:** One mason, one smith, and three laborers can fit the secured room,
  restraints, drains, guarded door, and private approach. The small crew does
  not lessen how widely knowledge of the work travels.
- **Keep:** One executioner operates the function, with two guards controlling
  prisoner and access. Tools, restraints, water, waste, and keys are maintained
  like other assigned equipment. That routine is part of the institution's
  corruption.
- **Working day:** Before use, guards search and bind the prisoner while the
  executioner lays out tools. Afterward, servants remove waste, clean surfaces,
  and carry away a confession whose truth cannot be measured by the suffering
  used to obtain it.
- **Ravenland:** The chamber produces fear and unreliable testimony while
  training ordinary employees to treat cruelty as scheduled work. Cleaning the
  floor does not remove what every resident knows happens behind the door.

#### Town Hall

- **Build:** One carpenter, one mason, and eight laborers can raise an ordinary
  hall with meeting floor, raised seat or common table, waiting space, records,
  secure chest, and messenger access. Residents often supply benches and labor.
- **Keep:** One mayor or lord presides, but a clerk and messenger make daily
  administration possible. Two guards serve on crowded hearing days. The
  Handyman keeps roof and hearth. The clerk keeps decisions from vanishing.
- **Working day:** Petitions are heard at a declared hour. The clerk calls names,
  records witnesses and obligations, and sends messages while claimants wait,
  trade news, and try to speak privately before judgment.
- **Ravenland:** Once a hall exists, people begin asking whether a bargain made
  elsewhere is valid. It turns custom into record, yet those who cannot read may
  still trust a remembered witness more than marks kept behind a locked door.

## Rendering Economy in Play

### Five Questions for Any Settlement

1. What do people eat between now and the next harvest?
2. Which necessary good must come from elsewhere?
3. Who controls the store, tool, crossing, or knowledge that coordinates work?
4. What obligation cannot be settled cleanly with silver?
5. Which road, season, death, or new function is changing the answer?

### Show One Chain Under Pressure

Do not inventory an entire economy at the table. Choose one chain and disturb
it.

- The field yielded, but the millstone cracked.
- The fishermen returned, but the salt tribute left yesterday.
- The forge has iron and no charcoal.
- The warehouse is full, but the bridge has gone downstream.
- The stronghold has coin for wages and no grain anyone will sell.

Each situation gives players several kinds of response. They may repair the
broken link or find a substitute. They can bargain, borrow, or invoke an old
obligation. When time closes those paths, they may ration what remains, steal
what is needed, reroute the supply, or change who controls the bottleneck.

### Sensory Evidence

- Full grain sounds dull beneath a probing stick. Damp grain smells warm.
- A working mill is felt through the floor before it is heard clearly.
- A tannery announces the downwind edge of a settlement.
- A forge roof holds soot even after rain.
- Thin fodder shows in the animals' flanks before the barn is empty.
- A market ending well leaves trampled ground, dung, dropped straw, and fewer
  arguments beside the measures.
- Deferred upkeep appears first as a bucket placed under something.

### Economic NPCs Want Concrete Things

A miller wants grain moving through the mill and the share recognized. A
headwoman wants seed protected without appearing to starve one household for
another. A hawker wants a safe return load. A smith wants charcoal, iron, and
time away from petty repairs. A resident wants proof that the stronghold will
still feed children when the adventurers leave.

Give each actor an asset, a constraint, and a claim another actor considers
unfair. Prices and shortages then become conduct rather than exposition.

### Keep the Economy Harsh but Playable

Ordinary work succeeds. Use detail to reveal preparation and consequence, not
to demand a roll for every loaf, hinge, and bucket. Press the economy when a
decision matters: the season is closing, labor is divided, stores are disputed,
transport is threatened, or a new source of wealth has changed who can command
whom.

Judge the stronghold by what its residents remember. Each wall had builders.
Each store has a key. Smoke rises beside a road that did not exist five years
ago.
