# Dwarven Religion of the Ravenlands

Dwarves worship Huge through sacred work, measured duty, and the promise of
return to another body. This reference holds their cosmology, rites, religious
authority, sacred time, Stonegarden observance, clan doctrine, death, and
anathema. Social standing, household law, contract, and apprenticeship are in
[Dwarven Culture](culture-dwarves.md). Named gods, clans, and cosmology are
manuscript facts. The gathering of the clans once every two years is an
established campaign truth. Detailed rites are grounded setting inference unless
marked as local practice.

---

## Huge and the Dwarven Work

### The World under Construction

Huge made the dwarves to fill the void with matter. The world is a sphere still
under construction, the sun is Huge's Hearth, and the stars are Outer Forges
that await the builders after they reach him. Dwarven labor is cosmological. A
retaining wall, mine stair, road bed, and apparently useless
foundation can all extend the made world against emptiness.

Huge judges skill, knowledge, and duty more readily than inward virtue. The
dead rest and train in a parallel workshop until the god reshapes them for
another shift. A new body may reward service or correct a badly used talent.
The belief dignifies ordinary work and permits brutal judgments about those
declared idle, incompetent, or misplaced.

Dwarven sects disagree over the next layer. Belderranians emphasize royal
direction and stone-song. Meromannians say lost works must be recovered before
new ones begin. Canides defend the surface where growth and weather attack
stone. Crombes keep and guard what others would expand. Each faction can accuse
another of building in the wrong order.

### Faith in Daily Work

A dwarf greets a task by touching raw material, tool, and finished neighboring
work in that order. The gesture places today between what the world supplied
and what earlier hands completed. If no finished work is present, a tool once
used by a teacher may stand in its place.

Measurements carry moral weight. A false measure wastes matter and endangers
the work of people not yet born. Apprentices learn established dimensions with
the names of failures attached: the span that sagged, the brace that split, the
king whose ornamental face weakened a gate.

Scrap is sorted rather than discarded. Stone chips fill voids. Bent iron is
reworked. Ash enters mortar or midden. Material too spoiled for use is carried
to a marked crack and named before disposal, lest waste be hidden as clean
work.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**Opening the Shift** begins a major work. The master strikes the intended
surface once with an unhafted tool, names who ordered the labor, who supplies
it, and who will maintain it. Workers answer with the part they personally
accept. Anyone omitted may later deny liability for failure.

**The Buried Tool** dedicates a foundation. A worn but serviceable tool is laid
where the first course will cover it. Burying a useless tool insults Huge;
dedication must cost future work. Poor crews often bury a good chisel and spend
the next year cursing their piety.

**The Reckoning of Hands** follows collapse or fatal failure. Surviving workers
reconstruct the sequence without regard to rank. Tool marks, fractures,
measurements, and orders are examined. The aim is repair, but blame determines
compensation, office, and whose name is cut from the next stone.

**The Last Shift** prepares the dead. A dwarf is laid beside one unfinished
object and one sound object they helped make. The unfinished piece states that
more work awaits. The sound piece proves what skill should be carried into the
next body. A liar may place another dwarf's work beside the dead. Stone Singers
claim Huge hears the difference.

### Dogmas and Anathemas

Huge's faithful hold that material resists falsehood, competence outranks
boasting, every structure creates a duty of maintenance, and descendants are
real parties to present construction. Completion is holy only when the work can
bear what was promised.

Grave offenses include concealing a structural fault, claiming another hand's
work, destroying sound work for vanity, wasting rare material to display rank,
teaching a dangerous method without its limits, abandoning dependents inside a
failing work, and entering the forbidden elder layers after the clans have
withdrawn their right.

Belial the Loose-Tongued supplies the darkest warning. Secret craft taught
without duty can strengthen the world or arm its enemies. Dwarves disagree
whether the offense was disclosure, betrayal of trust, or failure to control
the result. Every guild chooses the version that protects its own authority.

## Religious Authority

No single priesthood owns Huge. Authority follows demonstrated work. A Stone
Singer can settle questions of depth, resonance, and elder construction; a
master mason speaks on a failed arch; a clan elder rules on inheritance; the
crew that must maintain a drain has standing when a king orders it covered.
Rank still intrudes. A dwarf may be correct and remain unheard because another
clan controls the hall, tools, or food.

A shrine to Huge resembles a workshop kept ready between shifts. It holds
sound tools, measured samples, records of failure, and one piece of work left
open for teaching. Dust on a ceremonial hammer is discrediting. So is a tool
polished until no hand may use it.

Stone Singers preserve claims that ordinary inspection cannot settle. They
listen for worked chambers, hidden faults, and older intentions in stone. Their
judgments carry weight but do not end argument. A Meromannian may hear a lost
mine asking to be reopened where a Crombe hears a sealed danger warning later
hands away.

## Sacred Time and the Work Shift

Dwarven calendars follow work more closely than weather. A major undertaking
has an opening shift, correction shifts, periods when material must settle,
and a closing account. The same quarry may observe different holy days from a
forge two tunnels away because stone and iron impose different pauses.

Huge's Hearth gives the longest public measure. At first sunlight reaching a
new chamber, work stops. The crew marks where light falls, eats there if the
ground is safe, and records whose design admitted it. A chamber that never
receives sun is no less holy. Its light comes through labor, fuel, and mirrors,
all of which must be supplied.

Stars are the Outer Forges. Dwarves working on exposed roads or towers may
begin a difficult joining under a named star, then inspect the seam when that
star returns to the same position. The star does not make poor work sound. It
fixes the time at which excuses end.

## The Gathering Held Every Two Years at Stonegarden

### The Veneration of the Earth

Once every two years the four surface clans gather in the Stone Gardens of the
Canides for the **Veneration of the Earth**. The Canides announce the date early
enough for delegations to cross the longest roads after the thaw and return
before ice closes them.

The gathering lasts five days. Travel may take a season, and some delegations
arrive with wagons of work meant for the bazaar. The Canides provide halls and
water. Fuel and guards keep them usable; measured stalls and kennels order the
gathering. Safe-conduct begins at the foot of the plateau. The expense grants
them precedence as hosts but no additional vote in clan judgment. Canide rulers
have tested that distinction more than once.

Belderranians bring plans bearing royal seals and works chosen to show how one
part belongs inside a greater design. Meromannians bring recovered objects,
mine records, and names from abandoned holds. Canides present work exposed to
root, weather, hoof, and hostile hands. Crombes bring guarded things: locks,
dry weapons, sealed accounts, and objects whose merit lies in having survived.
Each clan arrives prepared to show that its own duty must come first in Huge's
next shift.

The gem-lit gardens make the claim visible. Crystal flowers stand on tourmaline
stems above malachite leaves. The ceiling carries the stars of the Outer Forges.
No clan made the whole garden. A visitor can walk from one disputed account of
dwarven purpose into another without crossing a door.

### The First Day: Opening the Ground

Every delegation carries one stone from work completed since the last gathering
and one fragment taken from a failure. The sound stones are fitted into a low
wall whose courses run through the public gardens. The failed pieces are laid
on tables with the name of the work, the responsible hands, and the repair made
or still owed. A clan that brings no failure is assumed to have concealed one.

At **The Setting of the Four Tools**, each clan places a working tool beside
Huge's bench. The tool must come from current labor. A jeweled hammer made only
for the rite is refused. The Canide host then reads the unfinished obligations
from the previous annal. Debts settled on the road are crossed out before
witnesses. The rest remain audible.

Visiting leaders enter after the tools are placed. They surrender no weapon,
but bind its grip or scabbard with a cord bearing the Canide mark. Drawing it
breaks the host's peace and makes the visitor's whole delegation liable for the
harm. Dwarven weapons remain under the same cord outside the contest grounds.

The first feast is deliberately plain. Each clan supplies one staple and eats
from the same measures used to provision the gathering. A host who stages
abundance while servants dilute their own pot has failed before the games
begin.

### The Second Day: The Open Measures

The first day of games tests craft, art, and wit. Every clan selects several
participants, and the same dwarf may enter more than one trial. Judges must
come from the other clans. The Canides supply standard weights and materials;
competitors may challenge either before beginning.

Craft trials use unfinished or damaged work. A mason may be given a cracked
drain course, a smith an iron bar with a hidden flaw, and a stone singer a wall
whose echo conceals a void. The contestant must state what can be completed in
the allotted time and what must remain undone. Promising an impossible repair
is judged more harshly than finishing little.

The arts trials include carving and metal relief. Shaped crystal and instrument
making test other hands. Patterned armor is judged beside work-songs composed
for a particular object or crew. Beauty without sound construction is treated
as painted rot. Sound work without a thought beyond use may win a buyer and
still lose the judges.

Dwarven wit is tested through song. One singer begins an account from an older
gathering, then stops at a disputed decision. The contestant must continue in
the same measure, distinguish the recorded facts from the clan's preferred
version, and answer an objection sung by another clan. A fine voice helps. A
missing name can lose the trial.

Victors receive tools, rare material, or a commission supplied by the clan that
judged them. The gift opens a duty. A Meromannian who wins Canide iron may spend
the next half-year repairing a Canide road, which is why clans argue over prizes
as fiercely as scores.

### The Third Day: Body, Guard, and the Four Champions

The second day of games begins with contests of load, footing, armor, shield,
and controlled violence. Some resemble work: carrying dressed stone over wet
steps, setting a support while another team shifts the weight, or finding a
stable stance beneath a swinging beam. Others use axe, hammer, shield, and
wrestling on prepared ground.

Contest weapons are inspected and marked. Edges may be keen when both parties
accept them, but a death ends the contest and opens a Reckoning of Hands. Killing
an opponent never counts as victory by itself. Dwarves remember champions who
won the stroke and lost every later hearing.

By midday each of the four clans must elect one champion from its entire
delegation. The choice need not be the strongest fighter or the contestant with
the most victories. A clan may choose the craftswoman who exposed a false
measure, the singer who preserved an enemy's name, or a guard who yielded a
point rather than let a spectator be crushed. The election tells the other
clans what kind of dwarf that clan wants Huge to reshape again.

The four champions enter two semifinals drawn by matched stone lots. Each
semifinal has three measures. The champions inspect an unknown work and name
its first failure; meet in an armed bout; and answer a sung question drawn from
the annals. The first to carry two measures advances. The clans eliminated in
the semifinals choose the order of measures in the final, preventing either
remaining clan from arranging the whole test around its favored skill.

The winner is named **Bearer of the Four Measures** until the next gathering.
The Bearer commands no clan. They may inspect any work claimed as an interclan
obligation, speak first after the hosts at the next gathering, and demand that
one disputed entry be read from the Stonegarden annals. These narrow rights can
delay a war, expose a failing bridge, or ruin a king's prepared speech.

### The Fourth Day: Accounts between Peoples

Clan rulers, guild elders, priests, creditors, and maintainers meet on the
fourth day. They divide shared work and hear compensation claims. They recognize
apprenticeships and exchange hostages or fosterlings where peace requires it.
They settle passage and decide which abandoned structure remains a duty. Contest
victories add standing but never prove a claim. The best singer in Stonegarden
still needs witnesses to a debt.

Leaders of other kin are often invited. Elven guests receive particular
attention because the quarrel between dwarven construction and elven growth
lies beneath many later wars. Orcs may attend as rulers and creditors despite
the history of enslavement. Human lords seek roads and stonework. A wolfkin
speaker may know which surface works survived a winter no dwarf patrolled.

At **The Root and the Course**, an elven keeper and a dwarven mason inspect a
garden bed maintained since the previous gathering. Stone must carry the path
without choking the planted roots. Roots must bind the soil without lifting the
course. Each names one fault belonging to their own people before naming the
other's. They then accept the repair together.

A renewed peace is attached to that bed. The named elf must send warning if
growth threatens the work. The named dwarf must repair water and stone without
cutting the roots for convenience. Other leaders witness the promise and may
claim compensation if either party's failure closes the road. Peace becomes a
maintained object with drains, roots, witnesses, and a place in the next annal.

The rite does not settle every dwarven and elven account. Some delegations use
it to reopen passage while refusing friendship. Others denounce the chosen bed
as a small repair covering an old enslavement or a stolen hill. Refusal is
recorded beside consent.

### The Fifth Day: Closing the Annal

On the fifth morning, four clan annalists and the Canide keeper compare their
records. They enter contest results and judgments. The annals preserve prices,
commissions, deaths, and peace terms as well. Declared failures remain beside
obligations due at the next gathering. A speaker may correct a name or measure
before the record closes. They may not erase an accusation that was heard. The
answer is entered beside it.

The master annal is cut on thin slate leaves and bound at the spine. Working
copies use parchment where it can be obtained. Each clan receives a copy or a
rubbing of the portions that bind it. The master joins the Stonegarden library,
where every gathering has its own place even when a king would prefer the shelf
to contain a gap.

At **The Reading of the Uncarried Load**, the keeper names every duty left
without a willing clan. Silence follows each one. A ruler may accept the load,
divide it, offer payment, or let the annal show that all four clans heard and
refused. The gathering closes only after the Bearer of the Four Measures marks
the first duty to be read when they return.

## Death, Rest, and Return

The dead enter Huge's parallel workshop to rest, train, and await reshaping.
Funeral speech sounds like a shift account. It names work accepted,
work finished, faults admitted, people taught, and maintenance transferred.
Praise without evidence is considered noise before a god of craft.

Bodies may be laid near worked stone, but access and sanitation govern the
choice. A tomb that weakens a living foundation insults Huge. Cremation is
accepted in some forge settlements because fire returns distinction to
material; mining clans more often prefer stone chambers where tools and bone
remain inspectable.

The living do not identify a newborn as a particular returning dead dwarf on
resemblance alone. Repeated craft habits, knowledge never taught, or an exact
fear may begin an inquiry. A clan that declares a prestigious rebirth too
quickly can seize a child, an inheritance, and a political office under one
religious claim.

At **Closing the Bench**, the dead dwarf's tools are divided into four groups:
those safe to use, those needing repair, those fit only for instruction, and
those whose hidden fault makes destruction necessary. The same division is
never applied publicly to the person's life. Dwarves consider that comparison
too easy and usually dishonest.

## Failure, Restitution, and Exclusion

Ordinary faults are repaired through correction. The responsible hand states
the error, supplies labor or material, and leaves a mark where future workers
can find the repair. Concealed repairs are suspect because they deny later
inspectors the history of the load.

Where correction is impossible, compensation follows reliance. A collapsed
wall cost more than stone if it buried grain or forced a winter evacuation.
Huge's account includes displaced work. Guilds that price only the masonry are
protecting masters at the expense of households.

Religious exclusion removes the right to witness measures, dedicate work, or
teach under a guild name. It does not automatically deny food or shelter. Some
halls do so anyway. An excluded master may still possess knowledge everyone
needs, which turns punishment into negotiation.

## Clan Readings of Huge

### Belderranians

Belderranians connect Huge's plan to royal direction. A work gains legitimacy
when it fits a larger design and answers a recognized chain of command. Their
great danger is declaring dissent a loose stone merely because the king dislikes
its shape.

### Meromannians

Meromannians treat abandoned mines, drowned galleries, and buried records as
unfinished accounts. Recovery can take precedence over expansion. They are
accused of letting the dead assign every living shift.

### Canides

Canides keep the surfaces where root, rain, hoof, and frost attack dwarven
work. They venerate inspection under bad conditions and distrust plans drawn
only underground. Their dogma gives unusual standing to scouts, foresters, and
anyone who reports a small exterior fault before it reaches stone.

### Crombes

Crombes make custody a craft. A sealed door, guarded road, and weapon kept dry
are all works that continue without expansion. They know that guarding can
become ownership by habit, and many refuse to discuss when the original owner
has been absent too long.

### Dwelvers

Dwelvers belong to elder work beneath current clan claims. Other dwarves treat
their withdrawals as divine caution, political secrecy, or contempt. Entering
a barred elder layer can be sacrilege, trespass, rescue, or necessary
inspection depending on who tells the account.

## Relations with Other Faiths

Clay receives respect as a shaper, though dwarves dispute elven claims about
who made rubies and orcs. Flow is honored where water must be directed through
work. Conflict begins when Flow keepers call a sound dam an imprisonment or
druids call a quarry wound that should close.

Raven oath practice is intelligible to dwarves because witnesses and reliance
matter. Wyrm's willingness to alter inherited wording troubles guilds that
depend on fixed measures. Rust Brothers imitate the language of iron while
neglecting sound metal. Dwarves notice that rust removes material. Calling the
loss holy does not restore the section.

## Temple Stores, Tithes, and the Price of Sound Work

A shrine to Huge consumes tools, fuel, light, shelf space, trained inspection,
and time taken from other work. These costs are entered openly. A priest who
calls maintenance devotion while leaving another crew to supply it has made
their worship compulsory.

Tithes are commonly paid in measured labor, usable scrap, lamp fuel, records,
or access to a skilled hand. Coin is accepted where it can purchase material.
An ornamental gift that cannot be used or sold may be returned with its weight
and the name of the task that weight could have supplied.

At **Opening the Store**, keepers place a sample from each class of material on
the bench and read the quantity held behind it. Guild and household witnesses
may inspect the scales. Sacred property hidden from count attracts exactly the
theft and favoritism its holiness is supposed to prevent.

Shrine loans carry a repair mark. A household may borrow a tool, brace, lamp, or
reserve of iron and return use rather than the same object. If the loan saved a
roof, the household owes work on another roof. Interest that grows while a
borrower performs necessary maintenance is condemned as a measure detached from
load.

The poorest dwarves often give the most expensive tithe because they contribute
their own shifts. Wealthy masters donate surplus material and retain their time.
Reformers demand that every clan count fatigue, injury, and lost household work
beside stone and metal.

## Stone Singing, Golems, and the Loose Tongue

Stone Singers work where religion, craft, and sorcery cannot be separated. They
hear faults, shape rock, read buried spaces, and preserve techniques ordinary
masons cannot test. Their authority is strongest underground and most dangerous
where no second singer can dispute the hearing.

At **Answering the Wall**, one singer describes what the stone says. A second
names the tool marks, moisture, weight, and ordinary cause that could produce
the same sign. Magic is accepted after craft has had its hearing.

Greater golems bear the old rune for traitor and recall Belial the Loose-Tongued.
To make a body into permanent guard is punishment, construction, and warning.
Dwarven theologians dispute whether the continuing mind serves Huge, suffers
without end, or has become an office no longer identical with the condemned
person.

At a golem station, the keeper records every command attempted and every change
in response. A silent guardian is still a worker under load. Treating it as an
object lets rulers alter instructions without accepting liability for the dead
at the gate.

The story of Belial is used to forbid teaching humans and elves. It is also used
against apprentices, foreign-born dwarves, and rival clans. Critics point out
that a secret locked so tightly no living craftsperson can repair the work has
already served the Iron Dragon.

## Evidence at the Table

Show dwarven belief through a plumb line hung beside a shrine, a repair dated
and signed where humans would hide it, a good chisel buried under the first
course, or an inheritance hearing conducted over tools still warm from use.

A religious dispute should concern a load someone must bear. The sealed mine
contains an ancestor's work and bad air. The bridge dedicated to Huge diverts
Flow from a human field. The heir knows the prayers but not the forge. The
exiled mason is the only person who understands the crack above the hall.
