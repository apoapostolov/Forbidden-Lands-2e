# War, Raiding, Occupation, and Aftermath in the Ravenlands

War reaches a village before the first spear. A messenger asks how many bodies
can leave the harvest. A buyer takes every sound horse; a stranger measures the
bridge. Somewhere, a widow learns that the promised pay exists only on a
captain's board. After the banners leave, bad sleep and unplanted ground remain.
So do disputed plunder and children who learned new silences. Every faction now
knows the road.

Chapter 12 resolves battles and sieges. Chapter 11 moves factions into campaign.
The medieval-authenticity references explain generic tactics and human strain.
This volume joins those systems to Ravenlands society before organized
violence, during a campaign, and through its aftermath.

## Authority and Scope

### Claim Levels

**Published canon** governs named factions and kin. It also governs places,
conflicts, and setting history.

**Manuscript fact** governs current faction and army procedure. Battle and siege
rules belong to it, as do supply, disease, and aftermath.

**Historical model** supplies the premodern levy and camp. It also supplies
logistics, raiding, ransom, occupation, household strain, and reconstruction.

**Setting inference** draws lived consequences from rules. An army consuming
provisions must take them from stores and carts. Animals carry some; people who
expected to eat them lose the rest.

**Personal campaign lore** expands Apostol Apostolov's Ravenlands campaign
without claiming every custom appears in published material.

### Human Scale and Unit Scale

Battle rules aggregate people into troops. This document moves between that
scale and the persons within it.

One lost troop may include dead, wounded, missing, and captured members. Some
scattered. Others fled home. Do not translate every mechanical loss into death.

### Violence with Purpose

Use violence to reveal its objective and institution. Show what it costs and
which relations it changes. Do not linger upon bodily suffering merely to make
a scene seem serious.

Hard truth needs consequence, not ornament.

### No Clean Rear

Premodern war has no separate civilian supply world untouched by campaign.
Fields and mills form the army's rear. Carts and boats connect them to the
force. Animals, workshops, and healers keep it moving; roads and households bear
the cost.

The rear can be distant from battle and still be inside the war.

## War Begins in Peace

### Inventory

Rulers preparing war count grain and animals. They count carts and boats
against the bridges. Weapons require smiths; casualties require healers. Last
comes the count of households capable of service.

The count itself alarms residents. A survey of horses tells every owner that
future need has entered the stable.

### Road Questions

Scouts ask which bridges bear wagons and where water lies. They measure how
quickly a ford rises and note which woods hide movement. They also discover
whose permission opens a gate.

Road repair can be evidence of peace or preparation.

### Purchase

Agents buy food and animals. Leather and iron equip the force; cloth and rope
keep it supplied. Salt preserves what must travel, while medicine follows the
injured. Early purchases may enrich producers. Continued demand strips ordinary
markets.

A price rise warns people before official proclamation.

### Rumor

Rumor assembles from separate facts. A captain is recalled and a marriage
delayed. Smiths work at night while grain remains locked away. Foreigners receive
quarters; a faction's messenger changes horses twice.

Nobody needs the whole plan to know peace is thinning.

### Training

Musters and weapon inspections can appear customary. So can watch drills or
horn practice. Contests hide preparation inside celebration.

The difference lies in frequency, attendance, and whether absent households are
punished.

### Debt

War requires credit before plunder or tribute can repay it. Victory may never
come. Merchants and temples advance goods against future collection; wealthy
households and mercenary captains may do the same.

The creditor acquires an interest in continued campaign success.

### Diplomatic Closure

Safe-conducts narrow and markets exclude certain traders. Rulers request
hostages. Ambiguous border incidents receive harsher interpretation.

The social space in which mistakes can be repaired begins to close.

### Household Anticipation

Families hide valuables and move animals. Some accelerate marriages or finish
roofs. Others preserve extra food, recall travelers, or send vulnerable people
away.

Such preparation can be accused of disloyalty by a ruler who wanted the same
resources.

## Causes and Escalation

### Claim

A ruler may claim land or office. Inheritance can justify either. Another claim
concerns a road, mine, or shrine and the tribute drawn from it. A promise of
protection can become a claim of rule. War begins when enforcement seems more
useful than continued ambiguity.

Claims need people willing to bear their cost.

### Protection

A faction intervenes because dependents were raided, taxed twice, harmed, or
abandoned by another protector.

Protection can be sincere and expansionist at once.

### Feud

Feud escalates when an answer exceeds accepted target, time, or measure. Kin and
allies enter because failure to answer would lower their own standing.

A small killing becomes war through obligation.

### Resource

Pasture, water, timber, metal, ruin salvage, ford, market, and winter quarters
can sustain conflict without any side calling greed its cause.

Resource wars are usually disputes over access and authority as well as matter.

### Religion

Sacred insult, suppressed rite, rival sanction, prohibited practice, or a faith's
claim to rule can make compromise appear betrayal of the world itself.

Religious leaders also possess means of restraint if doctrine provides lawful
peace.

### Fear

One faction mobilizes because another might. Defensive preparation becomes the
evidence that makes the feared attack rational.

Escalation can occur without deceit.

### Opportunity

Weak succession, failed harvest, distant army, lifted Mist, new route, faction
split, or monster attack can make an old claim suddenly actionable.

Leaders later describe opportunity as destiny.

### Spoilers of Peace

Raiders, hardline priests, creditors, mercenaries, ambitious heirs, dispossessed
exiles, and officials appointed for emergency may lose through peace.

They can spoil talks through incident rather than argument.

### The Last Reversible Acts

Before campaign, several acts remain reversible: compensation, hostage exchange,
shared investigation, boundary walk, truce, withdrawal, dismissal of a captain,
or public correction of an insult.

Show at least one such act before war. Its failure gives conflict shape.

## Muster

### The Call

Muster travels by messenger, horn call, beacon, priest, market proclamation,
clan word, written order, and household relay.

The form establishes legitimacy. A forged horn call can move bodies before
truth catches it.

### Reading the Obligation

Households ask who must go, for how long, with what equipment, under whom, fed by
whom, and what exemptions custom allows.

“Send one fighter” does not answer which indispensable worker leaves.

### Selection

Households choose volunteers, obligated adults, younger dependents, substitutes,
or the person least damaging to lose.

Selection exposes family hierarchy. The favored child may be protected or sent
to win honor.

### Equipment Inspection

Spears, shields, bows, armor, packs, shoes, blankets, vessels, rope, and tools
are inspected before movement.

Weapons kept as heirlooms may prove rotten at the grip. A sound shoe may matter
sooner than a fine sword.

### Farewell

Farewell includes food, repair, prayer, sexual intimacy, argument, instruction,
gift, debt, and the distribution of household work if the fighter does not
return.

People often avoid final language because naming death feels like invitation.

### Assembly

The muster point requires water, animal space, food, latrines, officers,
scribes, healers, and a way to sort arrivals.

Delay consumes supplies before the army moves.

### Absence

Some obligated people do not appear because of illness, fear, household need,
political refusal, poor information, or deliberate evasion.

Treating all absence as cowardice loses the reasons a levy exists.

### Substitution

Rich households may equip substitutes. Poor ones send their own labor.

A capable substitute can serve well while increasing the sense that obligation
falls unequally.

### Blessing and Oath

Rite binds the gathered force to purpose, commander, limits, and expected return.

An oath should say what soldiers are forbidden to do as clearly as whom they are
ordered to fight.

### The First Night

The first night away reveals sleeping arrangements, food quality, officer
competence, theft, fear, and whether units contain people who know one another.

An army begins forming before it meets an enemy.

## Levy, Retainers, and Mercenaries

### Levy

Levy fighters carry settlement obligations into war. Their strength lies in
existing social bonds and knowledge of local country.

Their weakness is that every day away consumes the household economy that
equipped them.

### Retainers

Retainers receive continuing maintenance, status, equipment, and closeness to a
lord or institution. They train, guard, enforce, and provide the core around
which levy can gather.

Their loyalty attaches to household and pay as much as to a banner.

### Mercenaries

Mercenaries sell organized competence. Their contract, company name, internal
discipline, camp household, and expectation of pay distinguish them from an
armed crowd.

Late pay shifts their relation from service toward self-payment.

### Mixed Hosts

Most substantial armies mix sources of force. Retainers hold a line, levy adds
numbers, riders screen, scouts guide, specialists manage engines, and mercenaries
cover gaps.

Different expectations of pay, plunder, honor, and service produce internal
politics.

### Veterans among Levy

Levy is not synonymous with inexperienced. Hunters, guards, raiders, former
mercenaries, and survivors of prior campaigns may stand within household units.

Commanders who identify them can improve cohesion. Commanders who humiliate
them waste local trust.

### Company Law

Mercenary companies judge theft, desertion, brawling, plunder, watch failure,
and disputes with camp followers through their own procedure.

Patrons need to know where company law conflicts with local law.

### Pay

Pay arrives in coin, goods, collection rights, food, quarters, plunder share,
land promise, or future settlement.

Every noncoin form makes soldiers creditors of somebody's place.

### Dismissal

Dismissed troops need food and a route home. Sending armed unpaid people into a
district is not the end of military responsibility.

The dissolving host can become the war's last raid.

## The War Household

### Labor Removed

The fighter's absence changes plowing, herding, craft, care, watch, courtship,
authority, and who sleeps near the door.

One absent adult can reorganize the whole house.

### Labor Added

Remaining people must produce supplies for the army, host messengers, maintain
watch, care for dependents, and respond to prices or requisition.

War increases work while removing workers.

### The Waiting Person

Waiting consists of ordinary tasks interrupted by every road sound. Rumor
creates grief and hope repeatedly before any reliable word arrives.

People may resent the absent fighter for choosing, obeying, surviving, or not
returning quickly enough.

### Letters and Tokens

Messages are carried by officials, companions, traders, priests, deserters, and
chance travelers. A token can authenticate the sender and announce death before
the words are spoken.

Silence may mean no messenger rather than bad news.

### Household Defense

People left behind hide goods, organize watches, move animals, reinforce doors,
and negotiate with passing troops.

Weapon scarcity may be greatest after the levy departs.

### Pregnancy and Birth

War separates partners, creates hurried unions, delays recognition, and leaves
birth households without expected help.

Return can bring joy alongside uncertainty about intimacy, inheritance, and the
time of conception.

### Children

Children inherit tasks, partial explanations, admiration, terror, and games
imitating what adults fear.

They may recognize banners before letters.

### The Missing

A missing fighter leaves property, marriage, debt, and authority unresolved.
Declaring death too early wrongs the survivor. Waiting indefinitely traps the
household.

Local custom needs a time and evidence standard.

### The Returning Stranger

A familiar person may return with altered sleep, speech, appetite, injury,
religion, loyalty, wealth, or silence.

Homecoming requires mutual relearning.

## Roads, Supply, and Requisition

### Armies Eat Roads

Movement wears surfaces, bridges, animals, wheels, shoes, and the patience of
settlements along the way.

An army can ruin a route without an enemy touching it.

### Provision Load

Grain, flour, dried food, animals, fodder, salt, drink, fuel, cookware, tents,
tools, medicine, and spare equipment require carts, packs, boats, or dispersed
carrying.

Every carrier also consumes.

### Fodder

Mounted force magnifies reach and supply. Horses and draft animals need water
and rest each day. Grass may sustain them in good country; hay and grain must
follow them when grazing fails. Hooves need care, and losses demand replacement.

A district can feed people longer than it can feed a large concentration of
animals.

### Requisition

Requisition converts household goods into military necessity. Officers inspect,
assess, seize, promise payment, and punish concealment.

Taking seed, breeding animals, or roof timber consumes future production.

### Purchase under Force

Payment does not make exchange voluntary when armed buyers set price and forbid
refusal.

Still, a receipt may matter later. Residents preserve it as claim against the
victor or successor.

### Foraging

Foragers gather dispersed food, fodder, fuel, and information. Uncontrolled
foraging becomes pillage and scatters soldiers vulnerable to ambush.

Local guides can direct foragers toward genuine surplus or a rival's fields.

### Supply Line

A supply line is a repeated relation among source, transport, route, escort,
store, and receiving unit.

Cutting one bridge, ferry, animal pool, or political agreement can break it.

### Depots

Depots concentrate goods and attract theft, fire, vermin, sabotage, corruption,
and attack.

Their clerks decide which unit eats first.

### Water

Watering a host requires access, sequence, clean upstream use, animal separation,
and vessels. Mud forms rapidly around a crowded source.

Poison fear can halt an army even when the water is sound.

### Waste

Dung, offal, blood, spoiled food, broken gear, latrine, and dead animals mark the
route behind a force.

Poor waste discipline converts supply into disease.

### Civilians on the Road

Refugees, traders, camp followers, messengers, pilgrims, and ordinary carts
compete with military priority.

Closing the road to civilians may also prevent food from reaching the army.

## Camps

### Choosing Ground

A camp needs water, drainage, fuel, defensible approach, animal space, latrine
ground, supply access, and enough distance from disease or flood.

The most defensible hill may have no water.

### Layout

Units, command, stores, animals, healers, workshops, followers, prisoners, and
latrines need known positions.

Order reduces night panic and time lost finding people.

### Building Camp

Arriving tired does not remove the need to dig, clear, pitch, gather fuel, fetch
water, cook, post watch, tend animals, and inspect equipment.

Routine is a form of morale.

### Camp Fire

Fire cooks, warms, lights, dries, signals, and spreads. Fire discipline sets
where a fire may burn, how large it may grow, who watches it, and when it is put
out.

Hidden camps eat cold or use screened flame.

### Latrines

Latrines go downhill and downstream from clean water where ground permits. They
must be marked, screened, filled, and moved before saturation.

Night access must be safe enough that soldiers do not foul nearer ground.

### Animals

Animals need picket lines, guards, water sequence, fodder, grooming, and space
away from panic or disease.

One frightened animal can wake and injure a camp.

### Camp Market

Sutlers and local traders sell food, drink, clothing, repair, entertainment, and
credit. Prices rise with distance and danger.

Commanders regulate the market because debt, drunkenness, theft, and shortage
affect discipline.

### Camp Followers

Followers cook, wash, nurse, repair, trade, carry, entertain, bury, and maintain
relationships that keep soldiers human.

They also need protection and voice in movement decisions that endanger them.

### Watch

Watch duty fails through exhaustion, cold, hunger, poor relief, isolation,
confusing orders, and the belief that nothing happened on prior nights.

Good officers inspect sentries without humiliating them.

### Alarm

Alarm procedure names signal, assembly point, equipment, animal response, fire,
prisoners, and who remains with stores.

Without procedure, armed sleepers become a danger to one another.

### Moving On

Breaking camp requires inventory, burial or transport of dead, waste handling,
fire extinction, animal check, rear guard, and recovery of stragglers.

The abandoned site tells enemies how disciplined the force was.

## Scouting, Intelligence, and Guides

### Scouts

Scouts read track, smoke, birds, road wear, water use, camp residue, distant
sound, and the behavior of local people.

Their task is to return with useful distinction rather than seek combat.

### Screen

A moving screen prevents the enemy from observing the main force freely. It also
warns residents that an army is near before officers can explain intent.

Poor screens steal and create enemies faster than they gather news.

### Guides

Guides know route, custom, water, shelter, danger, and which apparent shortcut
crosses somebody's sacred or political boundary.

Coerced guides can obey literally and still ruin a campaign.

### Local Informants

Residents report from loyalty, money, fear, revenge, coercion, or the hope of
directing violence elsewhere.

Commanders weigh each report against motive and access.

### Interrogation

Captives know only what their position allowed them to see. Pain produces speech
faster than accuracy.

Compare routes, timings, unit marks, food, officer names, and physical evidence.

### Spies

Spies travel as trader, pilgrim, laborer, deserter, refugee, entertainer,
messenger, or local dependent.

The cover works because the social role already moves information.

### Maps

Maps record selected routes, relations, and landmarks. They age as bridges fall,
water shifts, factions change, and vegetation returns.

A map without local interpretation can guide an army into old truth.

### Signals

Horn, fire, smoke, mirror, flag, runner, bird, drum, and magic extend command.

Signals require shared code and can be copied, delayed, or misunderstood.

### Counterintelligence

Counterintelligence controls route knowledge, watches strangers, varies orders,
uses countersigns, and notices questions unusual for a person's claimed work.

Suspicion applied to everyone destroys the candid reports commanders need.

## Raiding

### Purpose

Raids seize goods, animals, prisoners, information, and prestige; destroy
capacity; provoke response; enforce claim; or feed a force.

The objective determines speed and acceptable risk.

### Preparation

Raiders study the watch and the households likely to aid or betray it.
They learn where animals and portable goods are kept. Moon and weather govern
the approach; gates and escape routes govern the leaving.

Local complicity often matters more than stealth at the last wall.

### Approach

The force travels light, avoids known roads, controls sound, and chooses a route
that permits withdrawal under load.

The best approach can be useless if driven animals cannot follow it back.

### Breach

Raiders enter through surprise, help from within, threat of fire, a weak gate, a
climbed wall, or violence concentrated before defenders gather.

Delay converts raid into battle.

### Taking Animals

Animals scatter, resist strangers, require handlers, and move at their own pace.
Known calls or captured herders can be more valuable than another fighter.

Driving breeding stock injures future years.

### Fire

Fire creates confusion, light, terror, and uncontrollable loss. A raider seeking
portable wealth may destroy it through careless burning.

Fire also marks the raiders' route for distant response.

### Prisoners

Prisoners slow withdrawal and require guards. They may be intended for ransom,
exchange, labor, information, hostage, or terror.

The reason for taking them should be known before the raid.

### Extraction

Successful extraction depends upon rally point, load discipline, rear guard,
route denial, remounts, and the decision about what to abandon when pursuit
closes.

Greed kills raiders during the return.

### Defense against Raid

Lookouts, dogs, refuge, alarm, distributed stores, animal night placement,
barriers, rapid assembly, and neighboring signals reduce loss.

No defense protects every field and building equally.

### After a Raid

Residents count missing people before goods, pursue only if force and route
permit, tend injury, move surviving animals, watch for return, and send word.

The political question follows: who was supposed to protect them?

## Battle as Lived Event

### Before Contact

Fighters wait, adjust gear, empty bowels, drink, pray, joke badly, become silent,
and search faces for signs that others will remain.

Experienced people perform routine because thought has become unreliable.

### Formation

Formation places bodies close enough for mutual protection and command. It also
limits individual escape and sight.

The fighter knows the shoulders nearby better than the general's plan.

### Sound

Commands compete with feet, shields, animals, missiles, horns, screams, weather,
and the body's own breath.

Plans requiring precise speech after contact are fragile.

### Visibility

Dust, smoke, bodies, terrain, helmets, rain, darkness, and fear narrow what a
person can know.

Witnesses can honestly remember incompatible battles.

### Leadership

Immediate leaders point, repeat, stand where seen, restore small order, and know
when a unit can still obey.

Authority without presence becomes rumor during contact.

### Battle Fear

Fear tightens hands, empties thought, sharpens one detail, distorts time, and
makes bodily urges urgent.

Courage is continued useful action while afraid, supported by routine and other
people.

### Wounds

Many wounds disable through pain, blood loss, fracture, shock, impaired movement,
or fear before killing.

Comrades decide whether to carry, protect, mark, or leave the wounded under
pressure.

### Cohesion

Units hold when people believe neighbors remain, leaders perceive reality,
retreat is possible, and purpose still justifies danger.

They break when isolation becomes the dominant fact.

### Victory

Victory at the fighting point may be understood only when enemy pressure stops.
Fighters then discover losses, separation, thirst, injury, and orders for
pursuit.

Relief can become dangerous disorder.

## Siege and Blockade

### Invest the Place

An investing force controls approaches, tests walls, establishes camp, finds
water, watches gates, and decides whether to storm, starve, bargain, mine, burn,
or wait.

The defenders begin by counting mouths and time.

### The Defended Population

A fortified place contains garrison, residents, refugees, prisoners, officials,
animals, servants, healers, and people who arrived too late to leave.

Their interests diverge as stores decline.

### Stores

Grain, water, fuel, fodder, salt, medicine, missile, repair material, lamp oil,
and clean cloth determine endurance.

Hidden private stores become public moral questions under siege.

### Ration

Rationing begins with inventory and an argument about need. Fighters, workers,
children, pregnant people, sick people, animals, officers, and wealthy households
all claim priority.

Unequal rations may preserve capacity or expose privilege.

### Siege Water

A well inside the wall is not automatically safe or sufficient. Draw rate,
contamination, queues, sabotage, and the labor of carrying water upward matter.

Fire and sickness increase demand.

### Walls

Walls require continuous inspection, repair, bracing, debris clearing, missile
supply, and relief of exhausted defenders.

One breach can be contained by an inner obstacle if labor and material remain.

### Engines and Bombardment

Siege engines consume timber, rope, metal, stone, skill, space, and time. Their
missiles damage morale and buildings beyond the point struck.

Residents learn the rhythm between shots.

### Mining

Attackers dig toward foundations. Defenders listen through vessels, floors, and
stone, dig countermines, flood, smoke, or collapse passages.

Mining war is cramped craft conducted beneath people who cannot see it.

### Fire Attack

Fire arrows, heated material, incendiaries, and ordinary sparks threaten roofs,
stores, workshops, and panic.

Defenders organize water, hooks, demolition, watch, and clear movement before
the attack.

### Disease

Crowding, waste, poor water, vermin, dead bodies, smoke, and hunger make disease
a second besieger.

Quarantine consumes space already scarce.

### Sally

Defenders sally to burn engines, gather food, break works, escort messengers, or
restore morale.

The gate remains the dangerous point. A successful attack that cannot reenter
has failed.

### Blockade

Blockade can be loose enough for smugglers, small boats, hidden paths, and local
knowledge to matter.

Every successful passage becomes a route the besieger tries to close next.

### Civilians Leaving

Defenders may expel mouths. Besiegers may refuse passage to force them back.

People trapped between lines reveal the moral structure of both commands.

### Terms

Terms can address lives, weapons, property, garrison departure, hostages,
religion, officers, arrears, and protection from sack.

Vague terms preserve room for the victor to reinterpret mercy.

### Storm

Storming a breach produces concentrated violence, confusion, and rapid collapse
of distinctions between fighter and resident.

Commanders who want restraint must prepare units and objectives before entry.

### Relief

A relief force changes besieger posture, defender morale, and the timing of
every ration. Hope can provoke a premature sally.

Signals must be authenticated.

### Siege End

After surrender or relief, the living must bury the dead and contain any fire.
Food and disease cannot wait for a settlement of property. Disarmament and the
release of prisoners lead to the immediate question: who keeps the gate tonight?

The wall's ownership changes faster than the life within it.

## Magic and Monsters in War

### Magical Scarcity

Casters are scarce, carry limited willpower, require ingredients, and face
mishaps. An army cannot treat them as endless artillery.

Their greatest value may be communication, healing, scouting, weather judgment,
repair, concealment, or one decisive working.

### Magical Planning

Commanders identify purpose, target, casting mode, witnesses, protection,
ingredient, evacuation, and probable mishap before battle.

A caster ordered forward without preparation becomes a hazard to both lines.

### Safe and Chance Casting

Safe casting favors reliable limited effect where friendly troops stand close.
Chance casting guarantees mishap and should correspond to grave necessity.

Rank 6 workings make the mishap part of the operational plan.

### Healing

Magical healing returns some wounded people to function and cannot replace
triage, sanitation, splinting, food, rest, nursing, or rehabilitation.

Officers may pressure healers to prioritize fighters likely to rejoin battle.

### Shapeshifting and Awareness

Scouting paths can cross terrain, observe, communicate, and penetrate screens in
ways ordinary scouts cannot.

Countermeasures make animals, dreams, unexpected visitors, and private speech
objects of suspicion.

### Symbolism and Fortification

Symbols can defend, trap, mark, and persist. Their location must be known to
friendly troops and successors.

A captured gate may remain an enemy weapon after its defenders leave.

### Stone and Element

Stone Song, Elemental Magic, Ice Affinity, Nature, and Magma Song can alter route,
wall, ground, water, fire, weather, and concealment.

Material changes outlast the tactical moment and affect residents afterward.

### Mental and Dream War

Mentalism and Oneiromancy attack confidence, memory, sleep, information, and
command. Their suspected use can damage an army even when no spell was cast.

Leaders require verification procedures that do not paralyze every order.

### Blood, Death, and Demonic War

Prohibited disciplines offer rulers coercive power, terror, expendable bodies,
and weapons whose consequences exceed the campaign.

Their use should create political and moral aftermath. A stronger troop is not
enough.

### Monsters as Troops

Monsters require motive, restraint, food or other maintenance, handlers,
containment, route, and an answer for what happens when the target is gone.

Fear of the handler is not permanent control.

### Monster Shock

Troops encountering an unfamiliar monster face scale, sound, smell, attack form,
and the collapse of assumptions about cover or armor.

Experienced leaders give one concrete instruction rather than demand abstract
bravery.

### After Magical War

Altered ground, persistent symbols, unnatural weather, tainted remains, restless
dead, contaminated water, and rumors require investigation and cleansing.

The army that created the harm may be gone before residents understand it.

---

## Captives, Ransom, and Surrender

### Why Take Captives

Captives provide ransom, exchange, labor, information, hostage, prestige, and a
means to end fighting without killing everyone who loses.

The practice depends upon credible expectation that surrender preserves life.

### Yielding

Surrender may be signaled by dropped weapon, raised empty hands, named words,
kneeling, withdrawal to a place, or an officer's formal act.

Different traditions can misread one another at deadly speed.

### Accepting Surrender

The accepting fighter must control immediate anger, disarm the captive, search,
separate dangerous individuals, and protect the prisoner from comrades.

Command needs a known rule before battle.

### Captive Categories

Officers, skilled specialists, wealthy people, kin of importance, ordinary
fighters, coerced levy, camp followers, spies, and prohibited casters carry
different value and risk.

Category can be manipulated by clothing and false claim.

### Ransom

Ransom depends upon recognition, messenger, guarantor, price, payment route, and
the captive's household or patron accepting obligation.

A person valuable in reputation may belong to a cash-poor house.

### Parole

Parole releases a captive under oath not to fight, to obtain payment, or to
return if terms fail.

Breaking parole injures the practice that may preserve future captives.

### Exchange

Exchange requires matched value, safe meeting, lists, identification, and a
procedure for missing or injured people.

Families may pressure commanders to trade strategically minor prisoners first.

### Prison Camps

Captives need food, water, shelter, sanitation, guards, work, medical care, and
protection from private revenge.

Guarding them removes fighters from other tasks.

### Captive Interrogation

Question separately, establish what the captive could know, compare time and
material detail, and offer bounded incentive.

Torture gives captors a feeling of action while damaging information and future
surrender.

### Captive Labor

Prisoners may bury dead, repair roads, clear camps, carry loads, or work fields.

Labor under captivity is coercive even when it receives food. Dangerous work
and private exploitation require command responsibility.

### Execution

Execution may answer espionage, atrocity, prohibited magic, failed ransom,
political terror, or inability to hold prisoners.

Inability is an explanation of command failure, not moral cleansing.

### Rescue

Rescue requires information about site, routine, guards, captive condition,
route, and what happens to prisoners not chosen.

Freeing one famous person can doom the remainder if the plan ignores them.

### Return

Returned captives face suspicion of cooperation, debt for ransom, bodily harm,
shame, and changed household roles.

Survival in captivity does not require apology.

---

## Plunder and Distribution

### What Becomes Plunder

Weapons, armor, animals, carts, food, cloth, coin, household goods, tools,
documents, sacred objects, ingredients, and captives may be claimed.

Each object belonged within a life before becoming loot.

### Battlefield Salvage

Immediate salvage removes useful gear from dead and wounded under time pressure.

Taking a weapon may be necessary. Taking clothing needed to identify and bury a
body carries another cost.

### Rules of Share

Armies divide by rank, unit, deed, prior contract, communal pool, commander
choice, or first possession.

Unclear rules turn victory into internal violence.

### Commander's Share

The commander may claim valuable captives, banners, strategic documents, rare
weapons, and a portion for common expenses.

Taking too much teaches soldiers to conceal finds.

### Sacred and Kin Property

Temples, graves, heirlooms, clan objects, and magical texts may be materially
valuable and politically ruinous to distribute.

Restitution can purchase peace more effectively than sale.

### Captured Animals

Captured animals need immediate handling, fodder, identification, and
distribution. Owners may recognize them later.

Keeping a stolen breeding animal can prolong feud for generations.

### Documents

Letters, tallies, seals, maps, contracts, and grimoires can outweigh silver.

The soldier who finds them may not recognize value and may use them for kindling.

### Pillage

Pillage is seizure outside agreed military distribution. It rewards immediate
initiative and destroys command, civilian trust, supply discipline, and future
tax base.

Officers sometimes permit it as substitute for pay.

### Buying Loot

Camp merchants purchase bulky goods cheaply because soldiers cannot carry them.
This converts household property into portable coin and spreads it far from
possible restitution.

### Return with Wealth

Sudden plunder changes household standing, invites claims, and forces a moral
account of origin.

A decorated cup may be recognized by a refugee at the victor's table.

## Atrocity, Restraint, and Command

### Atrocity Is Organized through Permission

Mass harm requires orders, tolerated rumor, dehumanizing category, opened gates,
removed guards, supplied fire, selected targets, and people who understand that
punishment will not follow.

It is rarely an inexplicable instant of madness.

### Directed Terror

Factions may torture or execute selected people to make a larger population
submit. Limitation does not redeem the practice. It makes terror administrative.

The survivor is expected to carry the lesson.

### Collective Punishment

Burning houses, taking hostages, seizing food, or killing livestock for one
person's act makes the community police itself.

It also recruits people who had no prior cause to resist.

### Sexual Violence

Sexual violence can accompany conquest, occupation, captivity, coercive exchange,
and the collapse or deliberate suspension of discipline.

Handle it through survivor consequence, institutional responsibility, and
careful table boundaries. Do not use it as scenery or motivation shorthand.

### Loot Permission

Promised sack tells fighters that civilian property and bodies are part of their
pay. Commanders cannot later pretend surprise at the result.

Revoking the promise at the gate may provoke mutiny.

### Restraint

Restraint requires clear orders, credible punishment, adequate pay and food,
officers present, protected surrender, safe places for civilians, and a campaign
aim compatible with people remaining alive.

Mercy without structure lasts only as long as individual temper.

### Refusal

Fighters refuse orders through protest, delay, misinterpretation, warning,
desertion, protection of targets, mutiny, or violence against the commander.

Refusal carries risk and should not be expected equally from the least powerful.

### Witness

Witnesses remember insignia, phrases, officers, sequence, and people who tried
to stop harm. Their testimony may later threaten the victorious faction.

Protecting witnesses is part of aftermath.

### Command Responsibility

Commanders are responsible for orders, foreseeable consequences, known patterns,
discipline, supply, and response after discovery.

Punishing one low-ranking perpetrator cannot answer policy.

### Representation at the Table

State the nature and consequence of atrocity without dwelling upon exploitative
detail. Center the people harmed as persons with relations and futures.

Give players choices that can change what happens next. They may prevent harm,
shelter those threatened, or extract people already trapped. They can document
the crime, expose its perpetrators, and seek judgment. Healing and repair answer
what the judgment cannot restore.

## Retreat, Rout, and Pursuit

### Ordered Retreat

An ordered retreat requires signal, rear guard, route, preserved intervals,
wounded policy, baggage decision, and a destination capable of receiving the
force.

Turning away from an enemy is among the hardest collective maneuvers.

### Rear Guard

The rear guard purchases time through controlled resistance and must know when
and how to disengage.

Glory that leaves it abandoned is command failure.

### Baggage

Wagons, stores, followers, wounded, prisoners, and animals slow retreat.
Abandoning them may save fighters while destroying the army's future and moral
cohesion.

The decision should have a named author.

### Rout

Rout begins when enough people believe formation and command no longer offer a
survivable future.

Individuals flee toward familiar ground, open road, cover, water, horse, or the
place where they last saw comrades.

### Panic

Panic spreads through movement and interpretation. One unit withdrawing under
orders can appear to another as proof of collapse.

Signals and visible reserves can interrupt the contagion.

### Pursuit

Pursuit converts victory into captures and killing while scattering the victor,
exhausting animals, and moving troops away from command.

Disciplined pursuit requires limits and recall.

### Refuge

Defeated people seek woods, ruins, farmsteads, shrines, rivers, and friendly
settlements. Residents must decide whether to hide them and invite reprisal.

An army can dissolve into the population that supplied it.

### Rally

Rally points, familiar leaders, banners, horns, water, and defensible ground let
fragments become a force again.

The first task is counting and restoring order, not shaming people for flight.

### Stragglers

Injured, exhausted, lost, burdened, and frightened people arrive for days. Some
carry crucial news. Others loot under the name of the defeated army.

Settlements need a procedure for disarming and feeding them.

## The Battlefield after Fighting

### Silence

The battlefield does not become silent at once. Wounded people call, animals
struggle, small fires burn, gear settles, and scattered fighting continues.

The change is from organized noise to separate suffering.

### Security

Before care, commanders determine whether the enemy may return, whether weapons
are surrendered, and which routes remain dangerous.

Overcaution delays lifesaving work. Careless celebration invites reversal.

### Triage

Healers sort by immediacy, survivability, transport, available skill, and the
number of hands required.

Friends experience necessary triage as personal rejection.

### Searching

Search parties identify units, recover missing people, collect wounded, and mark
dead. They need lists, companions, light, and protection.

After dark, recognition becomes uncertain.

### Enemy Wounded

Treatment of enemy wounded reflects surrender policy, resources, revenge, and
future reputation.

Care can create prisoners and witnesses rather than friends.

### Dead

Bodies need identification, protection from stripping beyond necessity, removal
from water, record, burial or burning according to custom, and return of tokens
where possible.

Weather and numbers may make full rite impossible.

### Battlefield Animals

Wounded animals require capture, treatment, or killing. Loose animals can injure
searchers and represent needed transport.

Owners may be dead, missing, or on the opposing side.

### Salvage and Evidence

Weapons and armor are valuable. Positions, missile direction, wounds, and broken
orders can also explain what occurred.

Immediate stripping can destroy evidence of atrocity or treachery.

### Ground Contamination

Blood, bodies, waste, fire, magic, disturbed soil, and spoiled stores affect
water, animals, crops, and supernatural danger.

Nearby residents inherit the site.

### Returning Names

Lists of dead, wounded, missing, and captured are difficult to make and more
difficult to carry home.

Errors create false grief and false hope.

## Occupation

### Seizing the Working Points

Occupiers secure gate, bridge, ferry, mill, granary, well, market, shrine,
archive, stables, and messenger routes.

Controlling the hall without these points produces a banner, not rule.

### Disarmament

Weapons are surrendered, registered, hidden, or exempted by office. Tools blur
the category.

Complete disarmament leaves residents unable to defend animals and fields from
ordinary danger.

### Quartering

Troops enter houses, barns, halls, ruins, and camps. Their maintenance becomes a
daily relation with residents.

Concentrated barracks improve discipline and strain supply. Dispersed quartering
spreads burden and surveillance.

### Curfew

Curfew simplifies watch. It makes birth, illness, fire response, animal care,
secret worship, and ordinary movement grounds for arrest.

Permits turn clerks into gatekeepers of necessity.

### Hostages

Hostages secure compliance and burden selected households with the behavior of a
whole settlement.

Their treatment reveals whether occupation seeks stable submission or terror.

### Local Government

Occupiers retain existing elders, appoint collaborators, govern through officers,
or combine them.

Every choice trades legitimacy, knowledge, control, and dependence.

### Tax and Requisition

Occupation must feed itself. The occupier counts stores after war has already
damaged them.

Taking too much produces concealment, flight, resistance, and future famine.

### Law

Military security law and local customary law overlap. Theft from a soldier,
assault, land, marriage, debt, and resistance may be judged in different courts.

People choose jurisdictions strategically.

### Religious Control

Occupiers may demand public rites, close shrines, seize sacred objects, appoint
priests, or offer toleration for loyalty.

Private practice becomes a measure of resistance.

### Intimacy

Occupation creates coercive proximity, attachments, marriages, trade, assault,
children, and relations whose meaning cannot be decided only by later politics.

Power must remain visible when judging consent.

### Departure

An occupier leaves garrisons, debts, graves, damaged works, local officers,
mixed households, collaborators, and habits of command.

Liberation begins another struggle over those remains.

---

## Collaboration and Resistance

### Necessary Administration

Some residents keep water, grain, burial, sanitation, and judgment functioning
under whoever holds force.

Their cooperation may preserve the settlement without endorsing occupation.

### Profiting

Others use access to seize rivals' goods, gain office, monopolize permits, settle
feuds, or purchase confiscated property.

Survival and ambition can inhabit the same person.

### Informing

Informants act from belief, fear, coercion, pay, revenge, protection, or a desire
to direct punishment away from kin.

The information may still be accurate.

### Everyday Resistance

Delay, miscount, concealment, false direction, tool loss, song, secret rite,
sanctuary, and refusal to remember make occupation costly.

Small acts depend upon many people not exposing one another.

### Armed Resistance

Fighters need food, shelter, intelligence, medicine, replacement, and a political
relation with residents.

If they take these through fear, resistance becomes a second occupation.

### Reprisal

Occupiers punish communities to isolate fighters. Fighters may punish
collaborators to isolate occupiers.

Residents become the ground upon which both seek control.

### Double Service

Messengers, brokers, guards, and local officers may serve both sides, whether by
design, divided loyalty, or the need to survive every possible victor.

Evidence after war will rarely show the whole arrangement.

### Liberation Justice

After occupation, communities distinguish leadership, cruelty, profit,
administration, coerced labor, intimate relation, youth, and acts that reduced
harm.

Collective accusation is emotionally simple and politically destructive.

### Amnesty

Amnesty can prevent endless purge and conceal powerful offenders among ordinary
accommodators.

Its terms should name exclusions, restitution, testimony, and protection from
private revenge.

---

## Refugees and Displacement

### Decision to Leave

Households leave after weighing danger, road, destination, stores, animals,
elderly kin, children, season, and the risk of being treated as suspicious
strangers.

Departure is often delayed until options are worse.

### What Is Carried

People carry food, water, clothing, tools, bedding, documents, religious objects,
children, small valuables, seed, and whatever animals can move.

Every choice abandons another part of home.

### The Road

Displaced groups move slowly, need water and sanitation, attract trade and theft,
and struggle to maintain information about separated kin.

Military traffic may deny them priority.

### Arrival

Host settlements fear disease and ask whether food and space will last.
They seek kin ties or useful labor that might absorb the arrivals. Factional
danger grows with every sign that the stay will be long.

Emergency hospitality becomes politics if return remains impossible.

### Camp Life

Temporary camps need drainage, water, latrines, fuel, cooking, watch, childcare,
burial, and representation.

Without authority, the strongest distributors acquire power.

### Skill and Work

Refugees bring craft, animal knowledge, language, route, magic, and distant
relations. Giving them only relief wastes capacity and deepens dependence.

Existing workers may fear competition.

### Property

Abandoned land and houses attract occupiers, neighbors, factions, and later
resettlers. Use during absence can become claim.

Return requires evidence and a settlement capable of enforcing it.

### Separated Children

Children may be fostered, lost, orphaned, taken into institutions, or attached
to other households during flight.

Reunification can conflict with bonds formed during survival.

### Refugee Return

Home may be occupied, burned, mined, haunted, claimed, or simply changed by
people who remained.

Return is another migration rather than reversal of time.

---

## Veterans, Injury, and Return

### The Return Road

Units disperse by stages. Pay is settled or promised. Gear is accounted for.
Wounded people travel at the speed of pain and transport.

Some soldiers delay return because they fear what waits there.

### Homecoming

Homecoming includes welcome, washing, food, rite, report, distribution of goods,
and the first night in a bed that no longer feels ordinary.

Celebration can exhaust someone still living inside alarm.

### Bodily Injury

A missing limb, damaged joint, scar, chronic pain, impaired sight or hearing,
internal injury, and weakness change work and status.

Households adapt tools, tasks, space, and expectations.

### Invisible Injury

Poor sleep, startle, numbness, rage, guilt, fear, intrusive memory, withdrawal,
and difficulty concentrating may be understood through local language of soul,
nerve, courage, grief, or haunting.

Care need not wait for modern terminology.

### Killing Weight

Some veterans are burdened less by danger endured than by acts performed,
permitted, or failed to prevent.

Praise for victory can make confession harder.

### Routine

Work, sleep near trusted people, shared meals, animal care, worship, craft, and
bounded watch restore a world with predictable sequence.

Recovery is social and material.

### Alcohol and Other Relief

Drink can ease sleep, social contact, pain, and memory briefly. Repeated use can
injure household, judgment, and body.

Moral condemnation alone rarely removes the need it answered.

### Status

Veterans may gain honor, office, land, plunder, and storytelling authority. They
may also return poor, disabled, accused, or forgotten.

Unequal recognition becomes a new political grievance.

### Former Enemies

Peace may bring enemy veterans to the same market, road crew, pilgrimage, or
marriage network.

Recognition can produce violence, avoidance, testimony, or the unsettling fact
that both remember the same rain.

### Wolfkin Return

Wolfkin veterans require pack reconnection after prolonged violence and loss.
A survivor of a destroyed pack faces grief joined to fear of hunt-mad solitude.

Other wolfkin may maintain close company, shared sleep, scent, work, and direct
interruption of isolating behavior.

### Children of Veterans

Children learn war through objects, silences, scars, sudden rules, songs, and
the stories adults repeat or refuse.

The next generation inherits memory before it inherits explanation.

---

## Grief, Burial, and Memory

### Notification

The messenger names the dead, the manner of death if known, the location,
witnesses, possessions, and whether a body can return.

Uncertain information should remain uncertain.

### Bodies Returned

Transport requires weather, vessel, preservation, identification, and people
willing to perform the work.

Returning a body can consume resources because bodily presence matters to grief
and rite.

### Distant Burial

When bodies remain near battle, companions mark graves, record place, retain
tokens, and perform as much rite as conditions permit.

Families may later travel to complete or relocate burial.

### The War Missing

No grave fixes hope and fear in tension. Households create memorial objects,
empty burial, waiting custom, or a declared time after which inheritance can
move.

Rumor can reopen the wound for years.

### Enemy Dead

Leaving enemy dead unburied can be vengeance, warning, haste, or denial of
personhood. It also invites disease, scavengers, undead, and political memory.

Burial may be the first act of peace.

### Mass Graves

Mass death may exceed individual burial capacity. Identification, arrangement,
tokens, maps, witness, and later marking preserve persons within necessity.

Anonymous disposal deepens harm.

### Mourning

Mourning changes dress, work, food, speech, worship, and social expectation.
War can place so many households in mourning that ordinary settlement rhythm
alters.

### Memorial

Cairn, board, song, feast, empty chair, named road, damaged wall, and annual walk
can hold memory.

Memorials also argue about which dead count and what the war meant.

### Competing Memory

Victor calls liberation. Loser calls invasion. Residents remember both armies
taking grain.

Let memory remain attached to position and experience.

---

## Hunger, Disease, and Broken Works

### Unplanted and Unharvested Ground

Muster removes labor during critical weeks. Fighting tramples crop. Flight
abandons field. Requisition removes seed and draft animals.

Hunger may arrive a season after victory.

### Animal Loss

Dead, stolen, scattered, overworked, and consumed animals reduce plowing,
transport, manure, milk, fiber, breeding, and status.

Replacing a mature team takes more than coin.

### Mill and Oven

Damaged mills, ovens, bridges, wells, and stores create bottlenecks. Grain can
exist while bread does not.

Repair priority becomes government.

### Disease Movement

Armies and refugees carry infection among populations previously separated.
Crowding, waste, hunger, and poor shelter increase spread.

Blaming foreigners can conceal local administrative failure.

### Wounded Burden

Wounded people need food, bedding, cleaning, nursing, fuel, and replacement of
their labor.

Care households become critical infrastructure.

### Ruined Water

Bodies, waste, broken drains, burned roofs, siege works, and altered channels
contaminate water.

Restoring clean supply comes before ceremonial rebuilding.

### Vermin and Scavengers

Abandoned food, corpses, empty houses, and broken storage attract rats, dogs,
birds, insects, beasts, and monsters.

Removing one concentration can move it into another settlement.

### Tainted Ground

Mass death, demon-work, forbidden magic, and unburied grief can attract undead,
monsters, or supernatural disturbance.

Burial, cleansing, relief, watch, and time are part of recovery.

### Famine Politics

Authorities decide ration, import, seed protection, debt, price, movement, and
which settlement receives scarce aid.

Every decision creates remembered inclusion and abandonment.

---

## Reconstruction and the Political Settlement

### Count First

Count people, animals, seed, tools, shelter, water, stores, routes, injuries,
missing kin, and dangerous ground before promising restoration.

The count must include people with weak political voice.

### Stabilize

Immediate work contains fire and secures water. Survivors roof the spaces still
occupied, bury their dead, and protect what food remains. Sanitation and an
organized watch keep the first losses from multiplying.

Monuments can wait.

### Repair Order

The well, store, byre, and occupied houses compete for labor and material.
Bridge and mill may decide whether the settlement survives the next season;
shrine, wall, and market make claims of their own.

Priority reveals who the new peace serves.

### Remission

Tax, rent, debt, tithe, and service remission let damaged households rebuild.

Collecting ordinary dues from extraordinary ruin converts victory into flight.

### Restitution

Restitution returns land, animal, tool, house, office, sacred object, and
confiscated property where possible.

Several innocent users may now depend upon the same thing.

### Demobilization

Fighters need pay, food, transport, recognition, medical care, and a lawful
place in the peace.

Leaving armed people unemployed creates raiding and faction recruitment.

### Justice

Trials, compensation, amnesty, oath, exile, office removal, and truth-telling
answer different harms.

No single purge can sort every degree of conduct under war.

### New Authority

The force that won may lack people able to judge land, maintain water, count
grain, and mediate kin.

Keeping old officers preserves skill and complicates legitimacy.

### Return during Reconstruction

Return needs safe road, property process, seed, shelter, and protection from
revenge.

Some refugees will choose the new home. Peace must allow that choice.

### Reconstruction Labor

Communal work can restore both structure and relation. It can also be forced
labor under a new banner.

State who sets the task, who is exempt, and what participants receive.

### The Second Winter

The first winter may be survived through emergency stores and aid. The second
reveals whether fields, herds, roofs, and institutions were actually restored.

End war stories after this test when possible.

## Kin Traditions of War

### Humans

Human hosts mix levy, retainers, faith, claimants, mercenaries, and village
defense shaped by centuries of fragmented rule.

Their strength is adaptability. Their danger is making every emergency into a
new hierarchy.

### Elves

Elven war can join long memory, mobile hunting, ruby recovery, forest knowledge,
and objectives whose time horizon exceeds human campaigns.

Restraint and vengeance may both persist across generations.

### Elvenspring

Elvenspring fighters often serve as scouts, archers, healers, wardens, and
intermediaries in mixed forces.

Others may depend upon this translation while excluding them from final command.

### Dwarves

Dwarven war emphasizes prepared ground, armor, engineering, stores, disciplined
craft, clan record, and the political meaning of a work defended or completed.

Slow commitment can produce formidable endurance once terms are settled.

### Orcs

Orc traditions vary by clan and history. Freedom from imposed command, earned
share, leader competence, raid, camp, and memory of enslavement shape military
relations.

Outside rulers who treat orcs as one available troop repeat the logic of former
masters.

### Halflings

Halfling communities defend household, field, store, family reputation, and the
carefully maintained surface of peaceful life.

War exposes the pressure and exclusions concealed within that order.

### Goblins

Goblin bands favor mobility, night knowledge, ambush, scouting, concealment,
route, and affiliations proved through action.

Flexible leadership is command adapted to circumstance, not absence of
discipline.

### Wolfkin

Wolfkin units draw strength from pack cohesion, scent, pursuit, forest movement,
and shared bodily awareness.

Loss of pack is a mental emergency. Commanders must not disperse survivors as
isolated replacements without care.

### Mixed Forces

Mixed forces need agreed signals, surrender forms, food practice, command
interfaces, burial, plunder share, and recognition of different psychological
needs.

Difference becomes strength only after institutional work.

## Rendering War in Play

### Show Preparation

Begin with inventory, argument, shoe repair, route study, farewell, grain price,
and who stays behind.

Battle gains weight from what was assembled for it.

### Use the Battle Rules

Let Chapter 12 resolve troop action, morale, command, siege, supply, and events.
Use this reference to interpret what the results mean for persons and places.

Do not replace the game procedure with predetermined narrative.

### Name the Objective

Every fight should answer what each side wants from the ground, road, person,
time, or force.

Killing is a means, consequence, or loss of control rather than a complete aim.

### Give Leaders Constraints

The competent captain has insufficient food, divided troops, bad maps, a patron's
order, civilian presence, fragile allies, or weather closing.

Constraint makes decisions legible.

### Give Soldiers Relations

Name who trained together, who owes whom, which household supplied gear, and
what rumor occupies the unit.

Cohesion then has human content.

### Give the Rear Scenes

Cut to the mill queue, requisition board, waiting household, refugee road,
healer's floor, and workers repairing a bridge under guard.

War exists beyond the tactical map.

### Track Five Debts

Track food taken, pay owed, bodies unreturned, promises made to allies, and harm
done to places expected to submit later.

These debts generate the next season's politics.

### After Every Battle

After every battle, answer these questions:

- Is the ground secure, and will there be a pursuit?
- Who is wounded, dead, or captive?
- What remains of supply and command?
- What news leaves the field?

Victory is incomplete until those are addressed.

### Questions before Campaign

- What claim or fear makes cost acceptable?
- Which reversible act failed?
- Which households supply the first force?
- What season is being consumed?
- Which road and water source sustain movement?
- Who extends credit?
- What limits are declared before violence?
- Who benefits if emergency continues?
- What does the enemy need besides victory?
- What will remain if neither side wins quickly?

### Questions for a Raid

- What can be carried or driven away?
- Who inside provides knowledge?
- Which alarm spreads fastest?
- What must remain intact for the objective?
- Where does withdrawal slow?
- Which household bears the targeted loss?
- What response is the raid meant to provoke?
- Which restraint distinguishes raid from massacre?
- Who divides the take?
- What evidence identifies the raiders?

### Questions after War

- Who is missing rather than confirmed dead?
- Which fields were not worked?
- Which animals and tools vanished?
- Where is water unsafe?
- Who administered occupation?
- Which veterans cannot resume prior labor?
- Which refugees want to return?
- What property has two innocent claimants?
- Which atrocity has witnesses?
- What will the second winter reveal?

### Anecdote: The Seed Receipt

A captain requisitioned six grain sacks and marked them all as food. One was the
village's seed.

The clerk knew the difference and changed nothing because the army needed a
clean account. After victory, the receipt proved full payment while the empty
field proved the lie.

### Anecdote: The Closed Gate

A rear guard reached its own fortress after dark. The gate captain refused to
open because enemy riders pressed close behind.

The rear guard held beneath the wall until dawn. Three died facing the gate they
had protected. The fortress survived and spent a generation arguing whether the
captain had saved everyone or betrayed those specific people.

### Anecdote: The Captured Mare

An orc fighter received a fine mare as his share. Months later, a refugee child
called the animal by name and it crossed the market to her.

The fighter had fed, healed, and relied upon it through winter. The child's
family had bred it for twelve years. Restitution now had two true stories.

### Anecdote: The Mill First

After a siege, the new ruler proposed rebuilding the gate as proof of strength.
Residents repaired the mill in secret at night.

The ruler punished nobody. He moved his public ceremony to the mill and claimed
he had ordered it. The lie restored Mandate more cheaply than insisting upon the
wrong priority.

### Anecdote: The Pack Survivor

One wolfkin returned from a destroyed scouting band and asked to take the outer
watch alone. The human captain praised his courage.

Another wolfkin struck the watch list from the wall, lay beside him through the
night, and answered every movement with his name. The captain learned that
solitude was the wound, not the cure.

### Closing Principle

War is a political decision carried by bodies and maintained through ordinary
work. Its truth lies in objectives, supply, restraint, surrender, and the lives
that continue after force stops moving. Run the battle cleanly. Then follow the
road home far enough to see what victory costs.
