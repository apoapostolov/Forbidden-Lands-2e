# Monster Beliefs of the Ravenlands

The beliefs of saurians, ogres, whiners, and Misgrown are poorly documented.
Observed conduct and manuscript fact must be kept apart from outsider claim and
GM inference. These peoples do not share a religion. Hunters, captors, priests,
and scholars profit from misunderstanding them. Household, work, trade,
kinship, and rule are in [Monster-Kin Cultures](culture-monster-kin.md).

---

## Saurians: the Marsh Presences

Outsiders know that saurians worship beings sighted from barges in the Gargan
Marshes. They do not know whether these are gods, ancestors, great animals,
living places, or offices held by different bodies. Do not settle the question
merely to complete a pantheon.

Observable practice centers on water level and communal feasting ponds. Dams,
crocodiles, and winter fat enter those practices. Trade platforms keep a strict
peace. Saurian religion distinguishes water where outsiders may stand under
protection from water where anything alive may be prey. Posts and cut reeds mark
the difference. So do smell and spoken warning, not an abstract border.

At a **Bank Closing**, saurians inspect a dam before winter. Each family presses
mud into one weak place and leaves a scent mark. A collapse later reveals which
section failed and who had accepted it. The rite joins engineering, territory,
and accountability.

During a **Deep Feeding**, carcasses are allowed to ferment in a chosen pond
until the group can tear and swallow them. What outsiders experience as stench
and decay is controlled food preparation, feast, and return to marsh life. A
priest or elder decides when the food is ready. Premature eating insults the
group and risks sickness.

Crocodiles occupy an uncertain sacred position: trained burden-bearers,
dangerous kin-neighbors, and famine food. Killing one when other food remains
may require replacement, public feeding of its keepers, or surrender of metal.
No one who has only watched from a boat knows which remedy applies.

Likely anathemas include violence on a marked trade platform or breach of a dam
during cold season. Hiding disease in a feast pond threatens the whole
community. Wasting shared metal is another offense. So is claiming to understand
a marsh presence seen only at the surface. Treat these as local inference until
a saurian community shows otherwise.

### Presence, Depth, and the Unnamed Marsh

Flow left marsh water unnamed and without a directed will. Saurians do not
experience this as absence of sacred order. Water that spreads, sinks, returns,
and cuts another channel resists the names outsiders use to own it.

The beings seen from barges are called **presences** here because every firmer
translation makes an unsupported choice. One may be a large animal recognized
across generations, a body used by the marsh, an ancestor made immense through
feeding, or a place surfacing long enough to look back.

At **Waiting below Speech**, witnesses submerge until only eyes and nostrils
remain. They watch water and insects, reeds and birds. Distant disturbance
enters the account without being addressed. The first person to claim a sight
must also name what ordinary animal or current could have caused it.

Saurians distinguish surface knowledge from depth knowledge. Surface can be
traded: weather and safe platform, visible channel and fish movement. Depth
knowledge concerns winter hollows and egg places. Submerged entrances lead to
the paths of the presences. Asking for it under trade peace is still an attempt
to enter another jurisdiction.

### Death, Winter, and Return to Water

Death before winter threatens the living because labor and stored fat vanish
together. Funeral work begins with reassignment of the dead person's dam section,
clutch duty, animals, platform promises, and knowledge of submerged routes.

At **Lowering the Known Weight**, the body or a weighted token is placed in water
whose depth the dead knew well. Witnesses describe the last safe route the person
taught. The marker may be recovered after a season; the body need not be.

Some communities give the dead to a feast pond. Others regard eating named kin
as confusion of household and prey. Outsiders who witness one practice should
not extend it to every saurian settlement.

The first winter ice receives careful watch. Breaking it above a sleeping hollow
can kill an entire group through cold and exposure. Saurians may execute a person
who does so deliberately without treating the act as sacrifice.

The Blood Mist altered little beneath covered water and much at exposed banks.
Trade platforms became islands of daytime exchange. When the Mist lifted,
outsiders entered channels whose old warnings they had forgotten. Saurians did
not regard reopened travel as permission.

### Saurian Evidence at the Table

Show belief through a dam section carrying one family's scent, a protected post
moved by flood, an eel spear laid point inward, a feast keeper refusing meat that
looks ready, or a trader demanding the name of the person who harvested a basket
of purple shell.

A useful dispute concerns a clutch warmed by several households, metal diverted
from communal use, a crocodile killed before famine was declared, or a presence
an outsider claims to have named after one sighting from a boat.

## Ogres: Thunder, Mark, and Chosen Kin

Many ogres care little for religion as an institution. No common priesthood
records a creed. Their thunder gatherings, body marks, violent music, and chosen
attachments nevertheless carry sacred force.

When thunder begins, ogres howl, strike wood and metal, and search for a rhythm
loud enough to enter the storm. The practice recognizes a world greater than
speech and small law. An ogre who cannot join may be ill, grieving, or outside
the group's chosen bond.

Brands, stretched ears, and weighted noses turn endurance into visible history.
Marks can show standing, loss, adoption, boast, or punishment. Outsiders who
assume every mark means the same thing may insult an ogre by praising a sign of
disgrace.

Ogre adoption can be sincere and possessive at once. A chosen companion is fed,
defended, marked, and prevented from leaving. Some ogres understand departure
as proof the bond was false from the beginning. Others hold a **Last Noise**, in
which the departing person strikes one object and is allowed to walk while the
sound remains audible.

Common taboos are local: breaking another's status weights without challenge,
interrupting a thunder rhythm for mockery, claiming a mark never endured, or
killing an adopted person before witnesses have declared the bond broken. These
limits do not make ogre treatment of captives safe.

### Thunder and the World Larger than Speech

Ogres do not usually tell a creation story. Thunder arrives without needing one.
It shakes the hut, enters the ribs, drowns command, and gives several solitary
bodies one sound to answer.

At **Finding the Beat**, each ogre strikes a different object until two rhythms
hold together through a roll of thunder. Others may join or remain apart. No one
conducts the storm. The ogre who tries to own every beat is often struck off time
by the others.

Lightning scars on tree, stone, metal, or body are treated as events before
omens. The site is touched only after fire, split timber, and unstable ground are
checked. An outsider who cuts the marked wood for a charm may discover that an
ogre intended to strike it at the next storm.

Silence after thunder can carry more weight than speech. Some ogres sit until
the rain changes. Others fight, mate, drink, or smash the instruments that failed
to answer loudly enough. There is no priesthood capable of making these endings
uniform.

### Death and the Last Mark

An ogre's death leaves objects that may carry more recognized identity than the
body. Weights, brands cut from hide, clubs, storm instruments, and adopted
dependents all draw claims.

At **The Last Mark**, companions choose one sign that will not be inherited. It
is burned, buried, bent, or carried to an unreachable ledge. The loss prevents a
successor from wearing the whole dead person as rank.

Bodies may be exposed on moorland, placed in caves, burned in huts, or consumed
by animals without common doctrine. The practical question is whether the site
continues to draw the living. A favorite thunder rock may become dangerous if
every dead ogre is placed beneath it.

A chosen captive can inherit and remain unable to leave. Outsiders may call the
inheritance freedom because the owner died. Another ogre may claim that the dead
person's care passed with the club.

### Ogres and Other Religions

Dwarves may see ogres as displaced descendants and failed placements in Huge's
work. Ogres remember being forced from caves because their bodies did not fit.
A dwarven offer of craft can be welcome while its theology sounds like ownership.

Humans call thunder practice noise until an ogre's rhythm is useful in war or
festival. Raven priests value a clearly heard promise and dislike the Last Noise
because distance, terrain, and instrument alter its term.

Huntress packs understand body, howl, and chosen group but distrust possessive
adoption. Rust recruiters admire endurance marks and offer them institutional
meaning. An ogre may join for iron, audience, and permission to hurt people, then
discover that the church expects obedience between storms.

### Ogre Evidence at the Table

Show belief through a status weight no one will explain to a stranger, two
rhythms competing under thunder, a companion testing how far a bell carries, or
a punishment brand praised as honor by someone who cannot read it.

A useful dispute concerns a departing adopted person, a dead ogre's weights, a
storm site quarried for stone, or a dwarven hall claiming an ogre child is Huge's
misplaced tool.

## Whiners: Religion behind the Hollow

Almost everything outsiders say about whiner belief is compromised by hunting,
caging, and harvesting. A captive questioned by people who value its flesh has
no reason to reveal a shrine, queen, route, or true name.

What can be observed is collective defense of hollows, the recovery or exchange
of captive kin, stone chewed and remade through the body, and violent revenge
against harvesters. Druids claim deep colonies and an egg-laying queen. The
claim may be natural history, hostile analogy, or both.

One plausible religious principle is that body and shelter are continuous. A
whiner eats stone, changes it, and adds it to the hollow; other kin cut whiner
flesh and use it to change their own bodies. The first exchange is communal and
consensual. The second is the central violation.

Possible rites include sealing a dead whiner into fresh porous stone, carrying
a chip from a lost hollow until a new chamber is joined, and biting captors'
coins before returning them with living prisoners. Possible anathemas include
revealing a brood chamber, trading another whiner's flesh, abandoning a captive
whose location is known, and placing growth-gold beneath one's own skin for
private wealth. Keep all of these as GM inference until play establishes a
particular hollow's law.

### The Hollow as Body and Record

A hollow is eaten, changed through the body, and placed back into the world. Its
porous walls carry tooth, saliva, food, sleeping heat, and the paths of many
small hands. Calling it a nest misses the labor by which bodies make shelter and
shelter shapes later bodies.

At **Joining the Mouthful**, several whiners chew from one damaged edge and add
the softened stone to a repair. Each bite differs in texture. The patch records
who accepted the wall. A smooth repair made by one captive under command has no
such standing.

Chambers have uses outsiders rarely distinguish: feeding, young, sick, trade,
ambush, captive recovery, dead, and passages deliberately too narrow for larger
kin. A person who maps one chamber may still know nothing of the settlement.

Stone taken from a living hollow is not inert building material. Returning it in
another shape can be repair, insult, or introduction. Quarrying the whole
formation destroys accumulated body-work even if no whiner is killed at the
moment.

### Death and Continuing Stone

At **Closing the Pores**, fresh stone is worked around the dead until smell and
small scavengers no longer pass freely. The body becomes part of a wall no one
uses for ordinary waste or captive holding.

A chip from a lost hollow may be carried in the mouth or beneath cloth until a
new chamber is joined. It is softened into the first repair. The act moves work
and memory without claiming the old place can be rebuilt unchanged.

Bodies lost to harvest leave no stone burial. Survivors may use the captor's cage
bar, coin, or knife as substitute evidence. The object records violence but can
also draw hunters if displayed.

The Blood Mist isolated hunters as well as hollows. Some colonies expanded close
to settlements that could no longer organize long searches. When roads reopened,
old harvesting markets demanded flesh in larger quantities. Freedom from Mist
did not mean safety underground.

### Whiner Evidence at the Table

Show belief through a porous repair carrying many bite patterns, an empty
sleeping place still receiving food, a returned coin with tooth marks, or a
rescued captive whose first act is to ask about another cage.

A useful dispute concerns voluntary use of flesh, communal growth-gold, a druid's
claim about the queen, a rescued person left without a route home, or a hollow
quarried as ownerless stone.

## Misgrown and Religious Ownership of the Body

Misgrown are not one religious kin. They are humans altered through demonic
power, concentrated around authorities that benefit from the alteration. The
Rust church reads marked bodies as election when useful and contamination when
disobedient. Raven Sisters may see a patient and victim. Druids may see imposed
form with living claims. Villagers may see danger before personhood.

Misgrown religious life begins with jurisdiction over the body. Who may name or
touch it? Who may heal it or put it on display? Who may recruit the person or
decide that change has become identity? A private rite may consist only of covering a feature the
church demands be shown, or giving an altered limb a household name rather than
a doctrinal one.

The gravest offense within such hidden fellowships is delivering another
Misgrown person to a priest in exchange for one's own acceptance. The gravest
danger is that refusal may cost every marked person in the settlement what
little protection they possess.

### Death, Remains, and Posthumous Seizure

Misgrown remains attract priests, healers, sorcerers, collectors, frightened
villagers, and kin seeking an ordinary burial. Death does not end jurisdiction
over the body. It makes the person unable to defend it.

At **The Last Covering**, companions wrap or arrange the body according to wishes
stated while living. Features displayed by the church may be concealed. Features
hidden in shame may be shown to trusted mourners if the dead asked to be known.

Rust Brothers seize remains as sacred material, evidence, or threat. A hidden
fellowship may substitute animal bone, burn distinctive tissue, or bury parts in
separate places. These measures protect the dead and can prevent a family from
having one grave.

Ordinary burial may be unsafe where growth continues, blood taints soil, or a
second organism survives. Practical precautions are not proof that the person
was a monster. The grave keeper records what was done and who may inspect it.

Theft of altered remains for medicine or magic repeats the church's claim that
the body exists for use. Consent given for one purpose does not release every
piece to whoever arrives first.

### Relations with Gods and Orders

Rust and Heme provide the institution most willing to give marked bodies public
standing. The price is obedience, display, recruitment, and acceptance of the
church's origin story. Competence makes the offer harder to refuse.

Raven Sisters tend patients and oppose forced claim. They can still turn rescue
into a sermon about corruption survived. A patient may want a wound cleaned
without becoming proof of Raven mercy.

Clay traditions ask whether imposed form has acquired living claims. Melders and
druids possess useful knowledge and the dangerous confidence that every body can
be corrected. Restoration to an imagined earlier form may erase capacities the
person now values.

Wyrm keepers preserve names, descent, and prior wishes. Their records can protect
inheritance or guide a Rust search. Halfling back rooms hide marked people well
and may require them to remain hidden for life.

Wolfkin judge continued personhood through answer, boundary, food, and pack.
They may accept an altered person whom a village rejects. They may also hunt a
body whose smell they associate with demons before any relation can form.

### Misgrown Evidence at the Table

Show belief through clothing cut for a feature the church insists remain bare,
a household name for an altered limb, a healer's record separating known cause
from doctrine, or a grave divided so no collector can take the whole body.

A useful dispute concerns a child claimed before naming, a chosen craft treated
as compulsory use, a Rust remedy with a hidden source, an escapee whose former
victims live in the refuge, or a druid offering restoration the person does not
want.

## Using Outsider Accounts

Accounts of monster religion usually reach the table through hostile witnesses.
Hunters describe prey. Priests describe error. Traders describe the parts of a
people that enter price. Captives describe whatever might open the cage.

Keep each claim attached to access. A bargeman saw the top of a saurian presence.
A druid entered one whiner chamber. A Rust register named an altered child. An
ogre's former captive translated the Last Noise after surviving one household.
None of these witnesses saw nothing. None saw the whole people.

Contradiction should produce investigation rather than a single hidden answer.
Ask what each account allowed its speaker to do. Calling the presences gods may
support mission. Calling the whiner queen an animal may support harvest. Calling
an ogre bond adoption may conceal captivity. Calling a Misgrown body proof may
give a church the right to seize it.

The GM can establish a local truth through repeated material evidence: the same
dam marks, repair bites, status weight, chosen covering, or exchange formula.
Another community may maintain a different truth without making the first a lie.
