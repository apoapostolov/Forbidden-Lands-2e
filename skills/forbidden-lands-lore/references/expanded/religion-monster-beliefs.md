# Monster Beliefs of the Ravenlands

This reference groups the poorly documented beliefs of saurians, ogres, whiners, and Misgrown. It distinguishes observed conduct and manuscript fact from outsider claim and GM inference. These peoples do not share a religion; they share the danger of being described by hunters, captors, priests, and scholars who profit from misunderstanding them.

---

## Saurians: the Marsh Presences

Outsiders know that saurians worship beings sighted from barges in the Gargan
Marshes. They do not know whether these are gods, ancestors, great animals,
living places, or offices held by different bodies. Do not settle the question
merely to complete a pantheon.

Observable practice centers on water level, communal feasting ponds, dams,
crocodiles, winter fat, and the strict peace of trade platforms. Saurian
religion distinguishes water where outsiders may stand under protection from
water where anything alive may be prey. The difference is marked with posts,
smell, cut reeds, and spoken warning, not an abstract border.

At a **Bank Closing**, saurians inspect a dam before winter. Each family presses
mud into one weak place and leaves a scent mark. A collapse later reveals which
section failed and who had accepted it. The rite joins engineering, territory,
and accountability.

During a **Deep Feeding**, carcasses are allowed to ferment in a chosen pond
until the group can tear and swallow them. What outsiders experience as stench
and decay is controlled food preparation, feast, and return to marsh life. A
priest or elder decides when the food is ready. Premature eating insults the
group and risks sickness.

Crocodiles occupy an uncertain sacred position: trained burden-bearers,
dangerous kin-neighbors, and famine food. Killing one when other food remains
may require replacement, public feeding of its keepers, or surrender of metal.
No one who has only watched from a boat knows which remedy applies.

Likely anathemas include violence on a marked trade platform, breaching a dam
during cold season, hiding disease in a feast pond, wasting metal received for
communal use, and speaking as if one had understood a marsh presence seen only
at the surface. Treat these as local inference until a saurian community shows
otherwise.

### Presence, Depth, and the Unnamed Marsh

Flow left marsh water unnamed and without a directed will. Saurians do not
experience this as absence of sacred order. Water that spreads, sinks, returns,
and cuts another channel resists the names outsiders use to own it.

The beings seen from barges are called **presences** here because every firmer
translation makes an unsupported choice. One may be a large animal recognized
across generations, a body used by the marsh, an ancestor made immense through
feeding, or a place surfacing long enough to look back.

At **Waiting below Speech**, witnesses submerge until only eyes and nostrils
remain. They watch water, insects, reeds, birds, and distant disturbance without
addressing it. The first person to claim a sight must also name what ordinary
animal or current could have caused it.

Saurians distinguish surface knowledge from depth knowledge. Surface can be
traded: weather, safe platform, visible channel, fish movement. Depth knowledge
concerns winter hollows, egg places, submerged entrances, and the paths of the
presences. Asking for it under trade peace is still an attempt to enter another
jurisdiction.

### Authority and the Long Harangue

Authority follows the matter. Dam keepers speak on water and winter shelter.
Feast keepers judge fermentation. Crocodile handlers control approach to their
animals. Trade speakers remember prices, insults, and the exact protection
declared at each platform.

Saurian speech sounds rambling to outsiders because a public statement locates
water, season, witnesses, prior use, and exceptions before naming the demand.
Cutting the account short can change a temporary passage into a permanent right.

At **The Repeated Bank**, another speaker repeats a judgment from a different
place on the platform. The second voice changes any word that depends on where
the first speaker stood. If the ruling no longer works from both banks, it is a
claim of position rather than common water.

No central priest rules the marshes. A keeper may gain influence by knowing
several channels and presences. That knowledge remains suspect when used to
command a family whose winter pond the keeper has never entered.

### Household, Eggs, and Young

Saurian households form around warm water, stored fat, feeding ponds, platforms,
and the labor needed to keep channels open. Human ideas of one house under one
roof fit poorly. Several sleeping hollows may share one dam and one winter duty.

At **Warming the Clutch**, adults bring rotting plant matter, mud, and watched
heat to the egg place. Each contribution is pressed into a separate section.
Later failure can be traced without turning every lost egg into a divine sign.

Young are taught the difference between protected water and prey water before
they can understand a trade speech. A marked post is touched, smelled, and
circled from the safe side. Crossing it in play is corrected at once. Outsiders
who move the marker for convenience teach a lethal lesson.

Recognition follows feeding and protection more strongly than proven descent.
An adult who guarded a clutch through flood may hold claims equal to a parent.
Stealing an egg attacks a person and the whole labor that maintained its heat.

Cold kills young quickly. A ritual cannot substitute for dry cover, stored food,
and adults carrying heat between water and nest. Saurian religion places these
acts before any interpretation of unusual markings on the shell.

### Food, Fermentation, and Distribution

Saurians cannot chew. Bodies, roots, and fish must be torn small or softened.
Fermentation is cooking by time, water, and guarded decay. A feast pond is a
kitchen whose walls move.

At **Opening the Pond**, the feast keeper tests smell, surface, gas, insect life,
and the firmness of several pieces. A portion is removed for the sick and young
before the strongest enter. Outsiders invited to watch are placed upwind and
told exactly where not to step.

Waste carries a different meaning in a marsh. Bone, hide, offal, and spoiled
material feed water or become tools. The offense lies in concentrating rot where
it poisons a sleeping hollow, trade platform, or future clutch.

Eating speaking kin is possible under prey law. A trader under platform peace is
not prey. A trespasser beyond the marked water may be. Saurian communities differ
over captives who understand neither marker nor warning; some escort them out,
others say ignorance did not make the water less occupied.

Crocodile meat is divided first among handlers and animals that shared its work.
Famine may end the animal's protected status. Killing it early because a hunter
prefers the flesh turns a communal reserve into private appetite.

### Trade Peace and Metal

Trade platforms are built above water no party may hunt across while peace holds.
Posts, reed cuts, smell marks, and spoken terms define the protected approach.
The platform is inspected after every flood.

At **Laying down the Eel Spear**, traders place weapons with points toward their
own water. A knife for sale remains wrapped until a saurian handler opens it.
Drawing a second weapon to demonstrate quality can empty the platform before an
apology is translated.

Metal is valued for edge and durability, not coinage. A bent spearhead may be
worth more than silver because it can be ground into hooks. Gold unable to cut,
fasten, or weight a net has little claim on food.

Purple shell, fish, frog, and bread root leave the marsh through specific
families. Payment to the platform speaker does not settle the harvester's claim.
Unscrupulous traders exploit the difference, then call the later demand double
payment.

Violence under peace requires repair beyond blood-price. The platform must be
cleaned, damaged posts replaced, every party warned, and a new speaker found if
the former one can no longer guarantee approach. Until then, trade moves to a
worse bank and everyone pays.

### Death, Winter, and Return to Water

Death before winter threatens the living because labor and stored fat vanish
together. Funeral work begins with reassignment of the dead person's dam section,
clutch duty, animals, platform promises, and knowledge of submerged routes.

At **Lowering the Known Weight**, the body or a weighted token is placed in water
whose depth the dead knew well. Witnesses describe the last safe route the person
taught. The marker may be recovered after a season; the body need not be.

Some communities give the dead to a feast pond. Others regard eating named kin
as confusion of household and prey. Outsiders who witness one practice should
not extend it to every saurian settlement.

The first winter ice receives careful watch. Breaking it above a sleeping hollow
can kill an entire group through cold and exposure. Saurians may execute a person
who does so deliberately without treating the act as sacrifice.

The Blood Mist altered little beneath covered water and much at exposed banks.
Trade platforms became islands of daytime exchange. When the Mist lifted,
outsiders entered channels whose old warnings they had forgotten. Saurians did
not regard reopened travel as permission.

### Saurian Evidence at the Table

Show belief through a dam section carrying one family's scent, a protected post
moved by flood, an eel spear laid point inward, a feast keeper refusing meat that
looks ready, or a trader demanding the name of the person who harvested a basket
of purple shell.

A useful dispute concerns a clutch warmed by several households, metal diverted
from communal use, a crocodile killed before famine was declared, or a presence
an outsider claims to have named after one sighting from a boat.

## Ogres: Thunder, Mark, and Chosen Kin

Religion matters little to many ogres in the institutional sense. No common
priesthood records a creed. Their thunder gatherings, body marks, violent music,
and chosen attachments nevertheless carry sacred force.

When thunder begins, ogres howl, strike wood and metal, and search for a rhythm
loud enough to enter the storm. The practice recognizes a world greater than
speech and small law. An ogre who cannot join may be ill, grieving, or outside
the group's chosen bond.

Brands, stretched ears, and weighted noses turn endurance into visible history.
Marks can show standing, loss, adoption, boast, or punishment. Outsiders who
assume every mark means the same thing may insult an ogre by praising a sign of
disgrace.

Ogre adoption can be sincere and possessive at once. A chosen companion is fed,
defended, marked, and prevented from leaving. Some ogres understand departure
as proof the bond was false from the beginning. Others hold a **Last Noise**, in
which the departing person strikes one object and is allowed to walk while the
sound remains audible.

Common taboos are local: breaking another's status weights without challenge,
interrupting a thunder rhythm for mockery, claiming a mark never endured, or
killing an adopted person before witnesses have declared the bond broken. These
limits do not make ogre treatment of captives safe.

### Thunder and the World Larger than Speech

Ogres do not usually tell a creation story. Thunder arrives without needing one.
It shakes the hut, enters the ribs, drowns command, and gives several solitary
bodies one sound to answer.

At **Finding the Beat**, each ogre strikes a different object until two rhythms
hold together through a roll of thunder. Others may join or remain apart. No one
conducts the storm. The ogre who tries to own every beat is often struck off time
by the others.

Lightning scars on tree, stone, metal, or body are treated as events before
omens. The site is touched only after fire, split timber, and unstable ground are
checked. An outsider who cuts the marked wood for a charm may discover that an
ogre intended to strike it at the next storm.

Silence after thunder can carry more weight than speech. Some ogres sit until
the rain changes. Others fight, mate, drink, or smash the instruments that failed
to answer loudly enough. There is no priesthood capable of making these endings
uniform.

### Marks, Weight, and the Public Body

Brands and stretched flesh are records that cannot be left at home. The person
carrying one may have chosen pain, accepted punishment, survived capture, or been
marked while unable to refuse.

At **Naming the Iron**, the wearer explains a new weight before it is attached.
Witnesses lift it and hear the claim. A status weight that another person cannot
verify may still be worn, but it earns no place in common distribution.

Large ears and nose can show endurance and standing. Heavy ornament also tears
skin, impedes work, and catches in a fight. Older ogres whose flesh no longer
supports great weights may pass them to a chosen companion. Taking them before
the wearer releases the claim is stripping a living history.

Punishment brands are contested. Some groups require the offender to carry the
mark openly. Others hold that a scar imposed by an enemy records the enemy's act,
not the captive's guilt. Outsiders buying or praising marked skin usually cannot
tell the difference.

An ogre may cut away a brand, losing flesh and the story attached to it. Former
companions can treat the act as release, confession, or theft from witnesses who
shared the event.

### Chosen Kin, Captivity, and Possessive Care

Ogres have little broad kin loyalty. A chosen relation can therefore bear more
weight than ancestry. The companion is fed first, defended fiercely, and included
in thunder rites. They may also be watched, marked, or injured to prevent escape.

At **Sharing the Roof Stone**, an ogre and chosen companion each move one heavy
object into the other's sleeping place. The exchange makes mutual access visible.
If only the smaller companion carries weight, adoption has become service.

Some ogres cut a captive's tendons and call the resulting dependence proof of
kinship. Care after deliberate injury does not undo the act. It creates a closed
world in which the captor can point to every meal as evidence of love.

The Last Noise offers a rare bounded departure. The leaving person strikes an
object and may walk while the sound remains audible. A clever companion chooses
a bell, hollow cliff, or long metal sheet. An ogre who muffles the sound after
agreeing to the rite has attacked the witness as well as the departing person.

Children of mixed human and dwarven descent may be raised in halls rather than
among ogres. An ogre who later meets such kin can claim recognition that the
other person does not share. Body alone does not create chosen relation, though
many ogres find this refusal incomprehensible.

### Food, Gift, and Solitary Property

An ogre household may contain one person and still keep strict claims. The club,
drinking vessel, storm object, stored meat, and adopted person's bedding occupy
known places. Moving them can be challenge or invasion.

At **The Heavy Gift**, a giver offers something the recipient must physically
carry away. The weight proves no moral truth. It shows whether the giver
considered the receiver's body and journey. A millstone given to a human guest
may be mockery disguised as generosity.

Feasts happen when hunting, theft, trade, or thunder draws several ogres together.
Food is divided by contribution, challenge, affection, and the loud demand of
whoever reaches first. A chosen dependent may receive more than a visiting ogre,
which outsiders mistake for kindness until they see what departure costs.

Beer is wager, payment, and storm fuel. An intoxicated promise may still bind if
the others changed behavior around it. Some groups require the promise repeated
after sleep. Others consider sober denial cowardice.

### Death and the Last Mark

An ogre's death leaves objects that may carry more recognized identity than the
body. Weights, brands cut from hide, clubs, storm instruments, and adopted
dependents all draw claims.

At **The Last Mark**, companions choose one sign that will not be inherited. It
is burned, buried, bent, or carried to an unreachable ledge. The loss prevents a
successor from wearing the whole dead person as rank.

Bodies may be exposed on moorland, placed in caves, burned in huts, or consumed
by animals without common doctrine. The practical question is whether the site
continues to draw the living. A favorite thunder rock may become dangerous if
every dead ogre is placed beneath it.

A chosen captive can inherit and remain unable to leave. Outsiders may call the
inheritance freedom because the owner died. Another ogre may claim that the dead
person's care passed with the club.

### Ogres and Other Religions

Dwarves may see ogres as displaced descendants and failed placements in Huge's
work. Ogres remember being forced from caves because their bodies did not fit.
A dwarven offer of craft can be welcome while its theology sounds like ownership.

Humans call thunder practice noise until an ogre's rhythm is useful in war or
festival. Raven priests value a clearly heard promise and dislike the Last Noise
because distance, terrain, and instrument alter its term.

Huntress packs understand body, howl, and chosen group but distrust possessive
adoption. Rust recruiters admire endurance marks and offer them institutional
meaning. An ogre may join for iron, audience, and permission to hurt people, then
discover that the church expects obedience between storms.

### Ogre Evidence at the Table

Show belief through a status weight no one will explain to a stranger, two
rhythms competing under thunder, a companion testing how far a bell carries, or
a punishment brand praised as honor by someone who cannot read it.

A useful dispute concerns a departing adopted person, a dead ogre's weights, a
storm site quarried for stone, or a dwarven hall claiming an ogre child is Huge's
misplaced tool.

## Whiners: Religion behind the Hollow

Almost everything outsiders say about whiner belief is compromised by hunting,
caging, and harvesting. A captive questioned by people who value its flesh has
no reason to reveal a shrine, queen, route, or true name.

What can be observed is collective defense of hollows, the recovery or exchange
of captive kin, stone chewed and remade through the body, and violent revenge
against harvesters. Druids claim deep colonies and an egg-laying queen. The
claim may be natural history, hostile analogy, or both.

One plausible religious principle is that body and shelter are continuous. A
whiner eats stone, changes it, and adds it to the hollow; other kin cut whiner
flesh and use it to change their own bodies. The first exchange is communal and
consensual. The second is the central violation.

Possible rites include sealing a dead whiner into fresh porous stone, carrying
a chip from a lost hollow until a new chamber is joined, and biting captors'
coins before returning them with living prisoners. Possible anathemas include
revealing a brood chamber, trading another whiner's flesh, abandoning a captive
whose location is known, and placing growth-gold beneath one's own skin for
private wealth. Keep all of these as GM inference until play establishes a
particular hollow's law.

### The Hollow as Body and Record

A hollow is eaten, changed through the body, and placed back into the world. Its
porous walls carry tooth, saliva, food, sleeping heat, and the paths of many
small hands. Calling it a nest misses the labor by which bodies make shelter and
shelter shapes later bodies.

At **Joining the Mouthful**, several whiners chew from one damaged edge and add
the softened stone to a repair. Each bite differs in texture. The patch records
who accepted the wall. A smooth repair made by one captive under command has no
such standing.

Chambers have uses outsiders rarely distinguish: feeding, young, sick, trade,
ambush, captive recovery, dead, and passages deliberately too narrow for larger
kin. A person who maps one chamber may still know nothing of the settlement.

Stone taken from a living hollow is not inert building material. Returning it in
another shape can be repair, insult, or introduction. Quarrying the whole
formation destroys accumulated body-work even if no whiner is killed at the
moment.

### Authority, Queens, and the Limits of Outsider Analogy

Druids report egg-laying queens and colony structure. The observation may be
accurate. “Queen” may still import human rule into a body whose work concerns
birth rather than command.

Authority can be observed in who receives food, which call changes collective
movement, who decides a captive exchange, and whose body is guarded during
flight. Outsiders should record these acts before naming monarch, priest, or
brood mother.

At **The Many Answer**, a warning passes through walls by bite, scrape, thump,
and voice. No individual needs the whole message. A hunter who captures the
first caller has not silenced the hollow.

Some large individuals may lay eggs while others feed, tend, carry, or defend.
This division can create sacred dependence and coercion. Protecting a reproductive
body does not prove that body rules or consents to every use made of it.

Whiners questioned under threat may confirm whatever colony tale keeps captors
away from the real chamber. Contradictory accounts are evidence of danger, not
inferior reason.

### Flesh, Healing, and the Harvesting Violation

Whiner flesh heals and fattens other bodies. This property makes every whiner a
walking resource in the eyes of hunters, physicians, orc buyers, and wounded
adventurers. Religious life forms under the knowledge that a stranger's need can
become a justification for dismemberment.

Within a hollow, small voluntary cuttings may treat kin. If so, the practice
does not authorize outsiders to harvest. Consent inside a community of shared
risk cannot be purchased from a cage keeper.

At **Counting the Returned Flesh**, a rescued captive is examined without blame.
Missing tissue, scars, weight, infection, and the names or smells of other cages
are recorded. The first work is food and wound care. Questions wait until the
person can survive the answers.

Weapons used by harvesters may be bitten, fouled, broken, or built into latrines.
The treatment strips them of healing authority. A surgeon's clean knife and a
butcher's clean knife can be the same object in different hands.

Using flesh cut from a corpse remains disputed. One hollow may permit it if the
dead person released the body. Another may see every use by outsiders as keeping
the harvest alive. A trader who claims corpse source must still explain custody.

### Gold, Growth, and Dangerous Wealth

Gold placed beneath whiner skin grows heavier. Outsiders read the body as mine,
mint, or miracle. Captors insert gold and wait while the person bears pain,
infection, confinement, and the risk of being cut again.

Within a hollow, growth-gold could supply ransom, trade, or tools impossible to
make from stone. Its use would also create a class of bodies expected to carry
communal wealth. Treat the question as contested rather than assuming rejection
or easy acceptance.

At **Biting the Price**, whiners test a coin offered in exchange for a captive.
They may return the marked gold with the rescued person. The bite identifies the
payment if hunters later claim no bargain occurred.

Private insertion of communal growth-gold is a likely anathema because it makes
shared danger into hidden wealth. Forced insertion by hollow leaders would be
the same violation with a religious speech around it.

Gold that has grown inside a dead whiner carries a body claim. Melting it can
erase the marks by which the hollow might prove where it came from. Traders who
pay less for bitten gold profit from that erasure.

### Captivity, Recovery, and War

Every known cage creates a duty. Immediate attack may kill the captives. Delay
may cost more flesh. Whiner war therefore turns on information, exchange, traps,
and the price a hollow can force hunters to pay without exposing its chambers.

At **Holding the Empty Place**, the rescued person's sleeping hollow remains
open during captivity. Food is placed there and then reassigned. The act keeps
the missing person inside distribution without pretending they have eaten.

Captive exchange can include kidnapped travelers who had no part in harvesting.
Whiners may torture or trade them because larger kin recognize pain only when it
reaches their own people. The logic is understandable and does not make the
victim responsible.

A traveler who cuts cages open and leaves cannot claim the duty complete. Weak,
wounded whiners need food, shade, a route, and protection from the hunters who
own the land around the cage. Rescue without removal can make punishment arrive
sooner.

Whiners may maintain people outside the hollow who trade warnings, medicine, or
metal. Such intermediaries are targets for both harvesters and whiners who regard
any contact as betrayal.

### Death and Continuing Stone

At **Closing the Pores**, fresh stone is worked around the dead until smell and
small scavengers no longer pass freely. The body becomes part of a wall no one
uses for ordinary waste or captive holding.

A chip from a lost hollow may be carried in the mouth or beneath cloth until a
new chamber is joined. It is softened into the first repair. The act moves work
and memory without claiming the old place can be rebuilt unchanged.

Bodies lost to harvest leave no stone burial. Survivors may use the captor's cage
bar, coin, or knife as substitute evidence. The object records violence but can
also draw hunters if displayed.

The Blood Mist isolated hunters as well as hollows. Some colonies expanded close
to settlements that could no longer organize long searches. When roads reopened,
old harvesting markets demanded flesh in larger quantities. Freedom from Mist
did not mean safety underground.

### Whiner Evidence at the Table

Show belief through a porous repair carrying many bite patterns, an empty
sleeping place still receiving food, a returned coin with tooth marks, or a
rescued captive whose first act is to ask about another cage.

A useful dispute concerns voluntary use of flesh, communal growth-gold, a druid's
claim about the queen, a rescued person left without a route home, or a hollow
quarried as ownerless stone.

## Misgrown and Religious Ownership of the Body

Misgrown are not one religious kin. They are humans altered through demonic
power, concentrated around authorities that benefit from the alteration. The
Rust church reads marked bodies as election when useful and contamination when
disobedient. Raven Sisters may see a patient and victim. Druids may see imposed
form with living claims. Villagers may see danger before personhood.

Misgrown religious life therefore begins with jurisdiction. Who may name the
body? Who may touch it, heal it, display it, recruit it, or decide that change
has become identity? A private rite may consist only of covering a feature the
church demands be shown, or giving an altered limb a household name rather than
a doctrinal one.

The gravest offense within such hidden fellowships is delivering another
Misgrown person to a priest in exchange for one's own acceptance. The gravest
danger is that refusal may cost every marked person in the settlement what
little protection they possess.

### Naming, Display, and the Right to Cover

Rust doctrine gives altered features titles: sign, blessing, warning, use,
punishment, proof. The named person may use a household word instead. A horn can
be “left brow,” a scaled hand “the cold one,” and a second mouth “the eater.” The
plain name returns daily ownership to people who wash, feed, bind, or live with
the body.

At **The Chosen Showing**, a Misgrown person states which features may be seen,
touched, described, or covered in a particular setting. Healers can request
access. Priests can demand it. Only the person can turn either act into ordinary
consent when refusal is possible.

Church processions display marked bodies as proof of divine selection. The same
feature is hidden when it shows failed control, pain, or an origin the order
cannot explain. A person praised in armor may be locked away when infection
reveals the cost beneath it.

Covering can protect from stare, cold, sun, work injury, or doctrinal claim. It
can also worsen heat and wounds. Tailors, leatherworkers, and healers who know
altered bodies become important religious allies without holding priesthood.

### Hidden Fellowships and Mutual Care

Misgrown fellowships form through medicine, safe sleep, clothing, food that fits
an altered mouth, and warnings about recruiters. Shared form is not required.
The person with bone growth may have little bodily knowledge useful to someone
whose skin weeps resin.

At **Keeping the Night Body**, two people sleep in shifts when an alteration
changes during rest, draws predators, speaks, or acts without full waking control.
The watcher records what occurred and wakes the sleeper before making a religious
judgment.

Care creates difficult power. The only person willing to clean a hidden wound
may demand secrecy, loyalty, sex, coin, or obedience. Fellowship law treats such
demands as taking payment from a door no one else will open.

Medicines are shared with origin attached. Rust remedy, druidic salve, whiner
flesh, demonic secretion, and ordinary boiled cloth do not become equivalent
because all help. A recipient needs enough truth to choose the danger.

Marks can make ordinary work impossible and other work uniquely easy. A second
arm may lift, a claw may cut, night eyes may guide. Fellowship teachers resist
the church's claim that usefulness proves the alteration was given for that
labor.

### Birth, Descent, and Children under Suspicion

An altered parent does not know what a child will inherit. Rust priests use the
uncertainty to claim supervision of pairing, pregnancy, birth, and the newborn.
Villagers may demand separation before any sign appears.

At **Naming before the Mark**, trusted households give the infant an ordinary
name before priest or examiner records the body. The sequence establishes a
person whom later alteration affects. It prevents the first visible difference
from becoming the whole name.

Parents may hide harmless traits to keep a child outside church custody. Hiding
a dangerous need can kill the child or caregiver. The decision belongs under
pressure from institutions that punish either disclosure or concealment.

Some Misgrown cannot bear children or reproduce through unfamiliar processes.
Rust doctrine may call infertility an offering and seize fertile people for
breeding. Hidden fellowships build kin through adoption, apprenticeship, shared
escape, and long care.

A child whose body resembles a known demon can become evidence in another
person's trial. The resemblance proves no allegiance. It does reveal which
authorities have studied the demon closely enough to recognize it.

### Work, Service, and the Theology of Use

Rust teaches that iron and wood receive form through proper force. Misgrown
bodies are assigned tasks that fit doctrine and anatomy: guard, laborer, tracker,
executioner, vessel, omen, breeder. Refusal is described as material resisting
its maker.

At **Refusing the Use**, a fellowship records one task the person will not perform
merely because their body can. The refusal may cost food or shelter. Another
member witnesses it so later coercion cannot be rewritten as vocation.

Settlements outside Rust rule also exploit altered capacity. A village that
hides a Misgrown laborer from priests may expect unpaid night work in exchange.
Protection becomes ownership by smaller steps.

Wages and tools need adaptation. A clawed hand may destroy a standard grip. A
large body needs more food. Unusual sight can make bright work painful. Paying
the same coin for unequal cost is often praised as treating everyone alike.

Some people embrace a task tied to form and gain pride, office, and skill. The
wrong lies in making capacity into compulsory purpose, not in allowing the body
to become part of chosen craft.

### Death, Remains, and Posthumous Seizure

Misgrown remains attract priests, healers, sorcerers, collectors, frightened
villagers, and kin seeking an ordinary burial. Death does not end jurisdiction
over the body. It makes the person unable to defend it.

At **The Last Covering**, companions wrap or arrange the body according to wishes
stated while living. Features displayed by the church may be concealed. Features
hidden in shame may be shown to trusted mourners if the dead asked to be known.

Rust Brothers seize remains as sacred material, evidence, or threat. A hidden
fellowship may substitute animal bone, burn distinctive tissue, or bury parts in
separate places. These measures protect the dead and can prevent a family from
having one grave.

Ordinary burial may be unsafe where growth continues, blood taints soil, or a
second organism survives. Practical precautions are not proof that the person
was a monster. The grave keeper records what was done and who may inspect it.

Theft of altered remains for medicine or magic repeats the church's claim that
the body exists for use. Consent given for one purpose does not release every
piece to whoever arrives first.

### Relations with Gods and Orders

Rust and Heme provide the institution most willing to give marked bodies public
standing. The price is obedience, display, recruitment, and acceptance of the
church's origin story. Competence makes the offer harder to refuse.

Raven Sisters tend patients and oppose forced claim. They can still turn rescue
into a sermon about corruption survived. A patient may want a wound cleaned
without becoming proof of Raven mercy.

Clay traditions ask whether imposed form has acquired living claims. Melders and
druids possess useful knowledge and the dangerous confidence that every body can
be corrected. Restoration to an imagined earlier form may erase capacities the
person now values.

Wyrm keepers preserve names, descent, and prior wishes. Their records can protect
inheritance or guide a Rust search. Halfling back rooms hide marked people well
and may require them to remain hidden for life.

Wolfkin judge continued personhood through answer, boundary, food, and pack.
They may accept an altered person whom a village rejects. They may also hunt a
body whose smell they associate with demons before any relation can form.

### Escape, Conversion, and Returning to a Settlement

Leaving Rust service requires food, clothing, medicine, route, and a place whose
people can withstand church inquiry. Removing armor may expose tissue never
allowed to bear weather. The first free night can be physically more dangerous
than the last guarded one.

At **Taking Back the Name**, the escapee chooses which church name, rank, scar,
or title companions may continue to use. Witnesses record coercive acts without
making confession the price of shelter.

Conversion to another faith changes who may touch, bury, marry, employ, and
interpret the body. It cannot make the church forget a useful servant. A Raven
oath or Clay rite needs defenders able to enforce the new jurisdiction.

Return to a home settlement may fail even when relatives remain. The person who
left had one body and household role. The returning body may need space, food,
care, or secrecy the family cannot supply. Affection does not widen a doorway.

Some return as armed church agents and later defect. Their victims may refuse
fellowship. Rescue from the institution does not settle harms committed within
it.

### Misgrown Evidence at the Table

Show belief through clothing cut for a feature the church insists remain bare,
a household name for an altered limb, a healer's record separating known cause
from doctrine, or a grave divided so no collector can take the whole body.

A useful dispute concerns a child claimed before naming, a chosen craft treated
as compulsory use, a Rust remedy with a hidden source, an escapee whose former
victims live in the refuge, or a druid offering restoration the person does not
want.

## Using Outsider Accounts

Monster religion reaches most tables through hostile witnesses. Hunters describe
prey. Priests describe error. Traders describe the parts of a people that enter
price. Captives describe whatever might open the cage.

Keep each claim attached to access. A bargeman saw the top of a saurian presence.
A druid entered one whiner chamber. A Rust register named an altered child. An
ogre's former captive translated the Last Noise after surviving one household.
None of these witnesses saw nothing. None saw the whole people.

Contradiction should produce investigation rather than a single hidden answer.
Ask what each account allowed its speaker to do. Calling the presences gods may
support mission. Calling the whiner queen an animal may support harvest. Calling
an ogre bond adoption may conceal captivity. Calling a Misgrown body proof may
give a church the right to seize it.

The GM can establish a local truth through repeated material evidence: the same
dam marks, repair bites, status weight, chosen covering, or exchange formula.
Another community may maintain a different truth without making the first a lie.
