# Magic in Ravenlands Society

Magic leaves heat in the palms and blood beneath the nails. Sometimes it leaves
words in a dead teacher's hand. Neighbors remember which roof burned after the
miracle. Talent must be noticed before a teacher can be found. Ingredients
pass through gatherers and traders. Grimoires pass through copyists, thieves,
and heirs. Around them grow prices for service, customs for judging harm, and a
place for each discipline in ordinary life.

The workings beneath magic belong in [Magic Metaphysics and Path
Doctrines](magic-metaphysics-and-path-doctrines.md). The rules for learning and
casting belong in Chapter 7. Here the question is what those truths do to a
household or market, and how the shrine and the wider settlement answer the
caster after the spell is over.

## Authority and Scope

### Claim Levels

**Published canon** governs named peoples and places. It also governs published
faiths, histories, and magical traditions.

**Manuscript fact** governs the current Chapter 7 rules. These include seventeen
disciplines and access to teachers. Five Path ranks govern learning. Rank 6
spells remain possible only through chance casting. The chapter also controls
grimoires, ingredients, burn, rituals, casting modes, and mishaps.

**Setting inference** draws social consequences from those rules. If grimoires
reduce casting difficulty, people will steal or inherit them. Owners must
authenticate and conceal them before they can bargain.

**Historical model** supplies workshop practice and apprenticeship. Household
labor supports manuscript production. Customary judgment governs disputes.
Patronage and market relations suit a fragmented premodern world.

**Personal campaign lore** extends Ravenlands society for Apostol Apostolov's
campaign. A table may adopt it, alter it, or reject it.

### What This Reference Does Not Do

It does not add spells, alter casting mechanics, or declare one discipline's
doctrine finally correct.

It does not assume that every settlement has a resident caster. Rarity and
secrecy in Chapter 7 remain the controlling guide.

It does not make common folk ignorant. People may misunderstand causes while
possessing exact knowledge of signs and prices. They remember dangers and
reputations.

It does not make every accusation false or every caster persecuted. Magic can
heal a child or mend a wall. It can compel a mind, poison a well, or raise a
corpse. It can also bring something through the world's skin. Fear has objects.

### Social Knowledge and Path Knowledge

A villager may know that a healer requires rest after difficult work without
knowing how Healing doctrine describes the body's remembered shape.

A ferryman may recognize the smell after an Elemental mishap without knowing a
single spell.

A ranked caster learns doctrine through study, then tests technique against
consequence. A community knows magic through experience and warning. Its
stories preserve the behavior of people who survived it.

These forms of knowledge should meet in play without one automatically
invalidating the other.

## The Social Fact of Magic

### Magic Is Rare and Recurrent

Magic is rare enough to acquire reputation and recurrent enough to shape
custom. Most people cannot cast. Many have known somebody healed or cursed.
Others have seen a ritual search, taken fright at a sign, or been cheated by a
person claiming power.

Distance changes the story. A witnessed working has details: who stood where,
what was spent, what the air smelled like, and what happened afterward. Three
villages away, the same event becomes proof for a doctrine.

### Magic Is Work

Casting consumes Willpower and ingredients. Preparation demands attention, and
risk remains. Learning consumes years. Favors and travel secure instruction.
Secrecy and expensive mistakes consume more.

The caster who performs one spell has also gathered what it requires, kept
knowledge alive, judged the circumstance, and accepted responsibility for an
uncertain force.

People who treat magic as effortless often resent its price. People who know the
work may still resent a caster who uses scarcity to dominate them.

### Magic Is a Relationship

No spell remains only between caster and target. Witnesses interpret it.
Households remember it. Teachers answer for it. Rivals describe it. Authorities
ask whether it was permitted. Priests ask what was invited. Heirs inherit its
objects and enemies.

A private casting can have public consequence. It may change kinship or memory,
health or weather. It may alter property, or the credibility of testimony.

### Magic Marks Time

Settlements date events by memorable workings. A child was born the winter
after the stone singer opened the spring. The east barn was raised before the
dream sickness. The old bridge lasted until the fire-caller came.

This memory does not preserve precise rank or spell name. It preserves a social
before and after.

### Magic and Unequal Dependence

Scarce competence creates dependence. A settlement may hate its resident caster
and still require her at childbirth. The same caster may read monster signs,
find water, strengthen walls, or send messages.

The caster also depends upon the settlement for food and ingredients. Witnesses
and protection matter as much as people willing to report early signs.

Healthy relations make the exchange visible. Predatory relations keep one side
permanently afraid that refusal means abandonment.

### The Smell of Known Trouble

People remember sensory traces. They know the copper taste that followed one
blood working, the pressure in the ears before a stone answered, the moths that
gathered after a dream rite, or the frost remaining where no winter lay.

Such traces are evidence of a kind, but never complete proof of author or intent.
An enemy can imitate signs. A mishap can resemble malice. An old working can wake
without its caster.

### Public and Hidden Practice

Known disciplines acquire customary spaces. A healer keeps a room. A druid uses
a grove. Other paths claim quarry ledges, winter shrines, and fenced ritual
yards.

Strange disciplines are shown selectively. Disturbing disciplines are concealed
or practiced under powerful protection. Prohibited disciplines require a
hidden teacher and false profession. Distance helps, as does a patron willing
to silence inquiry.

Secrecy changes technique. A hidden caster cannot gather openly, store every
ingredient nearby, or ask witnesses to report small mistakes.

### Magic and Reputation

A caster's reputation should answer four questions.

- What can this person reliably do?
- What happened during the worst known failure?
- Whom will this person refuse?
- Which faction, household, or teacher answers for the work?

A reputation containing only power is a rumor. Trust grows around limits.

## How Talent Is First Known

### Talent Does Not Arrive with a Label

The first sign seldom names a discipline. A frightened child may know where an
absent parent stands, make a fever dream contagious, still an animal, draw iron
filings into a pattern, or speak a word that leaves everyone silent.

Some signs are magic. Others are illness, observation, coincidence, fraud, or a
story improved after the fact.

A wise examiner begins with what happened, not with what frightened adults have
called it.

### Bodily Sensations

Prospective casters often describe warmth behind the ribs, a pressure in the
teeth, a second rhythm beneath the pulse, or an eagerness that remains when the
body is spent.

These sensations become meaningful only after instruction. Before that, the
person may fear fever, possession, hunger, or madness.

Repeated adventuring and hardship can make willpower more recognizable. A
survivor may discover magical capacity late because suffering gave the inner
conflict enough force to be felt clearly.

### Accidental Effects

Untrained people do not usually produce disciplined spells. Their first effect
is partial, circumstantial, or entangled with a place.

A stone shifts when grief and danger meet. A wound closes badly. A pursued child
cannot be found though tracks enter the room. Every dog in the yard looks toward
the same empty corner.

Such an effect may lead to a teacher. It does not grant a talent rank.

### Community Examiners

Healers, druids, old sorcerers, religious officers, herb wives, and experienced
midwives may be asked to examine signs.

The examiner watches sleep, memory, animals, appetite, temperature, shadows,
dreams, and the person's response to simple materials associated with several
paths.

No universal test exists. An honest examiner can say, “I do not know.” A
recruiter rarely does.

### False Recognition

Families sometimes declare talent because they desire status, fear a child,
need an explanation for illness, or want a distant master to take an unwanted
dependent.

Institutions may identify a “marked” child when the child's body or behavior
fits doctrine even though no magic is present.

The harm of false recognition can include exile, forced apprenticeship,
dangerous testing, or a lifetime spent interpreting ordinary distress as
supernatural duty.

### Concealed Talent

Parents conceal signs where recruitment, persecution, or kin separation is
likely. They teach the child never to repeat certain words, draw attention, or
admit to dreams.

Concealment may prevent help. A small uncontrolled effect can grow around fear
and secrecy until the first public incident is also a disaster.

### Late Discovery

Adults may seek instruction after a survival experience, encounter with a
place, inherited grimoire, repeated dream, or season serving a caster.

Late students bring developed habits and obligations. They learn more slowly in
some respects and judge teachers more clearly in others.

Their household must decide who performs the work abandoned for study.

### The First Witness

The person who first believes the prospective caster can shape the whole path.
A gentle witness makes curiosity possible. A fearful one teaches concealment. A
hungry institution teaches that power belongs to whoever names it.

Record that witness as part of the caster's history.

## Family Responses

### Pride

A family may see magical promise as honor, protection, or escape from ordinary
poverty. It borrows for travel, preserves ingredients, and retells every early
sign as evidence of destiny.

Pride can support difficult training. It can also make failure unspeakable.

### Fear

Families fear mishap, religious accusation, altered behavior, bad teachers, and
the possibility that the child will return with loyalties elsewhere.

Fear may produce strict care: supervised practice, safe spaces, clear limits.
It may produce cruelty disguised as prevention.

### Economic Calculation

An apprentice removes labor from the household now in exchange for uncertain
future competence. Travel, gifts, clothing, tools, and food must be supplied.

A poor household may love a child and still be unable to sustain training.
Patronage then enters where hunger has already narrowed consent.

### Sibling Effects

One child's training redistributes work and attention. Siblings inherit chores,
comparison, secrecy, and sometimes the expectation that future magic will repay
their sacrifice.

The talented child may envy the sibling permitted an ordinary marriage and
home.

### Kin Reputation

Known practice can alter marriage, credit, and hospitality for an entire family.
Healing may improve standing. Symbolism may make every carved mark suspect.
Prohibited practice can endanger people who knew nothing.

Families therefore claim or deny relationship according to circumstance.

### The Child Sent Away

Travel to a teacher can feel like opportunity, fosterage, sale, rescue, or
banishment.

The child receives a new discipline of body and speech while home continues
without her. Return creates affection between people whose daily knowledge has
diverged.

### The Family Secret

Hidden practitioners require relatives to explain absences, strange purchases,
injuries, visitors, and smells. Secrecy becomes unpaid household labor.

The caster who says “I alone bear the risk” may be lying to himself.

### Refusal

A family may refuse to release a dependent to a teacher. The teacher may call
this ignorance. The family may possess good knowledge of the institution's
methods.

No talent grants an automatic right to another person's custody.

### Return after Mishap

A harmed apprentice may return with scars, altered memory, fear of casting, or
a master's explanation that blames weak will.

The household must choose whether to accept the explanation, seek another
teacher, conceal the event, or demand compensation from somebody with greater
power.

## Teachers and the Roads to Them

### Initiation

Disciplines taught by initiation are accessible only by Ravenlands standards.
The student must still find a willing person, establish character, offer
compensation, and remain long enough to learn.

Teachers may be attached to shrines, settlements, traveling circles, households,
or practical trades. The road to them is social before it is geographic.

### Demonstration

A teacher requiring demonstration wants proof of need, discipline, loyalty,
talent, restraint, or shared obligation.

The test should reveal the tradition's values. A shapeshifter may require weeks
of animal observation. A symbolist may demand exact copying under discomfort. A
swarm teacher may ask the student to tend rot without disgust or waste.

A test designed only to humiliate reveals the teacher, not the path.

### Journey

Journey disciplines require remote masters, secret chains, dangerous patrons,
prohibited texts, or travel into the regions where the path remains alive.

The journey filters students by resources and social freedom as much as talent.
A farmer with dependents cannot vanish for a season merely because doctrine
calls.

Those who complete the journey return carrying foreign custom and obligations
the home settlement did not witness.

### Finding a Name

Teachers are found through healers, caravan masters, old patients, religious
networks, retired adventurers, manuscript owners, kin elders, and people who
sell rumors.

A true name may be protected by an epithet, route, token, or chain of
introductions.

The person who supplies the introduction accepts reputational risk. If the
student behaves badly, the shame travels backward.

### False Teachers

Charlatans can demonstrate herbs, sleight, hidden accomplices, monster parts,
and one stolen working they do not understand.

More dangerous are real casters who cannot teach. Personal talent, secrecy, and
power do not guarantee patience or a coherent method.

A prospective student should ask whom the teacher trained and what became of
them.

### Teacher Scarcity

Scarcity lets teachers demand labor, service, silence, travel, ingredients,
political loyalty, marriage, or participation in their own unfinished struggles.

Some demands are the cost of sustaining instruction. Others are exploitation.
The difference lies in proportionality, disclosure, and the student's ability
to leave.

### Teacher Lineage

Lineage carries technique, doctrine, enemies, favored ingredients, mishap
stories, and habits whose original reason may have been forgotten.

Two teachers of the same discipline may regard one another's practice as crude,
dangerous, heretical, or merely accented.

Lineage gives players a social genealogy for magic without requiring a unified
school.

### Traveling Teachers

A traveling teacher can reach scattered students while lacking stable ritual
space and storage. Lessons occur beside roads, in borrowed rooms, and during
work.

The teacher's route becomes a calendar. Missing one visit may delay advancement
for a season.

Travelers also carry news of failures and innovations between otherwise isolated
lines.

### Resident Masters

A resident master acquires a household, local obligations, stored objects, and
neighbors able to notice changes in behavior.

Residence supports safer practice and invites political capture. The lord who
provides a tower may begin deciding who receives lessons.

### Teaching across Kin

Kin differ in lifespan, body, memory, ritual obligation, and assumptions about
property. A method suited to one may need translation before another kin can
use it.

Cross-kin teaching creates intellectual fertility and accusations of theft.

### The Dead Teacher

A teacher's death leaves unfinished apprentices, hidden ingredients, coded
notes, promised services, enemies, and questions about who may inherit the
lineage.

The best student may not own the house. The heir may not know magic. The temple
may claim the grimoire. The village may claim the wards protect everyone.

Succession in magic is a political event.

## Apprenticeship

### The Apprenticeship Bargain

An apprenticeship exchanges service, obedience, payment, or loyalty for
instruction and access. Its terms may be written, witnessed, sworn, or assumed.

State duration, maintenance, ownership of copied texts, permitted outside work,
conditions of dismissal, and what the teacher owes after injury.

Unstated terms favor the person with the room, reputation, and knowledge.

### The First Season

Early training consists of observation, household work, ingredient recognition,
memory, posture, breathing, copying, and learning when not to cast.

The impatient student sees chores. The careful teacher sees whether the student
notices contamination, protects tools, tells the truth about error, and remains
steady around distress.

### Daily Routine

The apprentice rises with the household, cleans the working place, checks stored
materials, copies or recites doctrine, performs ordinary labor, observes one
exercise, and records bodily or environmental signs.

Casting cannot fill the day because willpower is scarce and mishaps matter.
Most competence is built between workings.

### Observation

Watching a spell includes preparation and aftermath. The apprentice notes what
the master asked, refused, measured, moved away, and checked after success.

A spectacular effect teaches less than a careful debriefing.

### Memory Work

Oral lineages train exact sequences through verse, gesture, patterned walking,
knotted cords, object placement, and repeated correction.

Memory work can remain precise without writing. It is vulnerable to injury,
death, isolation, and the gradual authority of an elder's preferred version.

### Copying a Grimoire

Copying teaches hand, attention, terminology, and the relation between a mark
and a performed act. The apprentice may spend weeks copying material not yet
permitted to cast.

Errors are compared aloud. A beautiful copy can be magically useless if it
preserves appearance rather than working relation.

### Supervised Casting

The teacher chooses a place where failure can be contained, warns witnesses,
limits nearby materials, and assigns someone to watch the student's body.

Supervision cannot cancel a mishap. It can recognize one quickly and prevent the
second injury caused by panic.

### Discipline after Error

Good discipline reconstructs the sequence: fatigue, ingredient, declared mode,
attention, witness, environmental condition, and the student's own report.

Punishment may be appropriate for concealment or reckless disobedience.
Punishing the mere existence of a mishap teaches students to hide evidence.

### Work Owed to the Master

Apprentices gather plants, copy pages, tend animals, carry messages, assist
patients, clean ritual spaces, and perform the ordinary trade supporting the
household.

Work becomes exploitation when it prevents instruction while debt continues to
grow.

### Peer Apprentices

Several students create cooperation, comparison, rivalry, and witnesses outside
the master's authority.

Older apprentices translate instruction and can reproduce the master's abuses.

Peer groups often preserve a lineage after the master's death because no single
student learned the whole practice.

### Secrecy among Apprentices

Restricted disciplines divide knowledge deliberately. One apprentice knows the
route, another the cipher, another the ingredient source.

Compartmentalization protects the path and makes trust difficult. It can leave
survivors unable to use what they risked to preserve.

### Romance, Marriage, and Household Formation

Apprentices live close under stress. Affection can become partnership or
rivalry. Favoritism and jealousy enter the school with it. Pregnancy may bind
the pair to a household or lead them to form a new teaching household.

A master who controls marriage or intimacy controls succession. Doctrine does
not excuse such authority from scrutiny.

### Leaving Early

Students leave because of fear, disagreement, family need, injury, politics,
poverty, or the discovery that the teacher is dangerous.

An incomplete apprentice may possess enough knowledge to attract blame and not
enough to work safely.

### Completion

Completion should be witnessed through a competent act and an accepted limit.
The new caster demonstrates what can be done and names what will not yet be
attempted.

The teacher may give a tool, a page, an ingredient source, a public word of
confidence, or a debt to the lineage.

### Solitary Study

Chapter 7 permits advancement without a teacher at far greater experience cost.
In society, solitary study means reconstructing method without correction.

The student compares texts, experiments cautiously, seeks witnesses, and spends
long periods unable to tell whether difficulty comes from doctrine, hand,
material, or self-deception.

Solitary casters may innovate because nobody taught them which failures were
impossible. They may also repeat dangers a lineage learned to avoid generations
ago.

## Ranks as Social Standing

### Rank and Reputation Are Different

Path rank measures learned magical capacity. Reputation measures what people
believe a caster can be trusted to do.

A Rank 1 village healer may be trusted more than a concealed Rank 5 sorcerer.

Most non-casters cannot identify exact rank by observation. They infer from
effects, composure, teachers, years, and what respected people are willing to
risk nearby.

### Rank 1: The Working Beginner

A Rank 1 caster knows a path well enough to produce real effects and real harm.
The social danger is overestimation. Neighbors who hear “healer” may expect every
injury to be answerable.

Beginners should state narrow competence: fever and wound care, simple signs,
one kind of stone, familiar animals, or another honest boundary shaped by known
spells.

### Rank 2: Independent Service

At Rank 2, a caster can often maintain a modest practice, travel for work, and
supervise simple preparation by others.

Communities begin asking the caster to judge matters beyond spellcraft. Magical
competence lends borrowed authority to medicine, religion, law, and politics.

Wise casters name where that borrowing ends.

### Rank 3: Regional Name

A Rank 3 practitioner may draw students, difficult cases, faction interest, and
requests from several settlements.

Travel becomes triage. Helping one place means delaying another. Reputation
often grows faster than the household that schedules, supplies, and repairs the
work.

### Rank 4: Institutional Weight

A Rank 4 caster can alter the plans of rulers, temples, military captains, and
rival traditions.

Even a recluse has political weight because factions consider what might happen
if an enemy reaches the recluse first.

At this rank, refusal requires explanation or strong protection.

### Rank 5: Mastery and Burden

Rank 5 is the highest Path talent rank. Such a master may teach the full known
discipline and attempt Rank 6 spells only through chance casting.

Mastery does not remove uncertainty. It enlarges the scale at which certainty
is demanded.

Students, patrons, enemies, and communities preserve every decision as possible
precedent.

### Rank 6 Spells

Rank 6 spells are always chance cast by Rank 5 practitioners. They cannot be
made normal or safe through grimoire, reduction, or another effect.

Society should treat their preparation as an announced crisis, concealed
catastrophe, or act of final necessity. A community asked to support one must
understand that a mishap is guaranteed even if the intended effect succeeds.

Ritual form provides time for counsel and containment. It does not provide
safety.

### Titles

Titles such as master, sister, singer, wise one, dreamer, or ironbrow may name
lineage and office without revealing exact rank.

A self-awarded title invites scrutiny. A title spoken by former students carries
different weight from one painted on a door.

## Grimoires as Working Objects

### What a Grimoire Contains

A grimoire may contain spell forms, corrections, diagrams, ingredient notes,
mishap accounts, names of teachers, doctrinal arguments, debts, and warnings
whose importance is obvious only after failure.

The useful book is often untidy. Working pages acquire wax, blood, pressed
plants, ash, replacement leaves, and several hands.

### Material Form

Grimoires can be bound books, stitched gatherings, scroll cases, carved slats,
metal leaves, bark sheets, stone rubbings, knotted sequences, or another durable
form suited to the path.

Material affects travel, concealment, repair, fire risk, moisture, and the kind
of craftsperson required to copy it.

### Writing a Spell

Chapter 7 requires the caster to perform the spell normally before recording it.
Chance casting does not establish the controlled understanding needed for a
working text.

Writing is therefore a claim backed by prior competence. A copied spell may be
beautiful while lacking the author's own successful ground.

### Marginalia

Margins preserve the social life of the book. One student corrects a measure.
Another notes a teacher's death. A later owner warns that the listed ingredient
came from poisoned ground.

Erasing marginalia can make a grimoire cleaner and less safe.

### Authentication

Authentication compares hand, lineage phrases, material age, known errors,
ingredient references, and small executable portions.

No responsible examiner tests an unknown high-rank ritual merely to prove a
book genuine.

False grimoires combine real doctrine with dangerous substitutions. Their maker
needs enough knowledge to exploit trust.

### Ownership

Possible owners include writer, teacher, student, patron, temple, household,
settlement, or the kin whose resources produced the book.

These claims often overlap. The person physically holding the grimoire may have
the weakest moral claim and the strongest immediate one.

### Inheritance

A caster's heir may inherit parchment without talent. A senior apprentice may
inherit practice without legal ownership. A patron may claim repayment. A
settlement may argue that wards recorded there were purchased by communal labor.

Good magical wills name custody, copying rights, dangerous leaves, student
access, and what must be destroyed.

### Copying

Copying requires light, ink, suitable surface, time, a model, and a person able
to preserve exact distinctions.

Rare inks and materials create supply chains. A copyist may learn politically
dangerous content without being able to cast it.

Copying permission can be more valuable than ownership.

### Lending

A lent grimoire travels with conditions: named spell, duration, escort,
collateral, prohibition on copying, and responsibility for damage.

Borrowers may leave another book or a hostage in place of silver.

The lender must judge whether withholding knowledge creates greater danger than
losing control of it.

### Theft

Stealing a grimoire takes technique, lineage memory, and bargaining power. It
may also remove the only safe copy from people who depend upon it.

Fences prefer pages they can identify and buyers able to survive ownership.
Prohibited books invite blackmail from every intermediary.

### Ciphers and Concealment

Casters use false recipes, omitted gestures, layered languages, private symbols,
separate ingredient keys, and pages visible only under a condition.

A cipher protects against casual discovery while increasing the chance that an
heir destroys useful knowledge as nonsense.

### Damaged Grimoires

Water blurs proportions. Fire removes the beginning or end of a sequence. Mold
joins leaves. Vermin consume ingredient names. Blood fixes pages together.

Repair requires material craft and magical judgment. Filling a gap by intuition
creates a new text, not a restoration.

### The Grimoire in Combat

Chapter 7 requires ready access and a free hand. Social practice therefore
produces harnesses, cases, straps, page markers, weather covers, and assistants
trained to open the correct place.

A battlefield grimoire is smaller, sturdier, and less complete than a master's
house book.

### Public Grimoires

A temple or settlement may keep a book under several keys. Access requires two
officers, a witness, and a stated purpose.

Shared custody prevents private sale and makes urgent use slower.

### Destroying a Book

Destruction may prevent misuse and erase the only surviving method of repair.

Burning is dramatic. Separating key leaves, drowning ink, grinding tablets, or
altering one fatal measure may be quieter and crueler.

Destroying a grimoire is a political choice between dangerous use and
irreplaceable loss.

## Ingredients and Their Supply

### Ingredients Are Relations

An ingredient comes from a place and through someone's work. Soil and season
shape what can be gathered. Animals and crafts shape what can be prepared.
Trade, household stores, graves, and dangerous places all place claims upon the
final object. Even common materials have gatherers and owners.

The caster who names only the final object forgets the person who climbed,
butchered, stole, grew, dried, carried, or preserved it.

### Gathering

Gatherers learn timing, habitat, maturity, contamination, and respectful or
safe removal. A plant picked after seed differs socially and materially from one
torn before it can return.

Magical demand can strip a local source faster than custom permits.

### Cultivating Ingredients

Useful plants, insects, fungi, and small animals may be cultivated near a
caster's household. Cultivation exchanges wild potency, secrecy, labor, and
reliability.

Gardens become evidence. A prohibited ingredient growing behind the byre can
condemn people who only watered it.

### Animal Materials

Feather, tooth, blood, bone, fat, hide, organ, and shed covering require
different relations to killing.

Some materials can be taken without lasting harm. Others create a magical market
for carcasses and encourage wasteful hunting.

A responsible practitioner uses the rest of the animal or pays those who do.

### Human Remains

Hair, nail, blood, breath, milk, teeth, bone, and corpse matter carry consent,
kin claim, religious rule, and evidentiary danger.

An object freely given for one spell is not general permission for every future
use.

Theft from the dead injures the living community that keeps the grave.

### Monster Materials

Monster parts create dangerous collection work and markets full of
misidentification. Age, decay, preservation, and the creature's manner of death
may matter.

A settlement protected from a monster may still dispute whether the slayer owns
the whole carcass.

### Minerals and Metals

Stone, ore, filings, rust, worked iron, salt, crystal, ash, and clay pass through
miners, burners, smiths, potters, and traders.

Magical use competes with roofs, tools, weapons, preservation, and ordinary
craft. A sorcerer buying every iron filing in winter makes the smith's shortage
public.

### Water, Ice, Fire, and Air

Elemental materials appear abundant until a spell requires a particular state,
source, cleanliness, or immediate access.

Carrying winter ice into summer demands insulation and loss. Keeping a flame
from a named hearth requires attendants. Taking water from a sacred spring may
require permission no coin purchases.

### Storage

Ingredients need drying, salting, cooling, darkness, ventilation, sealed glass,
wax, separate shelves, labels, and protection from children or vermin.

Incompatible materials are stored apart. A household learns these rules even if
only one member casts.

### Identification

Markets support specialists who distinguish genuine material from substitution.
They use smell, texture, break, reaction, provenance, and trusted suppliers.

A fraudulent ingredient may merely fail to help. It may change the harm of a
mishap.

### Pricing

Price includes rarity, gathering risk, perishability, religious restriction,
transport, secrecy, legal danger, and the buyer's urgency.

Coin is only one payment. Gatherers may demand healing, protection, access,
training, or a share in the purpose.

### Substitution

Lineages preserve substitutions for famine, winter, travel, and persecution.
These may be less effective, differently dangerous, or unacceptable under local
doctrine.

Substitution requires its own competence. Resemblance or suggestive meaning does
not make one object equivalent to another.

### Ingredient Brokers

Brokers connect gatherers who do not want to meet casters with casters who
cannot reveal demand. They aggregate small supplies, judge quality, extend
credit, and learn unsettling patterns.

A broker who sees three prohibited buyers seeking the same material may know a
crisis before any ruler does.

### Taboos

Taboo can protect ecology, dead bodies, sacred places, kin boundaries, and
people vulnerable to magical harvesting.

It can also reserve valuable material for an elite while presenting monopoly as
cosmic necessity.

Ask who benefits and what prior disaster the taboo remembers.

### Ingredient Theft

Theft can look minor to a person who sees a bundle of herbs. To the caster it
may remove a season's work or the one material needed to keep a promised ward.

Conversely, calling every gathered object private magical property can dispossess
the community whose land produced it.

### Waste and Disposal

Spent ingredients may leave ash, blood, contaminated water, dead insects,
tainted cloth, sharp fragments, or objects still carrying attention.

Disposal is part of casting. Burying everything behind the house merely moves
the problem into soil and wells.

### Mog as a Forbidden Supply Chain

Mog requires resistant vessels and corrodes careless storage. Its smell, stains,
burns, and containers betray traffic even when buyers conceal doctrine.

Anyone transporting mog participates in demonic practice whether or not that
person can cast. Carriers therefore demand high payment, coercion, or an account
of the work persuasive enough to outweigh terror.

Demon metaphysics remains behind the campaign-spoiler boundary. The social
truth is sufficient: mog commerce brings contamination, secrecy, blackmail, and
powerful hunters.

## Willpower in Daily Life

### The Warmth after Struggle

Willpower is born where the self meets a world that resists it. The body tires,
the mind suffers, hope burns down, and something remains: warmth behind the
breastbone, intent without strength, an eagerness to act while every limb asks
for rest.

Experienced adventurers and frequent casters learn to recognize this presence.
It is bodily and mental experience rather than an abstract substance stored in
the air.

### Suffering Does Not Make a Spell by Itself

Pain can generate the inner conflict represented by willpower. Pain does not
teach a Path, choose a wise effect, or make suffering good.

A torturer cannot manufacture mastery by hurting apprentices. Such abuse may
break the body and Wits required to understand what remains.

### Grit and Survival

Willpower bears the shape of resistance survived: the breath after lifting, the
clarity after fear, the anger after humiliation, the decision made while hungry.

Casters cultivate awareness of that residue through rest, recollection,
breathing, bodily practice, prayer, and the disciplined refusal to spend it on
every available impulse.

### Cultivation

Cultivation does not mean seeking wounds for their own sake. It means meeting
real difficulty attentively enough that survival becomes usable intent rather
than mere exhaustion.

A cultivator trains, works, travels, fasts under bounded custom, keeps vigil,
accepts difficult service, and reflects upon strain. The practice creates
familiarity with the threshold at which the world takes and the self still
answers.

### False Cultivation

Institutions can romanticize deprivation imposed upon dependents. An underfed
student, beaten servant, or terrified novice may be praised for gaining the very
willpower the institution then claims.

Distinguish chosen discipline from coercion. Chosen hardship retains an exit,
purpose, proportion, and care afterward.

### Rest

Rest restores the person who can recognize and direct will. A caster who treats
sleep, food, companionship, and sanity as weakness eventually possesses intent
without judgment.

Households therefore watch for the master who remains warm-eyed and eager after
everybody else has begged him to stop.

### Willpower Envy

Non-casters may believe casters profit from suffering ordinary people merely
endure. Casters may secretly envy those who can spend hard-won resolve upon
family, craft, or escape without inviting magic into it.

This tension becomes bitter when a patron expects a caster to arrive already
filled with willpower but refuses to acknowledge what the journey cost.

### The Last Reserve

Communities learn that a caster sometimes refuses a useful spell to preserve
willpower for unknown danger. This can look miserly beside present pain.

Trust requires explaining the reserve before crisis. Secrecy makes every refusal
look like preference.

### Burn

Burn converts bodily harm directly into temporary willpower for one spell. It is
the clearest proof that magic can consume the person who directs it.

Witnesses see trembling, sudden weakness, confusion, emotional injury, or a body
that no longer obeys as it did moments before.

No patron should speak of burn as free power because the caster volunteered.
Consent does not erase consequence.

### Care after Burn

Aftercare includes warmth, water, stillness, examination, protection from
further demands, and people able to notice which attribute was harmed.

Wits or Empathy injury may be less visible than bleeding and more dangerous to
the next judgment.

The caster should not be left alone simply because the intended spell worked.

## Casting Modes as Moral Choices

### Normal Casting

Normal casting accepts the discipline's ordinary relation between power and
risk. It is neither cowardly nor brave. It is the working mode around which
teachers build expectations.

Witnesses should know that a normal casting can still mishap.

### Safe Casting

Safe casting becomes available when mastery exceeds the spell's rank. The
caster removes risk-bearing dice and also reduces the possibility of
overcharging.

Socially, safe casting says that reliable sufficiency matters more than a
stronger uncertain effect.

Where a spell carries a safe-casting limitation, safety also narrows its generic
use. That limitation belongs in the agreement with the recipient.

### Chance Casting

Chance casting guarantees a mishap. It may allow a spell above talent rank or
activate a spell's chance-casting benefit.

The choice can be defensible when failure to attempt the effect is worse than
the certain mishap. It can also be vanity disguised as courage.

People exposed to the mishap deserve a voice wherever circumstances permit.

### Voluntary Chance Casting

A caster may invite a guaranteed mishap even when the spell lies within known
rank. The added ribbon can produce useful control or utility, but the harm is
not ceremonial.

A settlement employing magic should state who may authorize voluntary chance
casting around dwellings, stores, animals, and children.

### Rank 6 Necessity

Every Rank 6 attempt is chance casting. Preparation should include evacuation,
witness, containment, succession instruction, and an account of why lesser
means are insufficient.

The guaranteed mishap is part of the planned event, not an unforeseen accident.

### Declaring the Mode

The rules require the caster to declare mode before spending willpower.
Society benefits when that declaration is audible to assistants and adult
recipients.

“Safe,” “ordinary,” and “I invite the break” can be lineage phrases with legal
weight.

### Emergency Consent

An unconscious patient cannot consent. A burning hall cannot assemble a moot.
Emergency custom may permit a caster to act under a reasonable belief of need.

Afterward, the caster still owes an account. Emergency authority without review
becomes habit.

### Shared Risk

A patron may order a dangerous casting while standing far from its likely
consequence. Require the patron to supply evacuation, repair pledges, guards,
ingredients, and care.

Risk becomes morally clearer when the decision-maker cannot export every cost.

## Mishaps, Liability, and Repair

### Mishap Is Not Automatic Innocence

A mishap is an uncontrolled magical consequence. The caster may still be
responsible for reckless location, exhaustion, false claims, poor storage,
concealed mode, or refusal to prepare containment.

Conversely, bad outcome alone does not prove negligence.

### The One-Turn Horizon

When a mishap says the next time a named event occurs, that pending effect lasts
for one turn unless the mishap gives another period such as an hour or Quarter
Day.

Witnesses may therefore keep watch through the full fifteen minutes, even if the
caster feels normal after seconds.

### First Response

Stop secondary harm. Move people away from fire or unstable stone before either
spreads the mishap. Poisoned air and panicked animals require different exits.
The hostile dead or the altered caster may require guards as well as rescue.

Do not begin blame while somebody remains beneath a beam.

### The Mishap Witness

A prepared working names one person to watch the caster and one to watch the
surroundings. These should be different people when possible.

Witnesses record declared mode, willpower spent, visible dice outcome if used in
the fiction, ingredients, immediate signs, and the sequence of later events.

### Quarantine

Unknown alteration may require temporary separation of person, object, room,
animal, or water source.

Quarantine should name duration, care, communication, and authority to release.
Abandoning a frightened person behind a locked door is imprisonment, not
procedure.

### Compensation

Compensation can cover lost labor, animals, crop, building damage, medical care,
ritual cleansing, travel, burial, and the damaged reputation of a wrongly
suspected household.

No fixed price can answer every magical injury. Local judgment needs comparable
ordinary harms and the caster's degree of fault.

### Teacher Liability

A teacher may share responsibility for sending an unready apprentice, teaching
reckless custom, concealing known danger, or failing to provide promised
supervision.

Lineages that answer for students earn trust. Those that disclaim every failure
teach settlements to punish casters collectively.

### Patron Liability

The person ordering, funding, or coercing a spell may owe more than the hand that
cast it.

Patrons prefer the story of the solitary sorcerer's error. Records should name
whose purpose placed the caster there.

### Repair

Repair combines craft, magic, labor, apology, restitution, and time. A mended
wall does not restore sleep to the household whose children were inside it.

The caster's visible participation in ordinary cleanup matters. Sweeping glass
and carrying water can restore more trust than another display of power.

### Recurrence

Repeated mishaps of one kind suggest method, environment, material, curse,
enemy action, or a teacher's concealed defect.

Do not treat recurrence as proof that the caster's character is rotten. Do not
ignore it because magic is always dangerous.

### Mishap Stories

Teachers preserve mishap stories as cases rather than omens. Name condition,
choice, effect, response, and later discovery.

Communities preserve them as warnings attached to people and places. Both forms
contain knowledge.

## Payment, Patronage, and Magical Labor

### Why Spells Lack a Simple Price

The same spell costs differently according to travel, urgency, secrecy,
ingredient, willpower, danger, mode, recipient, and the services displaced by
accepting it.

A healer treating one rich traveler may delay three village patients. A stone
singer opening a lord's quarry may change an entire valley's labor.

### Forms of Payment

Payment may include silver, food, lodging, livestock, cloth, craft, land use,
ingredient rights, manuscript access, political protection, future service, or
training for a named person.

Casters prefer goods their households can use. Patrons prefer promises whose
later value they control.

### The Poor Patient

Known beneficial disciplines develop customs of remission, communal payment,
seasonal dues, or service in kind.

Free work is still supported by somebody's labor. A village may feed its healer
year-round so no bedside bargaining is needed.

### Retainers

A retainer gives maintenance in exchange for availability and priority. Terms
should name residence, travel, private clients, dangerous castings, students,
and the caster's right to refuse.

The court caster risks becoming an instrument whose consent is assumed because
food was already accepted.

### Patronage

Patrons fund travel, ingredients, grimoires, protected space, and time away from
ordinary work. In return they seek prestige, service, discovery, or monopoly.

The patron who says “pursue knowledge freely” may still own the room, book, and
road papers.

### Collective Hiring

Several households can pool grain or labor for a seasonal service. Collective
hiring reduces individual dependence and creates disagreement over priority.

Write a queue before the caster arrives.

### Outcome and Effort

Magic does not fail in the ordinary sense, but mishaps and uncertain Power Level
can alter outcome. Payment terms may cover attempt, intended effect, achieved
effect, or time.

Charging only for perfect outcome encourages concealment. Charging fully after
recklessness invites justified anger.

### Dangerous Work Premium

Travel into cursed ground, use of disturbing disciplines, public exposure,
chance casting, and hostile political attention justify additional payment.

The premium should fund preparation and aftercare. Bravado earns nothing by
itself.

### Magical Monopolies

A temple, faction, lineage, or guild may claim sole authority over a service or
ingredient. Monopoly can preserve standards and suppress competitors.

Ask whether residents can appeal, seek a second opinion, or learn the practice
themselves.

### Magical Debt

Life-saving work produces gratitude too large for ordinary repayment. Predatory
casters convert that gratitude into permanent obedience.

Good custom sets a finite due. Saving a child does not purchase the child's
future marriage.

### Price Anecdote: The Winter Healer

A healer accepted no bedside coin but received one measure from every household
after harvest. A newcomer called the arrangement charity and offered silver.

The healer refused. Accepting private priority would tell the village that the
shared grain no longer bought equal claim.

## Consent, Evidence, and Magical Crime

### Consent Must Name the Effect

Consent to healing is not consent to memory work. Consent to a dream visit is
not consent to probe every dream. Consent to carry a symbol is not consent to an
unknown command bound within it.

Explain range, duration, likely sensation, casting mode, and meaningful risk in
terms the person can understand.

### Capacity

Children, unconscious people, terrified captives, cognitively injured people,
and those under magical influence may lack free capacity.

Kin or office can authorize necessary care under local custom. It cannot make
every convenient intrusion ethical.

### Coercive Circumstances

A choice between magical examination and banishment is coerced even if the
target speaks agreement.

Coercion may be legally accepted under grave threat. Name it honestly.

### Mental and Dream Intrusion

Mentalism and oneiromancy can alter or inspect domains in which ordinary
witnesses cannot observe the act directly.

Communities may require a sworn purpose, limited question, independent caster,
and record made immediately afterward.

The examiner's word cannot prove itself.

### Shapeshifting and Identity

Changed appearance complicates recognition, marriage, debt, testimony,
inheritance, and accusation.

Custom may use countersigns, private memories, kin witnesses, scent, known scars,
or magical examination. None should be treated as infallible.

### Symbolic Traps

A mark can remain after its maker leaves. Responsibility may lie with writer,
commissioner, installer, building owner, or person who knowingly concealed it.

Public spaces need rules about unexplained signs.

### Necromancy and the Dead

Speaking with or raising the dead touches corpse custody, burial, inheritance,
religion, testimony, and grief.

The dead may possess knowledge without becoming neutral witnesses. Magic does
not erase memory, loyalty, or the terms of the spell.

### Magical Evidence

Magic can reveal, distort, compel, imitate, or manufacture signs. Courts should
combine magical evidence with ordinary witness, material trace, motive, and
chain of custody.

A caster explaining an effect is an expert witness with interests, not the
voice of the world.

### Prohibited Practice

Blood, Death, and Demonic Magic are prohibited in most settlements under the
Chapter 7 rarity guide. Prohibition drives practice into patronage, remote
places, criminal networks, and institutions powerful enough to exempt
themselves.

Law must still distinguish possession, study, ingredient traffic, witnessed
casting, coerced assistance, and harm.

### Investigation

Investigators secure the place, separate witnesses, preserve residues, record
sensory signs, examine ordinary causes, and consult more than one tradition when
possible.

Beginning with a suspect and fitting every sign to that person is persecution
wearing procedure.

### Confession

Magical fear makes confession unreliable. People admit to impossible workings
under torture, exhaustion, doctrine, or the hope that a named offense is safer
than continued uncertainty.

A confession should contain verifiable method unavailable through rumor.

### Punishment

Possible responses include compensation, supervision, loss of office,
ingredient restriction, book custody, exile, service, oath, imprisonment, and
execution.

Punishment that ignores the caster's network may scatter students, texts, and
dangerous materials beyond all oversight.

## Sorcerers and Druids

### Overlapping Social Roles

Sorcerer and druid name different magical professions and disciplines, but
communities often judge the person before the category.

Either may advise, heal through other competence, teach, travel, investigate,
serve a faction, or become feared.

### Sorcerers

Sorcerers are commonly associated with learned imposition, grimoires, private
masters, difficult arts, and powers whose legitimacy rests upon competence or
patronage rather than communal rite.

These associations are common rather than universal. A sorcerer may remain
rooted in one village for life.

### Druids

Druids are commonly associated with living cycles and the healing of bodies.
Their dealings with animals and weather tie them to particular places. Dream
and religious service give those dealings a public meaning.

Public acceptance can hide real danger. Known status does not make every druidic
working harmless or consensual.

### Rival Accounts

A sorcerer may call druidic language metaphor wrapped around technique. A druid
may call sorcery a hand that demands before it listens.

Both can produce accurate criticism and self-serving caricature.

### Cooperation

Mixed magical households can compare signs, divide services, and recognize what
one discipline misses.

Cooperation also creates disputes over credit, payment, doctrine, and which
method receives public legitimacy.

## The Disciplines in Society

### General Spellcraft

General spells circulate across disciplinary boundaries as the common handwork
of trained magic. Their broad use makes them a point of contact among otherwise
rival lineages.

Because no separate Path governs them, their social authority comes from the
caster's actual discipline and teacher. A general working performed by a healer
is trusted differently from the same effect performed by a concealed
necromancer.

General spellcraft appears in travel, communication, protection, practical
alteration, and the small necessities around larger work.

Familiarity hides its danger. People may treat a broadly known effect as
socially neutral when the same working amounts to spying, trespass, fraud, or
threat in its present use.

### Healing

Healing is known and commonly taught through initiation. Healers become part of
birth, injury, fever, recovery, disability, and death even when their magic
cannot answer every condition.

The healer's household requires clean water, linen, fuel, food for attendants,
quiet space, ordinary remedies, and people able to provide long care after the
spell's immediate work.

Magical healing can create false expectations that recovery has ended. Mended
flesh may leave hunger, fear, infection risk, lost labor, or the circumstances
that caused injury.

Triage is political. The healer who has willpower for one intervention must
choose among loved child, skilled elder, armed protector, paying traveler, and
person most likely to survive.

Settlements protect trusted healers and can confine them through gratitude. A
healer who wishes to travel may be accused of abandoning people who never
agreed that service was lifelong.

### Shapeshifting

Shapeshifting is strange and requires demonstration. Its practitioners learn
animals through posture, appetite, sense, route, fear, and the limits of a body
unlike their own.

Hunters, scouts, herders, messengers, and wardens value the path. Households fear
identity fraud, spying, predation, and the possibility that repeated change
alters a person's loyalties.

Communities develop recognition customs that do not depend only upon face. A
returning shifter may answer a private question, submit to kin scent, repeat an
oath, or show a kept token.

The path complicates animal law. Killing a wolf near a known shifter can become
a suspected murder. A shifter using animal form to take livestock may be judged
as thief, predator, or both.

Good practitioners warn households before using familiar domestic shapes.
Nobody wants to wonder whether every cat near the bed is listening.

### Awareness

Awareness is strange but available through initiation. Its practitioners make
hidden relation, distant attention, and uncertain sign socially actionable.

They serve as scouts, investigators, route advisers, counselors, and witnesses
whose value lies in asking the right question before danger becomes visible.

Awareness can produce dependence upon consultation. A ruler who will not decide
without signs gives the interpreter power while denying that power exists.

The discipline also injures privacy. Knowing that attention can cross distance
changes how councils meet, lovers speak, and hidden people sleep.

Wise practitioners distinguish perception from interpretation. “I sensed
hostility” should not become “the accused planned murder” without other proof.

### Symbolism

Symbolism is disturbing and taught after demonstration. It gives persistent
force to prepared marks, boundaries, names, and arranged meaning.

Symbolists work as ward-makers, seal-cutters, curse-breakers, inspectors,
architectural advisers, and dangerous saboteurs.

Their craft joins magic to literacy and surface. Doors, bridges, weapons,
clothing, skin, milestones, graves, and contracts may carry effects beyond the
moment of writing.

Public trust requires recognizable ownership marks and registers of lawful
wards. Secret symbols placed in common spaces make every scratch suspect.

Erasure is a skilled act. Chiseling the visible mark may trigger, release, or
leave the working attached to the damaged surface.

### Stone Song

Stone Song is strange and guarded by demonstration. Its practitioners listen to
weight, fracture, memory, pressure, and the slow intentions of worked and
unworked earth.

They advise mines, wells, walls, roads, bridges, quarries, tombs, and stronghold
construction. A competent singer can save years of labor and expose a lord's
dangerous haste.

Stone work creates ownership disputes. If magic opens a seam, who owns the ore:
landholder, laborers, singer, clan, or settlement that supplied the work?

Miners may trust a singer's warning and resent magical authority over knowledge
earned underground. Good practice joins the singer's perception to miners'
daily evidence.

A stone singer's refusal can halt construction. Political patrons therefore
seek exclusive service and may imprison the very competence they claim to
honor.

### Blood Magic

Blood Magic is prohibited and hidden behind a journey. It makes lineage, life,
injury, sacrifice, and bodily relation into instruments of sorcery.

Its ingredients and effects create immediate questions of consent. Blood given
for healing, kin recognition, or one rite cannot be assumed available for
another working.

Practitioners hide within medicine, slaughter, warfare, kin ritual, and elite
patronage where blood already changes hands.

The path attracts rulers who want power through descent and enemies who need a
simple explanation for hereditary misfortune.

Even defensible use leaves dangerous precedent. A village saved through willing
blood may later face an officer who calls unwilling taking the same necessity.

### Death Magic

Death Magic is prohibited and requires a journey to learn. Its practitioners
study the border between living person, corpse, memory, spirit, and the forces
that remain after breath.

They can provide knowledge the bereaved desperately desire and create forms of
servitude that deny the dead rest.

Corpse custody belongs to kin, faith, custom, and public health before a
necromancer enters. Secret practice commonly begins by stealing access rather
than bodies.

Rulers desire dead witnesses, tireless labor, terror, and escape from mortality.
Each desire corrodes ordinary succession and law.

Necromancers may describe themselves as investigators of truth. Judge the truth
claim beside the bodies required to sustain it.

### Elemental Magic

Elemental Magic is disturbing, rare outside its native region, and learned
through journey. It manipulates existing earth, wind, water, and fire rather
than creating convenient matter from nothing.

Its practitioners become valuable around flood, fire, mine, sea travel,
fortification, drought, and dangerous terrain.

The required environment makes settlement planning part of magic. Water access,
firebreaks, stone chambers, and exposed heights become both tools and hazards.

People fear the discipline because its material is everywhere. A hearth, river,
wall, or storm may appear to give the caster a weapon already inside daily life.

Elemental workers need public limits. Manipulating the millstream affects every
household downstream.

### Ice Affinity

Ice Affinity is strange and taught by initiation in the north. It is difficult
to sustain in warm regions, where snow and ice become seasonal cargo.

Its casters support travel, preservation, water supply, defense, shelter, and
the reading of frozen country.

Ice work changes roads and risk. A new path across water may invite carts onto a
surface whose duration they do not understand.

Warm-country patrons may display an ice-affinity druid as exotic proof of reach.
The caster then lives far from the climate, teachers, and ingredients that make
the discipline ordinary.

Communities in cold country judge the path by whether it respects winter's
existing dangers rather than treating them as spectacle.

### Nature

Nature is known and taught through initiation. It joins plant, weather, wood,
path, glade, and living place to druidic service.

Farmers, foresters, hunters, builders, and settlements seek its help. Their
needs conflict: more crop, protected grove, clear road, hidden trail, cut wood,
and preserved habitat cannot always be granted together.

Nature magic can conceal extraction beneath green language. Sudden growth may
exhaust soil, change drainage, or favor one household's field.

Druids acquire authority by predicting consequences whose proof arrives next
season. This demands trust and creates opportunity for self-serving mystery.

Good nature practice names who will weed, prune, harvest, repair, and live with
the altered place after the spell.

### Swarm Magic

Swarm Magic is disturbing and taught after demonstration. Its practitioners
work with insects, spiders, worms, rot, consumption, return, and the many small
bodies that clean and remake the world.

They can aid corpse disposal, agriculture, investigation, pest control, waste,
pollination, and battle. Communities often see only plague and infestation.

A blanket of insects is visible, mobile, and difficult to keep socially neutral.
Neighbors need warning about food stores, sleeping places, animals, and the
Quarter Day during which it may linger.

Swarm workers live near materials others call filth. Their households require
contained breeding spaces, dry stores, careful waste, and tolerant neighbors.

Death mages may benefit when people mistake swarm practice for necromancy. A
skilled investigator learns the difference without assuming either path harmless.

### Magma Song

Magma Song is disturbing, native to the Bloodmarches, and learned through a
journey. Its doctrine hears matter's memory and unrealized possibility through
poetry. Its practitioners read heat and stone by what they remember. Ash and
steam are the nearer voices of volcanic force.

Magma singers support forges, hot water, stone repair, fertility through ash,
mining, tunneling, transport, and war.

Their work demands knowledge of material history. Limestone and sandstone do
not answer as stone born from magma does. Builders need such distinctions before
promising results.

Poetry is both technical competence and religious practice within the path.
Patrons who hear only ornament have misunderstood how the singer attends to
matter.

Every large working raises downstream questions: ash on fields, steam in rooms,
fractured watercourses, opened mineral, and heat remaining after the singer
leaves.

### Mentalism

Mentalism is strange, guarded by demonstration, and rare outside its native
traditions. It acts upon perception, memory, will, and the mind's relation to
body.

It requires directed attention through speech rather than a physical ingredient
for its ordinary advantage. Conversation can therefore become an instrument
without the listener knowing when mundane influence ends.

Mentalists serve as negotiators, healers of some injuries, interrogators,
protectors, performers, advisers, and covert political weapons.

Consent must be narrow and witnessed. Permission to calm fear does not permit
amnesia. Permission to test a memory does not permit planting one.

Courts employing mentalists undermine every later confession unless procedure
shows when influence was absent.

### Oneiromancy

Oneiromancy is disturbing, learned through journey, and practiced through sleep,
trance, visitation, shared dream, prophecy, and the dangerous country of an
unwaking mind.

Its practitioners help with rest, nightmares, messages, insight, and forms of
travel or prediction unavailable to waking methods.

The sleeping body requires guards, warmth, time, and trust. A dreamer's household
is partly an infirmary and partly a watch post.

Dream testimony is vivid and unstable. Symbols may be path truth, personal
memory, another mind's intrusion, or the dreamer's interpretation after waking.

Entering a dream is entry into intimacy. Good custom treats it like entering a
locked home, with purpose, limit, witness, and an obligation to leave what was
not sought.

### Magnetism

Magnetism is disturbing, learned through journey, and guarded within an iron
warrior tradition. It works through metal, attraction, repulsion, bodily iron,
weapon, direction, and forces most people perceive only through effects.

Military use is obvious: disarmament, protection, movement, weapon control, and
the turning of metal against its bearer.

Civil uses include finding metal, surveying, stabilizing damaged objects,
recovering tools, controlling dangerous loads, and working where ordinary hands
cannot reach.

The path changes armament custom. Visitors may be required to surrender metal
before entering a magnetist's hall, while the caster's own command of every nail
and hinge remains unexamined.

Because the discipline is institutionally guarded, teaching it is an act of
political defection as well as magical transmission.

### Demonic Magic

Demonic Magic is prohibited, journey-guarded, and hunted. It works through mog,
corruption, altered flesh, demons, and breaches toward Churmog.

Its hidden practice occupies clandestine laboratories and requires resistant
vessels and contaminated carriers. It breeds blackmail, mutilated assistants,
collectors of forbidden knowledge, and patrons convinced that they alone can
control the consequences.

Some practitioners seek weapons against demons. That purpose does not make mog
safe, remove corruption, or guarantee that the institution hunting them will
distinguish study from service.

Settlements should recognize containers, burns, hot-copper and bile odor,
discolored tissue, corroded storage, and unusual interest in demon remains as
grounds for careful investigation.

The full metaphysics of demons, mog, Churmog, and Merigall belongs to the
campaign-spoiler reference. Do not reveal it merely because a social clue appears.

### Disciplines in Combination

Casters who know more than one discipline create useful and frightening
combinations. Healing and mentalism can care or violate. Stone Song and
Symbolism can build lasting wards. Nature and Swarm Magic can restore a field or
make it uninhabitable.

Law organized by single path may fail to address the combined act.

Teachers often fear cross-training because another doctrine changes how the
student interprets limits once treated as natural.

## Magic and Religion

### Rite and Spell

Rite orders relations among worshipper, kin, dead, season, place, and god. A
spell produces a defined magical effect through a learned discipline. The same
act may contain both without making them identical.

A priest can conduct rites without casting. A caster can work without religious
office. Communities may still require sacred permission for certain effects.

### Religious Legitimacy

Faith supplies explanations for who should cast, what purpose is proper, which
ingredients may be taken, and what must follow a dangerous success.

Legitimacy protects some practitioners and exposes others. A sanctioned working
may receive witnesses, space, and repair. An unsanctioned one is pushed toward
secrecy where harm is harder to contain.

### Competing Doctrines

Different religions can accept the same effect for contrary reasons. One calls
healing restoration of given form. Another calls it fulfillment of living
potential. A third suspects theft from the suffering appointed to a person.

The effect does not settle the theology.

### Sacred Ingredients

Temple lands, graves, relics, ritual animals, consecrated fire, and offerings may
also be magically useful.

Use in a spell requires the authority that keeps the sacred relation. Purchase
from a corrupt custodian does not guarantee religious legitimacy.

### Clerical Casters

A caster holding religious office answers to both path competence and doctrine.
The institution may supervise students, keep grimoires, collect ingredients,
judge mishaps, and assign service.

Office can create accountability or protect abuse from outside examination.

### Miracles and Misattribution

People may call an unexplained mercy a miracle and a hated spell a fraud even
when both used known magic.

Religious meaning concerns relation and interpretation. Reconstructing the
technique answers a narrower question.

### Anathema

Religious anathemas mark workings, ingredients, targets, and purposes held to
rupture right order.

Anathema has practical consequences: refusal of burial, marriage, hospitality,
teaching, or witness. It can isolate a caster more thoroughly than civil fine.

### Cleansing

Cleansing after magic may combine washing, smoke, prayer, confession, disposal,
fasting, repair, and reconciliation with people exposed.

Even where the rite has no mechanical effect, it can close social danger and
restore a place to ordinary use.

## Magic and Political Power

### The Court Caster

Court service grants materials, protection, access, and influence. It also makes
every working part of a ruler's reputation.

The caster may advise privately and be displayed publicly. Rivals will assume
greater involvement than actually exists.

### Magical Counsel

Rulers seek signs before marriage, war, journey, judgment, and succession.
Counsel becomes dangerous when possibility is treated as command.

The caster should distinguish observed condition, path interpretation, and
personal recommendation.

### Faction Assets

Factions value casters for communication, intelligence, healing, fortification,
weather, terror, concealment, transport, and legitimacy.

One caster rarely sustains all these demands. A GM's faction notes should record
the household and ingredients that make magical service repeatable.

### Magical Decrees

Authorities may license teachers, register grimoires, forbid ingredients,
require mishap reports, claim monster remains, or restrict casting within walls.

Enforcement determines whether the decree improves safety or creates a bribe
market.

### Countermagic and Security

Powerful households use layered security: ordinary guards, controlled access,
countersigns, inspected surfaces, food practice, dream watches, metal rules,
animal familiarity, and more than one magical adviser.

No single caster should certify the whole system that employs that caster.

### Succession

A ruler's death changes the caster's patron, obligations, secrets, and safety.
The heir may inherit service by contract while lacking the trust that made it
work.

Court casters often know facts capable of deciding succession and destroying
their own credibility whichever side they take.

### Magical Deterrence

Rumored power can protect a settlement without being used. The rumor must remain
credible enough that enemies account for it and uncertain enough that they
cannot prepare a perfect answer.

False deterrence invites catastrophe when tested.

### Captive Casters

Imprisoning a caster does not produce loyal service. It produces sabotage,
misdirection, coerced casting, self-harm, or a prisoner waiting for the one
moment guards must loosen control.

Institutions still attempt it because scarce power makes moral failure look
strategic.

## Magic after the Blood Mist

### Enclosed Traditions

The Blood Mist isolated teachers, ingredients, students, and manuscript lines.
Some disciplines survived in one valley through a single household. Others
became names attached to effects nobody nearby could reproduce.

Isolation encouraged local variation and the belief that one lineage's method
was the whole path.

### Lost Teachers

An apprentice whose master died during enclosure might preserve Rank 1 or 2
practice for generations without access to correction.

Families turned lessons into sayings, chores, and prohibitions. The descendants
may not know which domestic custom once contained magical technique.

### Reopened Roads

The lifting Mist joins traditions separated long enough to call one another
corrupt. Teachers compare grimoires, discover shared errors, seek lost materials,
and compete for students.

Travel spreads remedies and dangerous workings at the same pace.

### Ingredient Shock

Materials once unobtainable arrive through caravan. Local substitutes lose
value. Gatherers discover distant buyers will pay more than neighbors.

New demand can strip rare sources before custom learns restraint.

### The Return of Prosecution

Isolation allowed some settlements to tolerate the only caster available. A
reconnected church or faction may now demand conformity and retrospective
judgment.

People forced to choose between outside legitimacy and the practitioner who kept
them alive do not experience the issue as abstract law.

### Magical Pilgrimage

Casters travel to meet lineages known only from damaged notes. They carry
questions, gifts, local spells, and diseases of thought as well as body.

The returned pilgrim may find home practice narrower than remembered and home
neighbors suspicious of foreign correction.

### Ruins

Reopened ruins may contain grimoires beside spaces built for ritual. Persistent
effects and failed experiments make their recovery dangerous. Ingredients left
inside may also be claimed by heirs who could not reach them during the Mist.

Recovery is scholarship, salvage, hazard, and politics.

### A Generation of New Students

Young people can now seek teachers beyond the settlement. Their departure
removes labor and their return introduces other loyalties.

Older local practitioners may welcome successors and fear being exposed as
incomplete.

## Charlatans, Accusations, and Witch Fear

### The Useful Charlatan

A charlatan may sell harmless ritual, comfort, herbs, theater, and attention
where no true caster is available.

Fraud becomes dangerous when it delays care, names innocent enemies, uses
poisonous materials, or claims authority over choices the client cannot recover.

### The Real Caster Who Exaggerates

Some practitioners possess one genuine discipline and claim competence across
many. Witnessed magic makes every later boast plausible.

Ask for lineage, limits, and prior cases outside the caster's chosen display.

### Accusation as Social Weapon

Magic accusations can remove rivals, widows, foreigners, healers, creditors,
unusual children, and people who know an inconvenient fact.

The accusation gains force from a real culture of magical danger. Its abuse does
not make all concern foolish.

### Pattern Hunger

After crop failure, infant death, strange weather, or repeated illness, people
seek one intention behind many losses.

A named witch offers a body upon which uncertainty can be punished.

Good leadership investigates material causes while providing rites and public
work that let fear move without selecting a victim.

### Signs and Proof

Hidden ingredients, unfamiliar writing, scars, solitary behavior, animal
familiarity, foreign accent, or survival of disease may become “signs.”

Each can justify a question. None alone proves a casting.

### Professional Hunters

Investigators can develop real expertise in residue, prohibited networks,
containment, and interviewing. They can also profit from continuous threat.

Judge a hunter by cases closed without execution, errors admitted, and the fate
of confiscated property.

### Crowd Dynamics

Fear moves through proximity. People repeat details they heard moments earlier
as personal memory. Public hesitation becomes suspicion.

Separate witnesses and slow irreversible acts.

### Sanctuary for Examination

A neutral shrine or household may shelter the accused while evidence is
examined. Sanctuary must also protect witnesses and prevent flight.

The examiner should have no claim upon property forfeited by guilt.

### After Acquittal

Formal innocence does not restore marriage prospects, patients, lost animals,
or the safety of a house whose hiding places were exposed.

Repair requires public speech from the authority that gave the accusation
weight.

### When the Accusation Is True

A guilty caster may still have been tortured falsely, accused of harms not
committed, or used by a patron who remains untouched.

Truth should sharpen judgment rather than justify every act performed in its
name.

## A Caster's Household

### The Working Room

The working room needs controllable light, ventilation, a clear floor, washable
surfaces, storage, water, and a route by which frightened people can leave.

Its objects should reveal discipline through use rather than decoration.

### The Store

Ingredients are labeled by source and date, separated by danger, inspected for
spoilage, and counted after every working.

The person keeping the store may know more about the practice's real frequency
than any student.

### The Threshold

Households establish who may enter, which objects remain outside, when animals
are admitted, and what visitors must disclose.

Threshold custom protects privacy and containment.

### Assistants

Assistants fetch, witness, restrain, record, clean, tend flame, keep time, watch
sleep, and care for the caster afterward.

Their competence deserves training and payment. Calling them servants does not
reduce the risk.

### Ordinary Livelihood

Many casters also farm, heal without magic, keep records, carve, smith, hunt,
teach, trade, or advise. Ordinary work feeds the household when willpower is low
and roots magical reputation in visible competence.

### Family Privacy

Clients arrive during meals, sleep, grief, and family argument. A famous caster's
household can lose the right to close its own door.

Set receiving hours and an emergency signal.

### Children in the House

Children learn danger boundaries early: which shelf, room, vessel, mark, animal,
or sleeping guest must not be touched.

Familiarity can make them careful and can make outsiders suspect them of powers
they do not possess.

### Neighbors

Neighbors smell smoke, hear chanting, lend buckets, report strangers, watch the
roof, and bear immediate consequence when containment fails.

A prudent caster maintains those relations before needing help.

### Daily Rhythm

Morning brings storage checks, ordinary work, patients, correspondence, and
preparation. Day permits travel and gathered assistance. Evening favors copying,
teaching, and consultation. Night belongs to rest, watches, dream practice, and
effects best kept from crowds.

The rhythm changes with path, season, and secrecy.

### Death of the Householder

When the caster dies, nobody should rush blindly into the room. Secure children,
animals, fire, water, vessels, active effects, and texts.

Summon a competent practitioner and an independent witness before dividing
property.

## Rendering Social Magic in Play

### Begin before the Spell

Show who requested the work, who gathered the ingredient, who will witness, and
what ordinary solution was rejected.

Preparation makes casting consequential without adding mechanical complexity.

### Continue after Success

Ask who cleans, rests, pays, interprets, and tells the story. A successful spell
can still create debt, envy, precedent, and ecological change.

### Give a Caster Limits

Record one service the caster performs reliably, one work refused, one mishap
remembered locally, and one relationship that makes practice possible.

Together they show the person who must live with the spell list.

### Give a Community Competence

Residents know how to prepare the healer's room, clear the singer's wall, keep
watch over a dreamer, or avoid a symbolist's fresh marks.

Competent communities make magic feel integrated without making it common.

### Use Sensory Doctrine

Describe warmth gathering behind a spent body, grit moving against weight,
insects tightening into shared direction, iron humming through a hinge, or a
dreamer's breath falling out of rhythm.

Sensation should reveal the path's relation to the world, not replace rules with
fog.

### Make Rarity Visible

Known magic receives a prepared space and established price. Strange magic
draws watchers. Disturbing magic changes hospitality. Prohibited magic changes
who can safely know the caster's name.

### Do Not Make Every Reaction Hostile

Fear may produce bargaining, deference, concealment, fascination, regulation,
dependency, recruitment, or quiet avoidance.

Violence is one possible response and often the least informative.

### Questions for a Magical Service

- Who asked, and who else will bear the result?
- What does the recipient believe the spell can do?
- Which casting modes are acceptable?
- Who supplies willpower conditions, ingredient, space, and witnesses?
- What happens if the caster burns?
- Which mishap can this place contain poorly?
- What payment creates obligation after the effect?
- Which authority may investigate?
- What ordinary work follows?
- Who tells the story in the next settlement?

### Questions for a Teacher

- Who taught this person?
- What proof is required before instruction?
- Which lesson is embedded in household work?
- What error does the lineage fear most?
- What does the teacher refuse to record?
- Which student left badly?
- Who owns copied grimoires?
- What compensation keeps teaching possible?
- Which faction wants control of the line?
- What happens to the apprentices if the teacher dies?

### Questions for a Magical Dispute

- Was the effect consented to, and how narrowly?
- Which mode was declared?
- What preparation was promised?
- Who ordered or funded the work?
- What ordinary cause has been excluded?
- Which evidence is magical, and who interprets it?
- What harm remains after physical repair?
- Which punishment would scatter greater danger?
- What precedent will dependents remember?
- What truth can be admitted without destroying every useful relation?

### Anecdote: The Page beneath the Floor

A healer died without a will. Her daughter inherited the house, her apprentice
claimed the working book, and the village claimed the pages recording a ward
paid for through twenty years of grain.

They found the ward page beneath the floor rather than in the grimoire. The
healer had separated it so no single heir could quietly sell the village's
protection.

### Anecdote: The Safe Flame

An elemental caster agreed to clear a winter blockage from a mill channel. The
lord demanded chance casting for greater effect before the thaw.

The miller refused to open the yard unless the lord stood beside the grain store
and pledged his own timber for repair. The lord chose safe sufficiency once the
risk could no longer be left entirely to other households.

### Anecdote: The Dream Testimony

A dreamer reported seeing a reeve accept a hidden purse. The reeve denied it.
The village nearly judged on the vision alone.

An old carter asked which hand held the purse. The dreamer named the reeve's
right. Everyone who worked with him knew that hand had been stiff since youth.
The dream contained a true fear and a false body.

### Anecdote: The Swarm Keeper

A swarm druid cleared rot from a burial pit during fever season. Residents
thanked her and then barred her blanket of insects from the grain lane.

She accepted the boundary and asked for a fenced route to the pit. Trust grew
because useful power had permitted itself to be limited.

### Closing Principle

Magic becomes part of society through customs repeated around its dangers.
Teaching and witness govern who may act. Payment and storage reveal what the
working consumes. Consent must still be remembered when repair begins. A spell
may last only a moment. The discipline survives in prepared hands and in the
households left to live with what happened.
