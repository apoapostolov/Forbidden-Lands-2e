# Orc Religions of the Ravenlands

Orc religion begins with abandonment by the elder kin. The Unabandoned,
command, sacred time, surrender, death, altered bodies, shrines, song, and
pilgrimage give that memory a living form. Kinship, adulthood, hidden female
government, war, craft, and speech are in [Orc Culture](culture-orcs.md). Clan
histories named in Book 02 are manuscript facts. The Unabandoned and detailed
ceremonies are grounded setting inference.

---

## The Gods Who Left and the Kin Who Remained

Orcs possess no single public church. Elves and dwarves say Clay made them as
servants. Many orcs reject the story because it turns enslavement into creation
and obedience into nature. Their religious memory is an absence: elder masters
sent them against human invaders, withdrew, and left them to hold a war already
lost.

Some clans honor **the Unabandoned**, ancestors and living leaders who remained
when flight was possible. Their names are recited with the work, fighters, and
dependents they kept together. A glorious death that leaves the clan helpless
earns less honor than an ugly survival that carries others out.

Women tend genealogies and living continuity while males monopolize public
ritual, title, and command. Males duel for standing. Women covertly shape which
victories may become marriage, succession, alliance, or exile, then arrange for
a male to pronounce the result. Outsiders and most males hear only challenge
songs and believe the religious judgment belongs to the victorious chief.

**The Stone That Stayed** is a Roka rite. A monolith or smaller upright stone is
surrounded while commands from a past battle are recited. One order that should
have been disobeyed is named with the others. For a clan built on obedience, the
lesson bites: leaders must be obeyed, and leaders who spend the clan without
purpose become traitors to those who stayed.

Urhur ceremony borrows freely from human and elven courts. Once repeated, an
invented rite can become binding because people organized gifts, rank, and
marriage around it. Their priests care less whether a custom is old than
whether the emperor has performed it publicly and others have relied upon it.

Isir trials with vipers and wasps treat poison endured as kinship with a hostile
world. The trial proves body and will but also consumes many candidates.
Survivors carry religious authority in their altered saliva and split tongues.
A leader who tests only followers and never risks poison loses standing.

Viraga rites use fox tokens, holy plants, secrecy, and the hunt. Their deepest
dogma is that the kin must gain knowledge the former masters denied them. The
dogma can justify theft, abduction, and sacrifice. Within the clan, destroying
useful knowledge is a greater anathema than lying to male rulers.

Across clans, grave offenses include selling orcs into elder-kin service,
abandoning dependents during retreat, using surrender law to enslave someone
whose defeat was arranged by treachery, and giving an outsider the concealed
roads by which females and young escape war. Clans disagree over whether fear
itself is shameful or only action ruled by fear.

## Sources and Authority

Orc religion survives in clan practice and the challenge formula. Women's
genealogies support camp law. Initiation scars and burial places make it
visible. It also survives in commands people still dispute after the commander
is dead. No central priesthood can declare one creed.
An outsider asking for the orc god may be asking the wrong question.

Religious authority appears undivided. Public male leaders judge courage,
obedience, tribute, descent, adoption, marriage, and war. In secret, women guard
the genealogies, routes, stores, and ritual knowledge without which those
judgments cannot take effect. They shape whether a victory becomes succession
or leaves a clan capable of another winter, but a male voice must declare it.
Trial survivors carry bodily proof. Elders remember which splendid command
nearly ended the clan.

The concealment creates checks and hypocrisies. A male chief may proclaim an
heir, only for another male to discover the ritual flaw women prepared. A
Viraga may save the kin through knowledge gained by kidnapping someone else's
craftsperson. A Roka general may obey a disastrous order because admitting
doubt before battle would fracture the line.

### Belief Without Proof

Orc religion does not require a god to appear and settle an argument. Magic,
dreams, poison visions, improbable weather, and survival can all be treated as
sacred without proving one explanation. A storm that breaks an enemy pursuit
may be the work of the Unabandoned, good command, a hidden witch, or chance.
The event matters; its interpretation becomes a contest over present duty.

No priest can cite a single universal Orc creed. An adventurer knows the rites
of their household and clan, several hostile versions, and rumors about the
rest. The same ancestor may be a traitor in one recital and the rear guard who
saved the kin in another. Lore should reveal a situated account—who teaches it,
who benefits from it, and which obligation it supports—not a neutral catechism.

This approach follows a recurring r/ForbiddenLands preference for keeping the
gods indirect and religion local to settlements and traditions. It is community
practice rather than a new statement of canon. See [How much role do gods play
in your game?](https://www.reddit.com/r/ForbiddenLands/comments/16i8vn4/how_much_role_do_gods_play_in_your_game/) and
[How much do you tell players about religion?](https://www.reddit.com/r/ForbiddenLands/comments/xnkj6c/how_much_do_you_tell_players_about_religion/).

## Creation, Service, and the Elder Kin

Elves and dwarves say Clay made orcs to serve them. Many orcs hear a master's
claim hidden inside the creation story: we made you, so your freedom is theft.
Some reject Clay entirely. Others answer that a maker who abandons a
living work loses authority over it. A few clans retain Clay rites but name the
first free orc, rather than Clay, as the founder of their moral world.

The old alliance against humans remains part of orc memory. So does the elder
withdrawal. Religious arguments turn on the exact moment service ended. Did
the command fail when the commanders fled, when supplies ceased, when the war's
purpose changed, or only when living orcs refused it? Different answers support
different leaders now.

The Unabandoned are named with acts, not praise: held the ford until the young
crossed; carried seed instead of a second weapon; returned for the wounded;
refused an order that spent the last hunters. A name without an act does not
enter the recital.

## Daily Observance

At first food, one portion is assigned before warriors eat. Depending on clan,
it belongs to the watch, young, pregnant, injured, absent scouts, or whoever
kept the camp during the raid. The allocation states what the group believes
strength is for.

Weapons are named by obligation more often than by glory. A spear may be “the
road-keeper” because it guarded a retreat. Taking such a weapon without taking
its obligation is theft even when the owner is dead.

Commands are repeated back in altered words. Human observers mistake this for
poor discipline. The repetition proves that the hearer understood purpose as
well as sound. Roka officers formalize it. Isir leaders often punish it.
Drifter bands survive by insisting upon it.

Women keep the routes by which children, wounded people, stores, and knowledge
leave a failing camp. These paths are seldom sung publicly. Markers may be a
fox hair under bark, three stones below a root, or a cut made where a mounted
warrior will not see it.

## Death and the Last Command

Orc funerals distinguish the body from the command that outlived it. Weapons,
food, teeth, stones, or fox tokens vary by clan. The funeral must name which
orders remain binding.

At **The Last Command**, followers repeat one order from the dead leader. They
then state whether it ended with death, passes to a successor, or must never be
given again. A successor who refuses the hearing inherits rank without the
dead leader's religious standing.

Bodies lost in retreat are not automatically dishonored. A marker can be raised
at the last place the person was known to protect another. Recovering remains
matters less than recovering the truth of who was abandoned.

## Clan Traditions

### Urhur

Urhur ceremony makes rule visible through feast, costume, title, and public
invention. Emperor Hroka's newest rite can become binding once gifts and
marriages rely on it. The religious question is reliance, not age. This makes
Urhur ceremony adaptable, but also leaves it vulnerable to a ruler who changes
custom faster than people can settle its costs.

### Roka

Roka religion joins obedience to the duty of worthy command. Monoliths mark
orders expected to outlast a speaker. The Stone That Stayed includes one order
that should have been refused so discipline does not become worship of waste.

### Isir

Isir faith sacralizes survival in a hostile body and world. Venom, split tongue,
swarm, and pain create a caste whose proof cannot be borrowed. Their danger is
making every question of wisdom answerable through endurance.

### Viraga

Viraga treat denied knowledge as stolen inheritance. Fox tokens mark indirect
paths. Holy plants loosen speech before the hunt, while practical knowledge
captured from other kin enters guarded teaching. The rite can preserve the
orcs or turn teachers into prey after their usefulness ends.

### Drifters

Drifters carry fragments of clan practice without a fixed genealogy. They may
keep the Unabandoned by adding anyone who chose the band when departure was
easy. Clan priests call this theft. Drifters answer that staying created the
claim.

## Captivity, Surrender, and Enslavement

Because orcs were treated as a servant kin, captivity carries religious weight.
A prisoner may owe labor, ransom, exchange, or a bounded term. Turning capture
into hereditary ownership repeats the elder claim most clans condemn.

Practice often violates doctrine. Orc raiders take slaves. Viraga abduct
teachers. Isir bands kill surrendering enemies. Orcs remember enslavement
clearly and can reproduce it when power changes hands.

A captive seeking standing should be given a term that can be completed and a
witness who survives completion. Without both, service is slavery with ritual
words around it.

## Magic, Demons, and Altered Bodies

Roka hate magic and demons because they remember battlefield consequences.
Viraga pursue useful knowledge wherever it lies. Urhur rulers collect spectacle
and specialists. One orc camp may burn a sorcerer while another bargains for
the same spell before winter.

Altered bodies are judged through cause and obligation. An Isir's venom follows
accepted trial. A Misgrown limb imposed by a human master records assault. A
demonic gift accepted to save the clan may earn honor and permanent suspicion.
Appearance alone cannot settle the account.

## Sacred Time and the Camp Year

Orc calendars begin with needs rather than numbered months. The first safe
movement after thaw, the return of biting insects, the time when stored meat
must be eaten, and the last week in which young can cross exposed ground all
carry names. A fixed date matters less than the leader who notices the change
soon enough to act.

At **Opening the Old Road**, scouts walk the route by which the clan escaped its
last failed camp. They repair markers, clear deadfall, and move any store that
has become obvious. The ceremony honors survival by keeping retreat possible.
A chief who calls the road shameful may still be forced to supply the work.

The **Empty Pot Night** comes when the last winter store is deliberately opened.
Every household reports what remains before the food is combined. Hidden food
discovered afterward belongs first to those who went hungry while it was
concealed. Wealthy females sometimes keep a second reserve outside the count.
They defend the practice as provision against a lying chief.

At the first large hunt after winter, no victory song is permitted until the
butchery is finished and the return road marked. Isir hunters often ignore the
rule. Roka elders recite the names of bands that sang beside cooling meat and
lost it to rain, wolves, or an enemy patrol.

There is no kin-wide holy year. Urhur dates follow the emperor's court. Roka
count from campaigns and winters under particular generals. Isir remember
swarm and venom seasons. Viraga travel by plant, moon, and the private needs of
women moving between clans. When several clans meet, disagreement over the date
is part of the rite. It discloses who expected whose calendar to rule.

## Shrines, Relics, and Places That Remain

Orc shrines are often evidence of survival: a stone that covered a retreat, a
tree under which litters were hidden, a broken gate held after its fortress was
lost, or a cooking pit used through a siege. The place is maintained because an
act occurred there, not because a god is believed to live in the material.

Weapons of the Unabandoned may be carried, but their obligations travel with
them. A spear that guarded children cannot be used to threaten a child without
public contradiction. Owners sometimes rename a relic to escape such limits.
Elders keep the older name alive.

Fox fur under Viraga custody identifies route, office, and protected knowledge.
It is not a general charm. Taking a token from a dead Viraga can summon aid,
accusation, or a hunter sent to learn why the wrong person carries it.

Roka monoliths are inspected for new cuts. Names carved by relatives may turn a
military marker into a grievance record. An officer who orders the marks ground
away has made the stone smoother and the dispute harder.

Urhur shrines accumulate gifts copied from foreign courts. A bent Raven feather,
dwarven measure, elven vessel, and human saint-board may stand together because
the emperor accepted each in public. Priests invent explanations after the fact.
Reliance gives the collection weight even when its theology is nonsense.

## Enslaved Orcs and Religion beneath Command

Half the male population may live under slave status in clans that define fear
or surrender as permanent loss of standing. Their religion cannot be read only
from the chiefs who own their labor.

Enslaved orcs keep small accounts in work rhythm, tool placement, scars covered
by clothing, and portions shared after the recognized distribution. A master may
own the public song and remain unable to tell why every slave strikes the third
blow softly.

At **The Hand Still Held**, an enslaved person names one duty that did not end
with status: a child, wounded companion, dead sibling, hidden teacher, or refuge
warning. Another person accepts witness. The act preserves moral standing where
clan law has removed public standing.

Self-gelding may be used to quiet male rank and accept slavery. The act can end
challenge and reduce violence. It can also make mutilation look like consent to
a status imposed under defeat. Female keepers disagree over whether the person
enters another social category or remains a wronged male denied return.

Release requires more than a master's word. A weapon, food share, sleeping place,
and right to refuse challenge must be recognized before witnesses. An orc freed
at the camp edge without these protections can be enslaved again at the first
insult.

Some Viraga free useful people and recruit them. Others exploit the absence of
male standing to gain informants no chief will notice. Rescue can become a new
secret command.

## Song, Dream, and the Unreliable Ancestor

Orc song preserves command, insult, route, defeat, and the names public leaders
wish remembered. A song's survival proves repetition, not accuracy. Women and
enslaved workers often keep the missing verse.

At **Singing the Other Place**, a performer repeats a known battle song from the
position of the rear guard, camp keeper, captive, or abandoned wounded. The
alternate voice changes whose labor made the victory possible even when the
outcome remains the same.

Dreams carry authority unevenly. Hroka follows dreams shaped through an elven
ruby without knowing it. Roka distrust a command received in sleep because no
subordinate could repeat it back. Isir accept dreams after venom trials and
often confuse bodily poison with proof.

Viraga cultivate dreams through plants, suggestion, and controlled information.
They know that a dream can guide a ruler more safely than an order spoken by a
woman he refuses to obey. Their success makes the ruler's inner life another
place held by the network.

An ancestor appearing in dream must name an act not already known to the sleeper
before receiving ritual weight. Even then, elders inspect whether a living person
benefits from the message. The dead are difficult to question and easy to recruit.

## Internal Pilgrimage and the Meeting of Clans

Orcs travel to battle stones, abandoned camps, poison grounds, fox paths, and
fortresses taken from elder kin. No destination belongs equally to all clans.
Pilgrimage can bring clans into contact without uniting them.

At **The Four Campfires**, visiting groups keep separate fires around one neutral
ground. Each brings food it can afford to lose and one unresolved command. The
commands are heard before challenge. A fight afterward does not erase what the
other clans learned.

Urhur delegations arrive with titles and gifts. Roka inspect exits and count
armed people. Isir test the surrounding ground for prey and insult. Viraga may
have arranged the meeting while appearing only as wives, healers, or captives.

Drifters use gatherings to request return, find relatives, sell routes, and learn
which chiefs intend to call them traitors. Their band-made ancestors offend clan
keepers because they prove continued choice can compete with birth.

A pilgrimage site controlled by one clan becomes toll, hostage ground, and
source of doctrine. The best-known sites retain several approaches so no single
gatekeeper can turn remembrance into ownership.

## Fault, Repair, and Anathema

An orc who fails a duty can repair standing through replacement labor, public
admission, protection of those endangered, return of stolen clan knowledge, or
a dangerous service chosen by the injured group. Trial without repair proves
only that the offender can suffer.

Acts beyond ordinary repair include selling kin into permanent service,
revealing refuge roads for personal safety, arranging defeat to claim surrender
rights, destroying knowledge needed by the clan, and giving an order meant to
kill followers as a way to remove rivals.

The worst leaders make followers bear an ordeal that the leader names holy and
will not share. Orc religion contains enough memory of abandonment to recognize
the pattern. Recognition does not guarantee resistance.

## Evidence at the Table

Show orc belief through a command repeated back with one crucial word changed or
a fox hair hidden beneath bark. A genealogy no male chief may touch can do the
same, as can an upright stone carrying a condemned order or a feast portion
assigned to someone absent.

Religious disputes ask who remained and who was carried out. They test whether a
command still binds, who may teach captured knowledge, and when service becomes
the slavery every clan claims to hate.
