# Power, Factions, and Post-Mist Rule in the Ravenlands

This reference explains how rule is lived and remembered. It also shows how it
is resisted, then repaired
in Apostol Apostolov's expanded Ravenlands campaign. Book 02 supplies the
faction procedures. This document supplies the people beneath those procedures:
the households that surrender grain and the ferrymen who decide whether a seal
is still feared. Hostages make an oath costly. Retainers must decide whether an
unpaid order remains an order.

Power in the Ravenlands seldom begins with a border. It begins with a claim
that can reach a road, a storehouse, or a body. A ruler is real where someone
lowers the ferry rope at that ruler's word. A church is real where a household
keeps back grain for its collector. A clan chief is real where cousins leave
their own harvest to answer the horn. Beyond those acts lies title without
weight.

## Authority and Method

### Claim Levels

This document uses five levels of authority.

**Published canon** names people and factions. It also governs published places,
histories, religions, and relations. A canon name does not make
every consequence drawn from it canon.

**Manuscript fact** comes from the current second-edition rules and setting
chapters. Faction terms belong to this layer:

- Mandate, Force, Reach, and Hearth
- Ruled, Protected, Tributary, and Vassal
- Occupied and Allied

**Historical model** supplies credible pre-feudal ways of organizing land and
service. Compensation and witness support judgment. Household authority and
hospitality persist into war.
Such models are tools. The Ravenlands are not a displaced earthly kingdom.

**Setting inference** follows from canon under the conditions created by ten
generations of Blood Mist and broken roads. Living non-human kin remember older
claims. Real gods and magic share the land with walkers and monsters. An
inference should answer a practical question
left open by the books.

**Personal campaign lore** is Apostol Apostolov's established answer for this
campaign. It remains authoritative here without being presented as published
canon.

When a distinction changes play, name it. A family may insist that its toll
right is canon because a dead lord granted it. The GM may know that the right is
personal campaign lore built upon an unreadable seal. Both facts can coexist.
The first motivates the family. The second gives someone leverage.

### The Political Constraint Chain

Read every institution through this chain:

`need -> contribution -> authority -> visible act -> refusal -> consequence`

A bridge needs timber and watch. Households contribute labor. The bridge keeper
receives authority over passage. A barrier is lowered at dusk. One household
refuses its repair days. The keeper closes the bridge to its carts, or fails to
do so and reveals that the office has become hollow.

The chain prevents power from becoming fog. A lord who claims a valley must
name what the valley gives and what the lord returns. Someone must carry each
demand; failure must have a known consequence. Without those answers there is only a title
spoken beside a fire.

### Politics Is a Distribution Problem

Rule distributes five things:

- food and stored goods
- dangerous work and armed service
- protection and exposure
- judgment and recognized truth
- access to roads, markets, shrines, and people

Every political act changes at least one distribution. Remitting a due leaves
grain in one village and removes it from a garrison. Granting sanctuary protects
one fugitive and transfers danger to the household that shelters them. Calling
levy moves strong backs from fields to roads. Declaring an heir changes who may
marry or inherit keys. It also changes who commands retainers and receives
hostages.

Faith and honor are common declarations. Blood, necessity, and revenge are
others. The GM should still trace the sacks and bodies, then the witnesses and
gates.

### Institutions Must Be Worked

A court exists because people sweep its floor and heat the hall. Others carry
summons or remember procedure. Guests must be fed; angry kin must be restrained,
and judgments repeated after the ruler has left. A faction with a grand title and no such workers rules only
while its armed party remains within reach.

Institutions survive through repeated small acts. The steward opens the tally
chest before witnesses. The hostage keeper shows a child alive at the expected
festival. The ferryman recognizes a seal. The priest speaks a ruler's name in
the correct place in the rite. The gate guard admits the messenger at night.

Break enough repetitions and the institution dies before anyone announces it.

### Political Knowledge Is Positioned

No observer sees the whole faction.

A lord sees promises and reports, especially missing revenue. A steward sees
labor and stores, including false measures. A village elder sees which households can still
pay. A hostage sees the temper of the hall. A groom sees night rides. A priest
sees who refuses public witness. A servant sees who eats after a failed levy.

Treat political truth as an assembly of positioned accounts. Contradiction is
evidence. If the steward calls a village disloyal and the miller calls it empty,
look for the sacks that never reached either of them.

### The Sandbox Test

Every substantial political element should answer six questions.

1. Who benefits while the arrangement holds?
2. Who pays its ordinary cost?
3. What visible act proves it still holds?
4. Who can interrupt that act?
5. What substitute becomes possible after interruption?
6. What worsens if no one intervenes?

A faction with no interruptible acts cannot be played against. A grievance with
no substitute settlement becomes a scripted war. Political lore earns its place
when players can bargain with an institution or expose it. They may delay or
redirect its work, then choose whether to support or break something whose
consequences can be read in advance.

## The Political Condition after the Blood Mist

### Ten Generations of Narrow Reach

The Blood Mist did more than prevent travel. It shortened the distance at which
any promise could be tested. A ruler beyond the next settlement could neither
inspect a steward nor answer a raid. A marriage compact could not be renewed by
visits. A distant shrine could demand obedience but rarely send practical help.
Road rights became memories attached to unusable roads.

Power contracted around places that could still coordinate life:

- a hall with grain and armed dependents
- a shrine whose rites answered birth and burial
- a mill that several households needed
- a mine or forge with irreplaceable skill
- a defensible ruin with water inside its walls
- a family whose members could move when others could not

Some old offices survived in name because a local household kept performing
them. Others became private property. The bridge keeper's descendants still
claimed a bridge that had rotted away. A reeve became a village elder because
no higher collector arrived. A shrine-wife kept marriage witnesses and burial
names after the church above her vanished.

### Isolation Preserved Contradiction

Two neighboring settlements could preserve incompatible versions of the same
law for generations. Neither had the means to impose its memory on the other.
One remembered a meadow as common grazing. The other remembered it as a dowry
grant. One treated a river island as shrine ground. The other cut willow there
under a right no living person had witnessed.

The Blood Mist did not freeze law. Each settlement adapted inherited claims to
survival. What it froze was comparison. Contradictions could mature without a
shared court, market, pilgrimage, or seasonal assembly forcing them into the
same room.

Reopened roads now carry those contradictions toward one another.

### Authority Became Intimate

Mist-bound authority was close enough to smell at supper. People obeyed a
person who could enter the byre, inspect the bin, judge the quarrel, or bring
armed cousins before dark. Distance stripped command of credibility.

This intimacy does not imply kindness. It means cruelty had a known face.
Households learned the habits of collectors, priests, guards, and headwomen.
They knew which official accepted poor grain, which one counted children as
labor, and which one punished a lie more harshly than a short measure.

Political skill became the skill of managing known dangers rather than relying
upon impersonal law.

### Mobile Powers Became Unnatural Powers

Any group able to cross the Blood Mist gained authority beyond ordinary
measure. The Rust Brothers made mobility into proof of divine selection,
tribute right, and terror. Demons, certain monsters, spellcasters, and other
exceptional travelers could connect places that had lost ordinary roads.

Information gathered by mobile powers became difficult to challenge. A Rust
Brother could say that another village paid twice as much. No local witness
could travel to ask. A wandering sorcerer could claim recognition from a school
that no one present had seen. A monster could carry an old mark from beyond the
Mist and be treated as envoy, omen, or lie according to local need.

Mobility itself became a political asset.

### The Mist Lifted Five Years Ago

Five years can reopen a ford. Ten generations of inheritance take longer to
settle.

The first years after the Mist brought scouts, lost kin, traders, collectors,
claimants, pilgrims, refugees, and armed people calling old service due. Some
arrived with documents. Some carried songs or seals. Others brought the one
surviving grandparent who remembered a place before isolation.

Settlements had meanwhile buried generations, redistributed land, altered
rites, and built law around who remained. A returning claim can be historically
sound and socially ruinous at the same time.

### Reopening Creates Political Inflation

The road lets more people claim authority than the land can support.

One village may hear from:

- an old lord's heir demanding rent
- a Rust prior restoring tribute
- a Raven Sister disputing the prior's witness
- a merchant seeking toll exemption
- a dwarf clan recalling a stone right
- an orc band naming an older occupation
- a returned household asking for its field

Each may hold a fragment of truth. The village cannot satisfy every fragment
without giving away the same grain, labor, or road several times.

Post-Mist politics is therefore full of overlapping legitimacy. The strongest
claim is often the one that bundles proof with protection, marriage, useful
work, or armed presence.

### The First New Lords

Some new rulers are people who kept others alive. A miller fed three hamlets.
A healer coordinated quarantine. A hunter maintained warning paths. A
stronghold owner opened storage during a failed harvest. Their authority grew
from practical memory.

Others are people who arrived first with force. They occupied an empty ruin,
found an old seal, married a local heir, and began calling collection custom
before anyone could remember when it started.

Both kinds need institutions if their rule is to survive them. Gratitude fades.
Fear becomes expensive. A child who inherits only the title must rebuild the
acts that once made it credible.

### Political Time Runs at Several Speeds

Households think toward the next meal, birth, sowing, and winter. Villages think
through harvests, marriages, repairs, and remembered feuds. Human claimant
houses think in generations. Dwarf clans may carry an inspection, debt, or
grievance for centuries. Elves can wait through mortal claimants while the
ground itself changes around the dispute.

Negotiation across those scales creates advantage. A human may demand decision
before winter. An elf may consider that haste an attempt to manufacture truth.
A dwarf may accept the immediate settlement while recording a defect for the
next gathering at Stonegarden. An orc captain may regard delay as refusal when
the camp cannot remain on poor grazing.

The party who controls the deadline often controls the bargain.

### The Political Weather of 1165 AS

The Ravenlands in 1165 AS are neither restored nor empty. Roads carry enough
traffic to awaken claims. No power can yet make every road answer in the same
way. Strongholds become seats faster than their owners become rulers.

Every reopening produces a local question:

- Who keeps this crossing?
- Which measure is accepted here?
- Whose dead prove the boundary?
- Which armed hand answers theft?
- Who may witness an oath between strangers?
- What old payment returns with the road?

Those questions are the daily surface of faction play.

## The Household as a Political Body

### The Household Holds More Than Kin

A Ravenland household may contain a married pair, children, an unmarried
sibling, an elder, apprentices, fosterlings, servants, refugees, hired hands,
and someone whose exact relation is never explained to guests. Its members eat
from connected stores and answer for one another before outsiders.

Political demands land on the household because the household controls bodies,
tools, animals, and stored food. A faction rarely collects from an abstract
individual. It asks the house for one levy fighter, two repair days, a cart, or
a measure of grain.

The house then decides who pays.

### The Key Holder

Keys are political objects. The person who controls the grain chest, tool shed,
stable, shrine box, or document coffer decides which promise can be fulfilled
today. Public authority may belong to one household head while material veto
belongs to another person carrying iron at the belt.

A collector who negotiates only with the loudest adult may leave with an oath
and no sacks. Experienced officials watch who opens the door behind the hall,
who measures salt, and whose glance stops a servant from bringing another jug.

### Household Council

Most households deliberate without a formal assembly. Work creates the council.
People speak while mending nets, sorting grain, or preparing the evening meal.
The person whose work is at stake speaks with special weight. A herder judges
whether one more animal can be taken. A midwife judges whether a pregnant adult
can travel. An elder remembers what happened after the last refusal.

Final authority varies. One house follows age. Another follows ownership of the
land. A third follows whoever has kept the others fed since the old head became
ill. The custom outsiders are told may differ from the hand that decides.

### Liability Is Shared Unevenly

When one member commits an offense, outsiders may demand compensation from the
whole house. Shared liability makes concealment and discipline household work.
It also permits abuse. A lord can punish an absent fugitive through children,
animals, and stored grain that did not flee.

Households resist by narrowing responsibility. They may publicly disown the
offender, surrender a tool or animal as compensation, offer service, produce a
surety, or place the accused under shrine protection until a hearing.

Whether the disowning is real depends upon who still leaves food at the forest
edge.

### Household Reputation

A household's reputation is task-specific.

One family may be trusted to keep a hostage but suspected of short measures.
Another may fight reliably and fail every debt. A third keeps guest right even
with enemies yet cannot keep its sons from raiding after drink.

Factions use these distinctions. Stewards recruit collectors from families
known for exact measure, messengers from those with kin along a route, and
hostage keepers from houses that understand how to preserve a valuable life
without making it comfortable enough to forget the bargain.

### The Political Marriage Bed

Marriage can join stores, labor, inheritance, witness networks, and armed kin.
The couple matters. So do every adult whose work or expectation is displaced by
the union.

A marriage that brings a new claimant into a house may threaten an older
sibling's standing. A bride-price paid in animals can weaken winter survival.
A spouse from a distant settlement brings road knowledge and unfamiliar
obligations. A politically useful marriage may still fail because the joined
households cannot agree who holds the keys.

### Fosterlings and Wards

A fosterling learns another household's habits from inside. This can create
affection, divided loyalty, or both. Fosterage makes peace durable when the
child is treated as kin and allowed to carry honor home. It creates a hostage
under a gentler name when departure is forbidden.

The difference is visible in ordinary freedoms. May the child visit birth kin?
Who chooses apprenticeship? Whose dead receive the child's mourning? Can the
child leave after reaching adult standing?

### Household Fission

A household divides when stores, marriage, authority, or space no longer hold
its members together. Division is political because fields, tools, obligations,
and witnesses must be apportioned.

Faction officers prefer houses that remain legible. A divided household can
make one due into three disputes. Local rivals may encourage fission to weaken a
dominant family. A ruler may forbid division during levy or debt, effectively
binding adults to a house they no longer accept.

### Household Extinction

War, disease, monster attack, or failed birth can leave property without an
obvious living claimant. The village must decide whether land returns to common
use, passes to distant kin, follows foster relation, attaches to a shrine, or
is seized by the protecting power.

The fastest claimant often arrives with a cart.

Objects complicate extinction. A sword may belong to a lineage. A mill share
may belong to the household office rather than any person. A shrine key may not
be inherited outside the faith. An elf or dwarf may remember an older claim
that makes every human succession appear temporary.

### Household Political Signs

Show household politics through things players can notice:

- two tally sticks kept where one should suffice
- a married adult seated below an unmarried elder sibling
- a levy spear maintained better than the plough
- a guest cup offered by someone other than the named head
- one locked chest in an otherwise poor room
- foster children wearing another settlement's knot or bead
- a grain measure with two carved fill lines
- an empty place kept for an outlawed relative

Each sign suggests a question. None announces the answer.

## The Village as a Coalition of Powers

### No Village Has One Voice

The person who speaks at the gate represents the village only as long as enough
households accept the representation. A headwoman may control the warning horn
and not the mill. A priest may witness oaths and lack the authority to assign
grazing. A wealthy herder may lend animals while remaining distrusted in burial
matters.

Treat the village as a coalition among functions rather than a miniature
kingdom. The coalition holds because ordinary life requires cooperation across
water, grain, animals, defense, worship, and marriage.

### The Functional Powers

Most villages contain several of the following powers even when only one has a
formal title.

**The water keeper** controls access, maintenance, or knowledge of the well,
spring, ferry, or irrigation ditch.

**The grain keeper** controls storage, milling, seed separation, or the measures
used for dues.

**The defense voice** calls watch, keeps weapons, assigns wall work, or knows
which households can answer quickly.

**The burial witness** remembers names, graves, walkers, compensation, and who
may be buried within the boundary.

**The shrine keeper** controls rites, offerings, sanctuary, and religious
recognition.

**The road voice** deals with traders, messengers, tolls, and outsiders.

**The marriage memory** knows kin relations, fosterage, prohibited unions, and
which household still owes a promised match.

One person may hold several roles. That concentration is a political fact, not
mere efficiency.

### The Elder Circle

An elder circle is usually a meeting of people who control necessary memory or
resources. Age helps because memory matters. Age without a household, craft,
following, or credible witness may earn courtesy rather than decision.

The circle rarely votes in a modern sense. It seeks a result that important
households will perform. Silence can indicate consent, fear, or a plan to ignore
the judgment. A wise presider asks each functional power what the decision will
cost before declaring agreement.

### The Reeve

A reeve is the point where outside demand enters the village. The reeve counts,
summons, transmits judgment, and becomes blame's nearest body.

During the Mist, many reeves ceased answering any superior and became local
rulers. Others remained servants of a remembered office. After reopening, a
distant claimant may try to restore the old chain by recognizing, replacing, or
punishing the reeve.

Replacing one without controlling the measures, messengers, and household
coalition usually produces two reeves.

### The Miller

The miller knows yield before the collector does. Grain passes under the
miller's hand in a form that can be compared across households. The mill share
also turns private harvest into public suspicion.

A faction that controls the mill can collect efficiently. A village that
controls its mill can hide shortage, delay dues, or expose a steward's false
tally. Burning the mill punishes both sides and feeds neither.

### The Smith

The smith repairs plough iron and weapons in the same fire. This gives the forge
political information. A rush of spear repairs reveals a hidden muster. Missing
nails delay a palisade. Refusal to shoe a collector's horse can be explained as
fuel shortage until the third refusal.

Smiths are difficult to coerce beyond a point. Killing one may win an afternoon
and lose the next year.

### The Priest and Shrine Keeper

Religious authority gives acts public weight. A priest can make an oath harder
to deny, a burial legitimate, a marriage recognized, or an outlawry fearful.
The office may also control offerings, guest shelter, healing, literacy, or
contact with a wider order.

No priest stands outside village politics. Refusing to witness is an act.
Choosing the order of names in prayer is an act. Declaring a granary measure
unclean is an act with hungry consequences.

### The Midwife and Healer

The healer knows bodies, births, hidden injuries, and which households are
closer to failure than they appear. Such knowledge creates influence without a
formal bench.

A midwife can establish or complicate descent. A healer can reveal that an
accused servant was beaten before a confession. Both depend upon trust across
households. A faction that compels their testimony may gain one judgment and
lose years of honest access.

### The Ferryman and Gatekeeper

The ferryman decides when a distant claim becomes physically present. The
gatekeeper decides whether a stranger stands inside the settlement's ordinary
protection. Both offices turn routine movement into political selection.

Their refusals are often framed as weather, darkness, damage, or procedure. An
experienced ruler investigates repeated inconvenience before calling it open
rebellion.

### The Wealthy Household

Wealth in animals, seed, silver, tools, or stored food lets one household absorb
risks others cannot. It can feed a watch, lend a plough team, ransom a captive,
or survive a collector's anger. Those acts create dependence.

The wealthy house may seek formal office. It may prefer influence without
responsibility. A village often resents the household it cannot survive without.

### The Poor Coalition

Several poor households can control labor, local knowledge, and collective
refusal. Their power appears when work must be done at once. A harvest cannot be
ordered into a barn by title alone.

Poor coalitions are difficult to maintain because each member can be bought,
threatened, or relieved separately. Shared meals, marriage, burial groups, and
mutual debt make them harder to divide.

### Outsiders within the Village

Refugees, Misgrown, resident merchants, itinerant craftspeople, freed captives,
and spouses from another kin may live inside a settlement without full standing.
They contribute while lacking recognized ancestry, graves, or witnesses.

An outside faction can recruit them as informants. Local households can use
them as intermediaries, scapegoats, or bridges. Full inclusion becomes visible
when outsiders may bury their dead, marry without special surety, and speak in a
judgment concerning established houses.

### Village Coalition Signs

The coalition can be read through placement and delay:

- the gate speaker waits for the miller before naming a grain due
- the priest sits near the hearth but away from the key chest
- three households repair one roof before touching the palisade
- the ferryman receives ale while the reeve receives formal greeting
- the smith's child is fostered in the wealthiest house
- a healer enters every home except one
- two burial paths reach the same graveyard through separate gates

Politics is already moving before anyone calls a council.

## The Eight Grounds of Rule

Book 02 names blood, custom, protection, conquest, wealth, cult sanction, oath,
and dread as common bases of rule. Most factions use several. One ground usually
answers the first question asked by a stranger: why do these people obey before
weapons are drawn?

Each ground produces a different obligation. Each fails in a different way.

### Blood

Blood rule claims that descent carries a right or duty that another person
cannot acquire through competence alone. The claim may pass through father,
mother, sibling line, clan branch, elven ruby, adopted descent, or another
recognized continuity.

Blood is useful because people can remember it without a standing office.
Genealogy travels in names, marriage prohibitions, scars, inherited objects,
and burial position. A child can carry a claim while unable to exercise it.
That permits regency, fosterage, hostage politics, and murder disguised as care.

#### What Blood Rule Must Maintain

Blood rule needs recognized descent. Someone must remember births, marriages,
adoptions, repudiations, and deaths. Midwives, priests, genealogy keepers,
elders, and foster houses become political officers whether or not they bear a
title.

It also needs a doctrine of defect. What happens when the heir is absent,
Misgrown, infertile, oath-broken, possessed, of mixed kin, or born under a
disputed rite? A lineage that has no answer will invent one during the crisis,
usually under armed pressure.

#### The Public Proof of Blood

Proof may include:

- a living witness to the birth or naming
- a hereditary object recognized by retainers
- resemblance treated as evidence rather than certainty
- a marriage compact kept by another house
- a grave sequence or household genealogy
- magical examination, with all the fear that creates
- acknowledgment by a rival whose own claim depends on the same rule

The strongest proof is often mutual dependence. Two enemy houses may agree that
one child belongs to a line because denying it would also dissolve their own
marriages and inheritances.

#### The Price of Blood Rule

People become vessels for continuity. Their marriages, fertility, bodies, and
children attract public interest. A capable younger sibling can be trapped
beneath an unfit elder. A widow can be forced to preserve a claim through a new
union. A child may be safer as a hostage than at home.

Blood rule also creates pretenders who cannot safely become ordinary people.
Even a reluctant claimant threatens whoever sits in the hall.

#### How Blood Rule Fails

Blood rule fails through disputed descent, extinction, rival branches,
unrecognized adoption, hidden birth, or the simple refusal of armed dependents
to treat inheritance as command.

The failure is visible when retainers ask to see the heir before taking an oath,
when a shrine delays the naming rite, or when two households maintain separate
grave lists for the same lineage.

### Custom

Custom says that a practice is binding because known people performed it under
known conditions and the settlement remembers the result. It is stronger than
habit. Custom includes a claim about what should happen when the pattern
returns.

A due paid every Harvest Day may become customary. So may common grazing,
rotation of watch, selection of a headwoman, or the right of one family to open
the spring moot.

#### Custom Needs Repetition and Witness

One payment is a bargain. Repeated payment under public memory can become due.
One act of mercy is personal. Repeated remission during crop failure can become
a ruler's obligation.

Custom therefore grows at the edge of ambiguity. Powerful people describe
exceptional concessions as gifts. Dependents describe them as precedent. Each
side knows that the word chosen this year may govern the next famine.

#### Local Custom after Isolation

Blood Mist settlements preserved customs without external comparison. A
practice unknown in the next valley may be treated as the oldest law in the
world. Reopening exposes local certainty to rival certainty.

Do not decide such disputes by asking which practice looks more historical.
Ask who relies upon it now. A grazing rotation may be invented within living
memory and still keep twelve poor households alive.

#### The Price of Custom Rule

Custom protects people from sudden invention by rulers. It also protects old
inequalities. A household may perform degrading service because everyone agrees
its ancestor once accepted the duty. A widow may be denied a voice by a rule
whose first purpose no one remembers.

Reform requires a reason custom recognizes: catastrophe, compensation, sacred
judgment, new oath, or a publicly acknowledged failure of the old pattern.

#### How Custom Rule Fails

Custom fails when repetition stops, witnesses die, conditions change, or two
memories meet. It also fails when rulers invoke custom only for extraction and
ignore the customary duties owed in return.

The sign is selective memory. The collector remembers the grain due and forgets
the escort once promised to the same carts.

### Protection

Protection rule rests upon a repeated exchange. A stronger hand answers threats
that households cannot answer alone. In return, the protected give food,
service, warning, shelter, passage, or obedience.

Protection is among the most credible grounds of post-Mist rule because its
proof is immediate. The road is safe. The stolen animals return. The monster is
kept beyond the marked stones. The feud is prevented from reaching the fields.

#### Protection Must Arrive

A protector does not need to prevent every harm. The protector must answer in a
way people can see. An unsuccessful pursuit can preserve authority if it was
swift, costly, and honest. A victory arriving after the barns burn may still
look like failure.

Reach matters more than grandeur. Ten riders who know the crossing may protect
better than a hundred soldiers behind a distant wall.

#### The Protected Retain Agency

A protected settlement usually keeps local authority. It pays upward because a
larger danger requires coordination. The protector who begins appointing every
elder and taking every dispute changes protection into rule or occupation.

That change may be accepted. It should never be invisible.

#### The Price of Protection Rule

Protection can manufacture the danger it answers. A lord may provoke raids,
refuse local peace, or exaggerate monster signs to keep payment credible. Armed
protectors quartered for too long become a threat to stores, daughters, and
local judgment.

Protection also creates dependency. A village may abandon its own watch and
find itself helpless when the protector turns elsewhere.

#### How Protection Rule Fails

Failure occurs when warning goes unanswered, compensation is refused, the
protector favors one settlement openly, or the cost exceeds the danger.

One public failure often matters more than several quiet successes. People pay
protection so that a particular bad night does not happen. After it happens,
the old payments resemble tribute.

### Conquest

Conquest says that possession gained by force has become a right. Its first
proof is simple: the conqueror holds the gate. Its later survival depends upon
turning seizure into another ground of rule.

A conquering faction may marry local descent, confirm custom, provide
protection, restore a shrine, or distribute captured land. Without such work it
must keep conquering the same place each week.

#### The First Settlement after Conquest

The terms immediately following surrender or breach shape later politics.

Questions include:

- Are weapons surrendered or registered?
- Which leaders remain in office?
- Who keeps the granary keys?
- Are hostages selected from rulers or households?
- Which dead may be buried inside the wall?
- Do soldiers quarter in homes or a separate camp?
- Are previous dues preserved, doubled, or suspended?

These choices tell residents whether the conqueror seeks rule, tribute, or
exhaustion.

#### The Conqueror's Dependence

Conquerors need local guides, bakers, healers, carters, translators, gravediggers,
and people who know which stream floods. This dependence creates the first
space for negotiation and resistance.

Killing every local officeholder leaves the conqueror with keys that open
nothing useful.

#### The Price of Conquest Rule

Conquest produces dispossession. Land grants to followers create new households
and turn old ones into tenants, servants, outlaws, or refugees. Confiscated
shrines and graves keep the loss present after material recovery.

The conqueror also becomes responsible for the conquered. Famine, monster
attack, or raid now tests the new rule. A victory can enlarge obligations faster
than stores.

#### How Conquest Rule Fails

Conquest fails when the garrison is unpaid, the conqueror cannot collect without
escort, local collaborators lose protection, or another power gives resistance
a plausible future.

The visible sign is a gate that opens for soldiers and closes behind them before
the officer has finished speaking.

### Wealth

Wealth rule coordinates through control of scarce, portable, or necessary
resources. Old silver, grain, animals, iron, salt, boats, mills, wagons, and
credit can bind people who reject a ruler's ancestry.

Wealth becomes political where it can survive one bad season and move before a
rival can answer.

#### Wealth Must Circulate

Stored wealth hidden forever creates security, not rule. Political wealth pays
guards, ransoms captives, repairs bridges, feeds guests, finances marriage,
buys warning, and sustains people whose loyalty would otherwise return to their
own fields.

A wealthy ruler is judged by timing. Grain issued after seed is eaten buys less
obedience than grain issued before the household opens the seed bin.

#### Debt as a Bond

Debt records an unequal exchange across time. It may be held in tally sticks,
witness memory, pledged objects, fostered children, labor, or a share of future
yield.

Debt can integrate strangers because it creates a recognized relation. It can
also consume them. A debtor who cannot clear interest or obligation becomes a
dependent whose work is permanently priced by someone else.

#### The Price of Wealth Rule

Everything acquires a measure. Hospitality becomes calculation. Marriage
becomes consolidation. Relief becomes a future claim. People who cannot be made
profitable may be abandoned.

Wealth rule is especially vulnerable to the appearance that silver has replaced
customary obligation. A lord who hires outsiders while local retainers go
unrewarded announces that loyalty is worth less than cash.

#### How Wealth Rule Fails

It fails when routes close, stores rot, measures lose trust, debtors coordinate,
or hired force discovers that the chest is lighter than the promise.

The first sign is usually delay. Wages arrive in mixed coin. Grain is offered in
place of silver. Creditors begin asking for hostages rather than tallies.

### Cult Sanction

Cult sanction makes obedience part of the world's sacred order. A priesthood or
religious institution witnesses rule, declares duties, interprets disaster, and
names refusal as fault rather than disagreement.

This ground is powerful because it reaches private conduct. A garrison sees the
gate. A faith may see the marriage bed, burial, oath, dream, crop failure, and
the body's marks.

#### Sanction Must Be Performed

The sacred claim lives through rites:

- anointing or crowning
- public oath witness
- naming a ruler in regular prayer
- receiving tribute as offering
- blessing levy or banner
- judging an offense against divine law
- controlling burial and memorial

If rites stop, sanction becomes memory. If rites continue under coercion, their
meaning becomes contested rather than absent.

#### Religious Office and Secular Need

Priests need food, shelter, roads, attendants, and protection. Rulers need
witness, literacy, sanctuary, healing, and a language that makes sacrifice
appear ordered. The relation is reciprocal even where one side claims supremacy.

A temple can withdraw sanction. A ruler can seize temple stores. Both acts
damage the system that gave their conflict public meaning.

#### The Price of Cult Sanction

Political opposition becomes heresy. Misfortune becomes evidence against the
disobedient. Private doubt risks household punishment. Religious minorities may
pay twice, once to the ruler and again for tolerated rites.

Cult sanction also burdens sincere priests. They may be required to bless a
levy, validate a dubious heir, or interpret defeat without destroying the faith
that feeds their dependents.

#### How Cult Sanction Fails

It fails through contradictory omens, corrupt priests, rival doctrine, public
ritual failure, desecration unanswered, or a sanctioned ruler who violates the
faith's recognizable duties.

The sign is a rite performed correctly while the congregation withholds the
response.

### Oath

Oath rule binds distinct powers without pretending they have become one
household. It is suited to clan confederacies, war bands, alliances, vassalage,
merchant leagues, and stronghold coalitions.

An oath specifies acts. Loyalty without acts cannot be tested. Good oaths name
who answers, what is given, where service ends, who judges breach, and which
conditions release the parties.

#### Oath Needs Witness and Memory

Witnesses make denial expensive. They need enough independence to be believed
and enough connection to care. A dependent servant may have seen everything
and still carry little public weight. A rival priest may be believed precisely
because the judgment harms an ally.

Objects help memory. Rings and cut sticks give an oath a body that can be shown
later. Cords or feathers travel more easily; stones remain in place. Shared cups
and marked weapons carry the witness of use.

#### Confederated Rule

Oath-bound factions preserve separate leaders. Common action requires council,
messenger work, agreed command, and repeated distribution of credit and spoil.
Their strength is resilience. One branch may fail without ending the whole.

Their weakness is timing. A direct lordship can issue one order while a
confederacy is still deciding whether the request falls within the oath.

#### The Price of Oath Rule

Ambiguous words become future weapons. People may serve a destructive promise
because public identity rests upon keeping it. Dependents who never swore can
still pay the oath's cost.

Oath culture also rewards careful liars. A person can keep the exact words while
destroying the expected bargain.

#### How Oath Rule Fails

It fails when terms become impossible, witnesses lose standing, one member
receives unequal reward, or the common danger disappears.

The sign is literal obedience. A vassal sends the required number of fighters,
all old, injured, or badly armed.

### Dread

Dread rule makes fear itself a means of carrying orders. It relies upon visible
punishment, uncertain surveillance, cultivated reputation, supernatural threat,
or the belief that resistance will harm more than the resistor.

Dread can reach farther than a patrol because people enforce it upon one
another. A parent silences a child before any collector hears. An elder reports
a hidden book because the whole settlement may be questioned.

#### Dread Requires Selective Proof

Indiscriminate destruction cannot collect, recruit, or govern. Effective dread
punishes enough people to preserve uncertainty while leaving enough survivors
to carry the lesson.

The Rust Brothers understand this distinction. Their power is cruel because it
is organized, not because it lacks restraint.

#### The Economy of Anticipation

People under dread prepare before demands arrive. Tribute is measured early.
Questionable objects disappear. Misgrown children are hidden or surrendered.
Road news is controlled. Compliance consumes labor even when no officer visits.

This makes dread appear efficient from the seat. The hidden cost accumulates in
flight, false information, damaged trust, and the loss of initiative among
people afraid to act without permission.

#### The Price of Dread Rule

Dread converts neighbors into potential witnesses against one another. It
rewards cruelty that produces visible submission. Officials conceal failure
because bad news threatens the messenger. Rulers receive orderly reports from
places already preparing to revolt.

Children learn the fear before they understand the doctrine.

#### How Dread Rule Fails

It fails when punishment becomes predictable, agents are defeated publicly,
safe refuge appears, surveillance is exposed as bluff, or people decide that
compliance and resistance now carry the same risk.

The first open laugh at a feared symbol can matter more than a dead patrol.

### Mixed Grounds of Rule

Most durable factions combine grounds.

A claimant house uses blood to name the ruler, custom to fix dues, protection to
justify service, and cult sanction to witness succession. An orc confederacy may
use oath between clans, conquest toward enemies, and gifts within the war host.
A merchant league uses wealth, charter custom, and protection of roads.

Mixed rule provides redundancy. It also creates internal contradiction.

A blood heir may lack cult sanction. A protector may be too poor to answer. A
conqueror may confirm custom that limits the spoils promised to followers. A
dread ruler may need honest information that dread has made impossible.

### Diagnosing a Faction's Ground

Ask what happens when the ruler gives an unpopular order.

- **Blood:** retainers obey because the speaker is the recognized heir.
- **Custom:** elders search memory for whether the order is permitted.
- **Protection:** settlements ask what danger the order answers.
- **Conquest:** the garrison prepares to enforce it.
- **Wealth:** officers ask who is paid and from which chest.
- **Cult sanction:** priests decide whether obedience is holy.
- **Oath:** bound parties examine the exact promise.
- **Dread:** households comply before learning the reason.

The answer identifies the ground currently carrying the weight, which may
differ from the faction's preferred story about itself.

## The Seat and Its Working Household

### The Seat Is a Place of Conversion

A seat converts local resources into distant acts. Grain becomes a patrol.
Silver becomes a marriage gift. A hostage becomes delay. News becomes a closed
road. A shrine rite becomes levy obedience.

The conversion requires workers. A hall without them is a large room containing
one important person and several unsolved problems.

### Seat Forms

A seat may be:

- a timber hall within a village
- a castle or fortress stronghold
- a temple and its storehouses
- a market court beside a crossing
- an inhabited section of a larger ruin
- a dwarf clan hall or workshop complex
- an orc camp whose center moves with the leading household
- an elven meeting place recognized through memory rather than walls
- a hidden network of houses with no public center

Form changes vulnerability. A fixed seat can store and fortify. A moving seat
can evade siege but struggles to keep records, prisoners, and heavy stores.
A hidden seat protects leaders while making public authority difficult.

### The Ruler's Household

The ruler's household includes more than family. It contains the people who
make the ruler's day possible: steward, cook, groom, guard captain, priest,
messenger, healer, scribe, hostage keeper, fosterlings, servants, and visiting
dependents.

Their arrangement is the faction in small form. Who eats near the ruler? Who
may wake them? Who holds keys after dark? Who can countermand a guard? Who hears
petitions before they reach the chair?

Change those relations and the faction changes before its sheet does.

### The Steward

The steward translates promises into stores and labor. This office knows what
the seat can actually do. A ruler may promise three patrols. The steward knows
there are horses for one and food for two.

Stewards gain enemies by making scarcity visible. They are accused of hoarding
when they preserve seed, cruelty when they collect, and treason when they tell a
ruler that the order cannot be supplied.

A corrupt steward can weaken a faction without stealing much. Favoring one
route, delaying one household's payment, or reporting every shortage as
disloyalty will turn administration into feud.

### The Keeper of Seals and Records

Where writing is used, the keeper controls continuity between spoken command
and distant reception. Seals, witness lists, tallies, copied charters, and old
judgments let a seat act after memory becomes disputed.

Records do not remove politics. They move it into access, interpretation, and
authenticity. Who may inspect the original? Who can read the old hand? Which
copy was made before the fire? Why does fresh wax sit on a weathered cord?

The keeper's greatest power may be knowing which document nobody can safely
produce.

### The Guard Captain

The guard captain decides how command becomes controlled violence. This office
assigns gate watch, escorts collectors, separates petitioners, protects
hostages, and determines which insult reaches the ruler as a threat.

A loyal captain can still become politically dangerous. Guards owe daily
obedience to the person who feeds, drills, and posts them. If the ruler and
captain divide, the gate often chooses first.

### The Hostage Keeper

Hostages require food, clothing, health, recognizable identity, controlled
contact, and protection from private vengeance. The keeper preserves their
political value.

The office demands a difficult balance. Excess cruelty may provoke rescue or
make the hostage useless. Excess intimacy may divide loyalty. Neglect can kill
the bargain without anyone choosing to break it.

Hostage keepers learn family secrets through illness, letters, visits, and the
unguarded speech of children.

### The Hall Priest

The priest witnesses, interprets, buries, blesses, and refuses. Close service to
a ruler gives access while exposing the faith to every political failure of the
seat.

The hall priest may become advocate for petitioners, keeper of sanctuary, or
religious mask for extraction. Rival clergy will judge the same acts according
to doctrine and interest.

### The Groom and Stable Office

Horses reveal political movement. The groom knows which mount was ridden at
night, which messenger arrived exhausted, which saddle carries foreign mud, and
whether the promised remounts exist.

Rulers overlook grooms because the work smells of dung. Spies do not.

### The Kitchen and Cup

The kitchen controls hospitality in material form. Portion size, order of
service, quality of ale, and access to the inner fire place guests within the
seat's hierarchy.

Cooks and servers hear captains after formal speech ends. They also know whether
the seat is prosperous or disguising shortage. Thin stew in polished bowls
deceives only visitors who do not watch the servants eat.

Poison fear gives kitchen workers dangerous intimacy. A ruler who humiliates
them must then choose between hunger and trust.

### The Messenger Office

A seat with regular reach needs spare mounts, route knowledge, safe houses,
recognition tokens, and people authorized to make decisions when exact orders
become impossible.

Messengers are selected for memory and judgment as much as speed. A spoken
message may need to survive capture because nothing written can be risked.

The office becomes a political network. Messengers compare settlements,
identify cold gates, and know which claimed roads exist only on maps.

### Petitioners and Waiting Space

Where people wait reveals access. A petitioner kept outside in rain receives an
answer before the hearing. A rival invited to eat has gained temporary standing.
Separate entrances allow a seat to conceal who seeks judgment.

The person who orders the waiting line possesses quiet patronage. They can place
a widow before a merchant, delay an angry captain until sober, or ensure that
two enemies meet in the same narrow passage.

### The Seat at Dawn

Politics begins before council.

The kitchen counts mouths. Guards report night arrivals. Grooms inspect horses.
The steward checks what left storage. Messengers wait for seals. Petitioners
gather near the gate. The priest learns who died.

By the time the ruler sits, the household has already decided which problems
can be presented together and which must be kept apart.

### The Seat at Night

Night exposes dependence. Doors close. Keys move to sleeping bodies. Hostages
are counted. Servants carry private cups. Guards decide which late visitor is
worth waking the hall.

A seat that fears its own night watch has already divided into factions.

### Seat Failure

Seats fail through ordinary interruption:

- the steward's tally is no longer believed
- the guard captain cannot pay or feed the watch
- hostages become sick or disappear
- messengers refuse one route
- the priest withholds public witness
- the kitchen cannot feed guests at the expected rank
- records contradict the ruler's memory
- servants begin carrying news outward before orders leave

The walls can remain sound throughout.

## Witness, Record, and Political Memory

### Witness Makes an Act Public

A political witness does more than observe. The witness carries an event beyond
the room and gives others reason to act as though it occurred. An oath with no
credible witness may bind conscience. It will struggle to bind a village.

Witness weight comes from position. A priest carries ritual standing. A midwife
carries bodily knowledge. A rival carries the credibility of someone who had
reason to deny the act. A child may remember accurately and still lack the
social strength to make adults admit it.

### The Witness Circle

Serious acts often use several witnesses chosen for different constituencies.
A marriage may be seen by birth kin, local elder, priest, and representative of
the protecting power. Their agreement makes later denial costly across several
networks.

The witness circle also limits fabrication. Bribing one voice is easier than
aligning four people whose interests differ.

Too many witnesses create another danger. Each leaves with a version.

### Interested Witnesses

Every witness has an interest. Neutrality is less important than declared
position. People trust a witness whose likely bias is known and whose statement
runs against that bias.

A retainer admitting that the ruler promised remission may carry great weight.
A hostile elder confirming the heir's birth may settle a line. A paid scribe is
useful when everyone knows who paid and can compare the record with living
memory.

Hidden interest poisons testimony after discovery.

### Bodily Witness

Bodies carry evidence through scars, pregnancy, kin resemblance, wounds,
tattoos, brands, disease, magical alteration, and the effects of confinement.
Healers, midwives, torturers, and experienced fighters interpret those signs.

Bodily evidence is never self-explanatory. A scar proves injury, not lawful
cause. A resemblance suggests descent, not consent. A mark called demonic may
be birth, disease, craft injury, or something that knows how accusation works.

The authority to interpret bodies is therefore political.

### Objects as Witnesses

Objects outlive voices. Seal rings, tally sticks, oath cords, inherited blades,
marriage gifts, boundary stones, keys, grave markers, and repaired tools can
prove that a relation once existed.

Objects can also lie. A ring can be stolen. A tally can be recut. A boundary
stone can move at night. The repair style on a bridge may reveal that the family
claiming hereditary care has not touched it in twenty years.

Material examination gives adventurers a route into political truth without
requiring a confession.

### Written Records

Writing fixes words while loosening them from the people who spoke. This is both
its strength and danger. A charter can cross generations. It can also be read by
someone who has never met the conditions under which it was granted.

Ravenland records are uneven. Some seats maintain copied judgments and due
lists. Some villages rely on marks no outsider understands. Dwarf annals may
preserve defects humans considered settled. Elven memory can challenge written
dates through living testimony.

No central archive settles every dispute.

### Tallies and Measures

Tallies answer narrow questions well. How much grain was delivered? How many
labor days remain? Which household supplied a cart? They answer motive and
justice poorly.

Split tallies let two parties hold matching halves. Marks on beams, bins, tool
handles, and doorposts make accounting visible to people who cannot read a
book. Public measures reduce dispute only when the measuring vessel itself is
trusted.

An official who changes the measure has changed the law without issuing a
decree.

### Oral Formula

Repeated phrases preserve obligations. An oath, boundary recital, inheritance
sequence, or sanctuary formula may be spoken in words older than the speaker's
ordinary language.

Formula limits improvisation. It also creates expert interpreters. The elder
who knows where one pause belongs can distinguish a remembered oath from a
clever reconstruction.

Players listening carefully should be able to notice missing names, changed
order, or a response that no one gives.

### Song and Annal

Songs preserve who deserves memory. They compress, select, and assign blame.
An annal records who attended, what was measured, and which judgment was
entered. Neither is free from politics.

Songs can carry a defeated claim through generations. Annals can omit people
who refused the recorder's authority. The conflict between them is useful. One
may preserve motive while the other preserves date.

### Graves as Records

Burial order shows descent, alliance, exclusion, and changing authority. A body
placed inside a family boundary can establish belonging after death. An empty
grave may preserve the claim of someone missing. A marker turned face down can
signal judgment without disturbing dangerous remains.

Exhumation is therefore political and religious work. Moving bones can alter
the evidence on which land, inheritance, or grievance rests.

Walkers make careless revision physically dangerous.

### Memory Keepers

Every settlement relies upon people who connect present acts to old
arrangements. They may be elders, priests, singers, scribes, midwives, stone
singers, foster parents, or the servant who has poured at the same table for
forty years.

Memory keepers are vulnerable to pressure. Killing one destroys evidence but
also announces fear of what they knew. Bribing one may be quieter. Marrying
their household may be quieter still.

### Contradictory Archives

When records conflict, preserve the conflict until a decision is required.

Ask:

- Who created each account?
- What did that person need the account to accomplish?
- Which material facts remain testable?
- Who has lived according to each version?
- What present arrangement would each interpretation destroy?

The oldest account need not be the fairest. The newest need not be false.

### Manufactured Memory

New rulers often create ceremonies that imply age. They restore a ruined seat,
invent an annual procession, place ancestors in an existing graveyard, or
rename a due with an older word.

Such invention can become real custom if people perform it long enough. Its
young age remains leverage for rivals who remember the first performance.

### The Politics of Forgetting

Communities sometimes choose not to preserve a fact. A child born during
occupation receives a simpler descent. A collaborator's service is omitted
after liberation. A monster payment is called a seasonal offering once the
original bargain becomes shameful.

Forgetting can protect people. It can also bury a load-bearing truth until the
old danger returns and no one remembers what was promised.

## Oath, Gift, Due, and Tribute

### Four Different Transfers

The same sack of grain can carry four political meanings.

**Gift** creates or acknowledges a relation without claiming exact return.

**Due** fulfills an obligation recognized by custom, oath, tenure, or office.

**Tribute** is paid because acknowledged superiority or threatened harm makes
refusal dearer.

**Plunder** is taken without maintaining a reciprocal political claim beyond
the taker's strength.

Confusing these meanings creates conflict. A ruler calls tribute due to make it
lawful. A village calls due a gift to preserve the right to stop. A raider calls
plunder tribute because returning next year sounds more dignified than theft.

### The Gift

A good political gift is specific to the receiver. Grain helps a hungry seat.
A breeding animal repairs a herd. A copied book acknowledges a learned order.
A weapon can honor a captain while making future service visible.

Gifts need witnesses when they carry public meaning. Private gifts create
intimacy, suspicion, or corruption according to who learns of them.

The receiver gains an obligation to answer, but the form and timing remain
negotiable. That uncertainty is the gift's political strength.

### Refusing a Gift

Refusal prevents the relation from being created or deepened. It may also insult
the giver by declaring the offered thing unclean, insufficient, dangerous, or
presumptuous.

A careful refusal names a reason that preserves future exchange. The animal is
accepted for the shrine rather than the ruler. The cup is touched but not
drunk. The silver is returned with food of equal value.

Keeping the gift while denying obligation is a bolder act.

### The Due

A due should answer five questions:

1. Who owes it?
2. To whom is it owed?
3. What amount or service satisfies it?
4. When and where is it delivered?
5. What reciprocal duty justifies it?

If any answer remains vague, someone benefits from the vagueness. A customary
labor day can become three days when no one remembers whether travel counted.

### Paying in Kind

Most dues are paid through goods or service because those are what factions
need. Grain feeds retainers. Hay feeds horses. Labor repairs roads. Ferry duty
moves messengers. Shelter sustains patrols.

Payment in kind transfers quality disputes into politics. Wet grain, old hay,
weak animals, and unwilling labor may satisfy the stated quantity while
violating expectation.

Exact collectors learn to inspect. Cruel collectors reject anything they can
reject profitably.

### Commutation into Silver

Silver lets a household replace difficult service with portable value. It also
lets the receiving power buy elsewhere. Commutation becomes attractive where
roads and markets work.

It disadvantages households with adequate labor and little coin. A due once
shared through work can become a cash demand that only traders and wealthy
houses meet easily.

Old Alder War silver carries prestige because its recognition reaches farther
than local measures. That prestige invites clipping, testing, and dispute.

### Arrears

An unpaid due can become debt, grievance, or proof of rebellion. The category is
a political choice.

One collector grants delay and records arrears. Another seizes an animal. A
third accepts a child into service as surety. Each response changes the relation
between household and faction.

Arrears accumulated beyond plausible payment are usually a path to dependency,
confiscation, flight, or revolt. They are rarely a recoverable account.

### Remission

Remitting a due leaves resources with the payer. It also spends the ruler's
Hearth or Treasury elsewhere. Wise remission is public enough to create
gratitude and narrow enough to preserve ordinary expectation.

Remission can be patronage. Favored households receive mercy while rivals are
collected to the last measure. It can also be repair after disaster.

The words matter. A temporary remission preserves the due. A forgiven due may
create precedent.

### Tribute

Tribute begins where refusal is answered by punishment rather than ordinary
judgment. It may still be regular, measured, and ceremonial. Organization makes
tribute more effective, not less coercive.

Tributary settlements often retain local authority because the collecting power
wants payment rather than administration. This leaves room for internal life
and concealed resistance.

### The Tribute Visit

A tribute party announces itself through riders, smoke, bells, drums, religious
signs, or a messenger sent ahead. Warning gives the settlement time to measure,
hide, borrow, and decide who will speak.

Collection commonly includes:

- presentation of the demand
- inspection of local authority
- weighing or counting
- hearing excuses and accusations
- selecting surety for arrears
- public confirmation of submission
- departure under guard

Each stage gives players a different point of intervention.

### Tribute as Information

The collector learns population, stores, illness, new buildings, missing
households, weapons, road news, and local divisions. Tribute routes are
intelligence routes.

Settlements therefore stage appearances. Healthy animals are hidden. Empty bins
are displayed. A sick elder remains at the head of the table to conceal
succession. Children are sent to distant kin before household counts.

### Competitive Tribute

Two factions may demand payment from the same settlement. Paying both can
consume survival. Paying one declares preference. Paying neither requires
defense or escape.

Settlements answer through divided payments, false measures, delayed delivery,
secret protection bargains, or public submission to the power most likely to
hear of it.

Competitive tribute is one of the fastest routes from reopening trade to
faction war.

### Plunder Disguised as Collection

Collectors cross into plunder when they seize beyond recognized demand, take
goods that destroy future payment, or refuse every form of satisfaction.

The distinction is visible to the settlement before it is visible to the seat.
A steward may record increased revenue while the village has already stopped
considering itself bound.

### Who Carries the Payment

The carrier bears risk and political meaning. Sending the reeve shows ordinary
compliance. Sending children may plead weakness. Sending armed household heads
may demonstrate respect or threat. Sending outsiders preserves deniability.

A payment that fails on the road creates a three-sided dispute among payer,
carrier, and receiver. Banditry is sometimes the least interesting explanation.

### The Measure at the Table

Always place the measure physically. Who brought it? Who filled it? Who leveled
the top? Who watched? Who keeps it afterward?

Changing any one of those people can shift the meaning of the entire collection.

## Protection and the Price of Failure

### Protection Is a Schedule

Protection becomes credible through repeated acts:

- patrols pass when expected
- warning signals receive an answer
- roads are cleared after attack
- captives are pursued or ransomed
- disputes between protected settlements are judged
- monsters are watched, fed, diverted, or hunted according to local arrangement

The schedule need not be written. People know when the riders are late.

### Warning Systems

Horns, bells, fires, pigeons, runners, dogs, marked branches, and agreed songs
carry warning. Each system has a range, false alarms, and people responsible for
maintaining it.

Political control of warning is serious. A faction can ignore one household's
signal. A village can delay a horn until raiders have passed toward a hated
neighbor. A watch can manufacture danger to justify more food.

### Patrols

Patrols gather information and display care. They also consume hospitality,
fodder, and patience. A route chosen for tactical value may burden the same
houses every week.

Residents judge patrols by conduct. Do they repair what they break? Do they pay
for food? Do they carry messages for ordinary people? Do they harass anyone who
cannot complain at the seat?

The answers become the protector's local reputation.

### Pursuit and Recovery

Recovering stolen animals, captives, or goods proves that protection reaches
beyond the wall. Complete recovery is rare. Visible effort and fair division of
what returns may preserve the bond.

A protector who recovers only a wealthy household's cattle teaches every poor
house what its due purchases.

### Arbitration

Protected settlements often accept an external ruler because that ruler can
judge disputes that local relations have made impossible. The arbitrator's
distance is useful only if the judgment can be enforced.

An arbitrator who always favors the greater payer becomes another market. One
who ignores local custom creates compliance without settlement.

### Monster Protection

Some protection agreements concern monsters rather than human enemies. The
protector maintains wards, payments, hunts, warning paths, or negotiated
boundaries beyond one village's means.

Breaking such an arrangement can make liberation and catastrophe arrive
together. A faction may conceal the contract because admitting dependence upon
the creature weakens its public story.

### Failure of Warning

When no warning arrives, ask where the chain broke.

- The watch slept or fled.
- The signal device failed through neglect.
- A messenger chose kin over duty.
- An official suppressed warning to protect reputation.
- The threatened settlement had been excluded from the network.
- Something imitated the expected all-clear.

Each cause points toward a different remedy and a different enemy.

### Failure to Answer

A protector may fail because force is absent, roads are broken, stores are
thin, command is divided, or the threatened place is judged expendable.

Honest inability can be politically survivable if admitted early enough for
the settlement to seek another answer. False reassurance converts later loss
into betrayal.

### Compensation after Failure

Protection can be repaired through returned dues, rebuilding, blood price,
hostages, public dismissal of a failed officer, or service against the next
threat.

Compensation must fall upon the protector. Requiring the harmed settlement to
fund its own relief confirms that the bond has ended.

### The New Protector

A failed protection bond creates an opening. Neighboring factions, mercenary
bands, religious orders, monster brokers, and adventurers may offer help.

The offer's price matters more than its immediate success. A village rescued by
a company may discover that winter quarters were part of the bargain. A Raven
Sister's aid may require public rejection of Rust tribute. An orc clan may ask
for fosterage and road rights rather than grain.

### Self-Protection

Settlements sometimes replace a protector with stronger local watch, walls,
alliance, or a stronghold. Success changes internal authority. The defense voice
gains weight. Households that supplied labor expect a say in future dues.

Local defense can also produce aggressive confidence. The wall built against
raiders becomes a base for collecting toll from weaker neighbors.

### Protection Signs at the Table

Show the health of the bond through:

- fresh patrol hoofprints on a dangerous road
- a warning horn with a cracked mouthpiece
- fodder set aside for riders who have not come
- recovered cattle marked for disputed division
- a child who knows three alarm signals
- a repaired gate bearing another faction's timber mark
- an empty watch post whose fire is still warm

The condition of the system tells players more than a speech about loyalty.

## Hostages, Fosterage, and Marriage

### Living Surety

A hostage gives a bargain a body. Harm to that body carries political cost, but
only while the hostage remains loved, identifiable, and plausibly recoverable.

Hostage-taking is not one practice. It ranges from honored residence with
limited freedom to chained confinement. The terms determine whether the bond
resembles fosterage, imprisonment, or a delayed execution.

### Choosing a Hostage

A useful hostage connects to decision-makers. An heir is obvious and dangerous.
A beloved sibling, skilled child, spouse, priest, captain, or genealogy keeper
may exert greater practical pressure.

Taking the wrong person reveals ignorance. A despised cousin may become an exile
at the captor's expense. Taking the only healer may provoke resistance because
compliance cannot keep the settlement alive.

### The Hostage Compact

Terms should name:

- whose conduct the hostage secures
- what counts as breach
- where the hostage resides
- what treatment is owed
- who may visit or inspect
- how long the compact lasts
- what releases or exchanges the hostage
- which witnesses judge harm

Vagueness gives the stronger party room. It also gives every later act the
capacity to trigger war.

### Honored Hostages

An honored hostage eats at table, learns the seat's customs, receives training,
and may form bonds with captors. Freedom remains bounded. Guards may be called
companions. Departure still requires permission.

Such treatment preserves value and can turn future enemies into intermediaries.
It can also estrange the hostage from birth kin, which may be the captor's
intention.

### Hostage Cruelty

Cruel treatment can force immediate compliance. It weakens long bargains by
making rescue, vengeance, or self-sacrifice preferable to patience.

Visible injury reduces deniability. Hidden deprivation preserves appearances
while risking sickness and death. Threats against children spread fear through
people who had no voice in the compact.

### Showing the Hostage Alive

Regular proof sustains the agreement. The hostage may appear at festival, send
a recognized object, speak through a trusted visitor, or be inspected by a
priest or healer.

Proof can be manipulated. Letters may be dictated. Objects may be old. A
shapechanger or illusion can imitate appearance. Knowledge of a private scar or
habit may become politically decisive.

### Hostage Escape

Escape embarrasses the keeper and frees the secured party only if the compact
treated captivity as the guarantee. Some rulers declare escape a breach by the
hostage's kin. Others conceal it while searching.

Helping an escape is an act against the bargain, even when morally defensible.
Players should know which households will pay if they succeed.

### Fosterage

Fosterage binds households through care and shared formation. The fostered
person learns work, language, ritual, kin history, and obligation inside another
group.

Good fosterage gives the child standing in both houses. Bad fosterage extracts
labor, disciplines identity, or keeps a hostage beneath the language of kinship.

The line is tested at adult standing. Can the fosterling choose where to live?

### Political Apprenticeship

A child or young adult may be fostered to learn craft, letters, horsemanship,
religion, or command. Skill makes the exchange valuable beyond peace.

The teacher gains influence over future office. The birth house gains knowledge
and a person able to move between networks. Both may fear where loyalty settled
during training.

### Marriage as Coalition

Political marriage links households rather than merely rulers. It reallocates
inheritance, residence, labor, protection, and future children.

Negotiations therefore involve people whose names do not appear in the formal
compact: siblings displaced from rooms, elders losing care, apprentices losing
a teacher, and servants moving with stores.

### Bride-Price, Dowry, and Settlement Gifts

Marriage transfers may include animals, silver, cloth, tools, land use, service,
or shares in future yield. Their purpose varies. They can compensate a household
for lost labor, establish the new unit, secure the spouse's standing, or display
the alliance publicly.

Dispute arises when one side treats the transfer as purchase and another as
protected property. Record who may reclaim it after death, infertility,
desertion, or repudiation.

### Residence

Where the couple lives decides which household gains daily labor and which kin
network surrounds children. Residence may matter more than the ceremony.

A powerful house may demand that the incoming spouse leave all familiar
witnesses behind. A balanced alliance may alternate residence or establish a
new house on contested ground.

### Marriage and Peace

A marriage can lower feud because harming the rival now harms recognized kin.
It can also carry the feud into one household. Spouses become messengers,
suspected spies, or living reminders of a settlement neither side accepted
fully.

Peace marriages work best when supported by shared property, fosterage, and
regular visits. Ceremony alone cannot hold a road.

### Failure of the Marriage Compact

Failure may come through abuse, infertility accusations, disputed children,
failure of residence, withheld transfers, religious conflict, or political
change that makes the alliance inconvenient.

The response can include return, compensation, separation, repudiation,
hostage-taking, or feud. A person seeking to leave may have fewer recognized
rights than the households that negotiated the union.

### Human and Non-Human Timescales

Marriage between different kin can join unequal lifespans and concepts of
descent. Elvenspring already live within that tension. Dwarf and human families
may disagree about when an inheritance matures. Elves may view a human marriage
as a brief political season while the human house stakes generations upon it.

Do not reduce such unions to romance or tragedy. Trace property, children,
memory, bodily aging, and who remains to interpret the compact.

### Signs of Living Surety

Use visible details:

- a hostage wearing fine clothes without a belt knife
- a fosterling answering to two kin names
- marriage silver kept sealed rather than spent
- a child's letters checked by the same priest each season
- two households displaying matching repaired cups
- a spouse seated among guests after years of residence
- a hostage room with an outside lock and an inside shrine

The arrangement's tenderness and violence can occupy the same object.

## Charters, Tolls, Markets, and Roads

### A Charter Is a Recognized Exception

A charter grants a person, settlement, shrine, or faction the right to do
something others cannot do freely: hold market, collect toll, operate ferry,
store goods, settle strangers, cut timber, quarry stone, or pass under special
protection.

The right exists while enough powers recognize it and someone maintains the
work beneath it.

### Charter Proof

Proof may be written, sealed, sung, carved, or embodied in repeated practice.
A surviving document gains force only if its grantor still matters or another
power adopts the obligation.

A charter from a dead lord can remain useful because neighboring settlements
built expectations around it. It can also become a scrap used to justify a new
toll on a road nobody kept.

### Market Right

Market right coordinates time, measure, protection, and dispute. It permits
strangers to arrive with enough confidence to expose goods.

The holder must provide known market days, safe ground, measures, waste control,
and an answer to theft or violence. Failure in any function lowers the value of
the right before it erases the title.

### Toll Right

A toll claims payment for maintained passage or enforced control. The strongest
tolls connect to a visible service: repaired bridge, guarded road, ferry,
cleared pass, or convoy protection.

A toll without service approaches tribute. A toll without enforcement becomes
an invitation to bargain at spearpoint with each traveler.

### Exemptions

Priests, messengers, allied households, market sellers, funeral parties,
military escorts, and old local families may claim exemption.

Exemptions reveal hierarchy. They also create fraud. A merchant travels beneath
a shrine sign. A raiding party carries a funeral cloth. A lord's cousin claims
that every wagon in the train belongs to the household.

Gatekeepers need discretion. Discretion becomes patronage.

### Roads as Political Works

A road requires clearing, drainage, bridges, markers, patrol, and repeated
repair. The faction that claims it must coordinate labor across settlements.

Road work creates resentment where benefits travel elsewhere. A village may
maintain a route used mainly by collectors and soldiers. Another profits from
market traffic without contributing to the difficult marsh section.

Work quotas become border politics written in mud.

### Ferries and Bridges

Crossings concentrate power because geography narrows choice. Ferrymen,
bridgekeepers, rope makers, carpenters, and guards form a small political body
around the right.

Flood or damage can suspend the toll while increasing the crossing's strategic
value. Whoever funds repair may acquire a claim stronger than the old charter.

### Safe-Conduct

Safe-conduct places a faction's reputation around a traveler, convoy, envoy, or
route. It promises that harm will be answered.

The token may be seal, cord, marked staff, escort, letter, or spoken message.
Local people must recognize it. Enemies must believe retaliation can reach
them.

Forging safe-conduct attacks Reach and Mandate together.

### Convoy Politics

Convoys need guards and carts. Animals require fodder and repair keeps both
moving. Lodging and information must be secured ahead of them.
Their route distributes income and burden. Settlements passed over may sabotage
or lobby. Settlements chosen for overnight stops gain trade while feeding
strangers.

The convoy leader decides which delayed wagon is abandoned and which receives
the spare wheel. That decision can start a feud between merchants whose faction
claims to protect them equally.

### Road Closure

Closing a road can enforce quarantine, deny an enemy, protect a monster
boundary, compel toll, or manufacture scarcity. The stated reason may differ
from the useful consequence.

Closure is real only at crossings, gates, inns, markets, and guide networks. A
decree cannot block every forest path. It can make unauthorized travel costly
by denying shelter and lawful protection.

### The Road after the Mist

Reopened routes carry strangers into places whose charters assumed local
knowledge. Measures clash. Old exemptions return. Ruined bridges force traffic
through fields. New strongholds collect before older claimants arrive.

Every repaired road redistributes political weight. The place that was central
under isolation may become a backwater. The poor ferry may become the only
crossing between two reviving markets.

### Road Signs at the Table

Use:

- two toll boards naming different authorities
- fresh gravel ending at a settlement boundary
- a safe-conduct token no young guard recognizes
- bridge timbers marked by three contributing villages
- a funeral party waiting while merchants cross first
- an exempt shrine wagon carrying concealed trade goods
- a closed road with no guards and no travelers willing to test it

The right is visible in who waits.

## Judgment, Compensation, and Outlawry

### Judgment Restores a Usable Relation

The best judgment lets people return to work without pretending the injury did
not occur. It names responsibility, fixes compensation or restraint, provides a
way to verify performance, and declares what happens after satisfaction.

Punishment alone may deter. It rarely repairs a mill share, marriage, grazing
boundary, or blood relation.

### Jurisdiction

Before hearing a case, people ask who has the right to judge it. The answer may
depend upon place, kin, faith, household, oath, victim, or type of injury.

A lord may judge road violence. A village circle may judge grazing. A priest may
judge oath breach. An orc women's council may judge descent and partnership. A
dwarf clan may reserve defective work for inspectors whose authority outlives
the local ruler.

Competing jurisdictions let an accused seek a favorable bench. They also let a
powerful judge expand authority by accepting cases others cannot settle.

### Summons

A summons must reach the accused under terms that make appearance possible.
Safe-conduct may be required. Hostages or sureties may guarantee return.

Ignoring a summons can count as defiance only when the court had recognizable
jurisdiction and the road was open. Rulers often forget the second condition.

### Hearing

Hearings combine statement, witness, oath, reputation, material evidence, and
the judge's need to preserve peace. Exact procedure varies. The practical
questions remain stable:

- Who may speak?
- In what order?
- Which witness can be challenged?
- What physical evidence can be inspected?
- Who restrains the parties?
- What result can actually be enforced?

A brilliant judgment beyond enforcement weakens the bench.

### Oath and Ordeal

Where evidence fails, a court may accept sworn denial, supporting oath-helpers,
ritual test, ordeal, divination, or magical examination. These practices do not
remove uncertainty. They assign uncertainty to a recognized sacred or social
process.

Magic complicates ordeal. A spell may reveal, alter, imitate, or coerce. The
authority to select the caster becomes part of the case.

### Compensation within Feud

Compensation converts injury into a transfer the injured side can accept without
further retaliation. It may include silver, animals, tools, land use, labor,
service, marriage revision, public apology, burial duty, or surrender of the
specific object involved.

The amount reflects standing as well as material loss. Killing a household head
damages more than one laboring body. Burning a shrine injures worshippers whose
individual losses cannot be counted separately.

### Surety

A surety answers if the judged party fails. Sureties are chosen from people who
can exert pressure and pay the consequence. Kin, patron, guild, shrine, or
household may stand behind one person.

Accepting surety reintegrates the offender. Refusing every surety pushes the
case toward exclusion or feud.

### Public Apology

An apology matters where humiliation, false accusation, or insult created the
injury. It must repeat the relevant truth before witnesses. Private regret does
not repair public standing.

The form can include kneeling, returning a seat, naming another person's title,
carrying an object, feeding the offended household, or withdrawing words at the
same place they were spoken.

Apology without material repair may be cheap. Material repair without restored
speech can leave the injured person wealthy and dishonored.

### Corporal and Capital Punishment

Maiming, beating, torture, and execution display a power's claim over bodies.
They remove labor, create dependents, and give kin a memory no fine can close.

Such punishment may be accepted where custom, faith, or immediate danger makes
it intelligible. Acceptance does not make it socially cheap. Executioners,
guards, witnesses, burial keepers, and households all carry parts of the act.

### Confiscation

Confiscation transfers property from offender to ruler, victim, shrine, or
community. It can compensate. It can also reveal that prosecution was a route to
land and stores.

Seizing tools may prevent repayment. Seizing seed punishes next year. Seizing a
house displaces dependents who were never heard.

The destination of confiscated goods tells people what the judgment was for.

### Outlawry

Outlawry removes ordinary protection. It may deny shelter, trade, judgment,
burial, or lawful retaliation against anyone who harms the outlaw.

The sentence reaches only as far as recognition. An outlaw in one lordship may
be a useful scout in another. Religious sanctuary may suspend pursuit. Kin may
feed the outlaw while denying knowledge.

Outlawry is therefore a test of Reach and Mandate as much as Decree.

### Degrees of Exclusion

Exclusion can be narrow or total.

- removal from one office
- ban from market or shrine
- expulsion for a season
- loss of weapon or witness rights
- prohibition on guest shelter
- full outlawry
- condemnation of the household as well as the person

Narrow penalties preserve more routes to repair. Total exclusion creates people
who can survive only through rival protection, banditry, wilderness knowledge,
or monsters willing to bargain.

### Sanctuary during Feud

Sanctuary delays ordinary enforcement within a recognized sacred place or under
a protected person's word. It creates time for hearing, negotiation, or escape.

Sanctuary depends upon the strength of the institution. A ruler who violates it
may capture one person and damage every future oath witnessed there.

The sheltered person still consumes food and brings danger. Priests must decide
how long holiness can be provisioned.

### Failed Judgment

A judgment fails when parties reject jurisdiction, compensation cannot be paid,
sureties withdraw, witnesses are exposed as false, enforcement targets the
wrong household, or the judge benefits too openly.

Failure returns the dispute to feud with added grievance against the court.

### Signs of Judgment

Use:

- a compensation animal kept apart until witnesses arrive
- two oath sticks whose cuts do not match
- a gallows maintained beside an empty road
- a sanctuary sleeper near a shrine fire
- confiscated tools rusting in a lord's yard
- a household door marked against sheltering an outlaw
- a widow carrying the key of a house awarded but not surrendered

The verdict continues through objects and daily inconvenience.

## Information, Messengers, and Quiet Power

### Information Has an Owner

News is valuable before it becomes common. The first person to learn that a road
is open, an heir is sick, a levy failed, or a monster moved can trade, flee,
prepare, accuse, or buy before others act.

People therefore ask who told a fact, who was meant to hear it, and why it
arrived now.

### The Information Chain

Every report passes through stages:

`observation -> interpretation -> carrier -> gate -> advisor -> ruler -> order`

Error or manipulation can enter at each stage. A scout sees smoke. The scout
calls it a burned village. A frightened carrier enlarges the force responsible.
The gate delays the message. An advisor suppresses it to protect a captain. The
ruler closes the wrong road.

Investigating a political failure means tracing the chain backward.

### Messengers

Messengers need memory, endurance, route knowledge, and judgment. They must know
which detail can be omitted if capture threatens and which word makes the whole
message useless when changed.

They also need recognized status. Killing a messenger may break safe-conduct
and announce refusal before the message is heard.

### Written and Spoken Messages

Writing preserves exact language and exposes the message to any reader who
captures it. Spoken messages conceal from the illiterate and change inside the
carrier's memory.

Important orders may use both. The letter bears seal and narrow instruction.
The messenger carries context, private limits, and the response expected if the
road has changed.

### Tokens

Seals, broken coins, cords, feathers, marked rings, tally halves, and private
objects authenticate messengers. The token proves access more readily than
truth.

Old tokens become dangerous after office changes. A dismissed steward may still
possess the object that opens three storehouses.

### Route Knowledge

Messages move through inns, shrines, ferries, foster houses, hunters' camps, and
other places able to shelter a carrier. The network is social before it is
geographic.

A road drawn on a map may be slower than a chain of households willing to change
a horse, hide a rider, or guide through a marsh at night.

### Dovecotes and Magical Speech

Pigeons carry short messages home after being transported outward. Their speed
belongs to whoever controls the birds and understands which loft receives them.

Speak to the Wind and other magic can outrun ordinary routes under specific
conditions. Such communication remains scarce, personal, and vulnerable to the
caster's limits. It creates political value around people previously seen only
as strange practitioners.

No communication method removes the need to trust the sender.

### Rumor

Rumor is collective interpretation moving without a responsible carrier. It
often preserves the part people find useful rather than the part first spoken.

A rumor should have a source category:

- road talk from several travelers
- market claim spread by interested sellers
- shrine warning attached to doctrine
- soldier talk carrying partial witness
- servant knowledge escaped from a seat
- deliberate faction work
- old story made current by resemblance

The source determines what distortion is likely.

### Deliberate Rumor

A useful planted rumor contains a fact people can verify and a conclusion the
faction wants them to draw. Pure invention collapses under local knowledge.

To discredit a captain, enemies may reveal a real unpaid debt and imply that the
whole war chest is empty. To turn a village against refugees, they may identify
one genuine theft and attach every missing item to it.

Countering the rumor requires more than denial. People need another explanation
for the verified fact.

### Spies

Most spies are people with an ordinary reason to be present: traders, servants,
grooms, priests, craftspeople, fosterlings, prisoners, entertainers, healers,
or relatives.

They observe what their work exposes. A groom reports mounts. A cook reports
mouths and stores. A scribe reports seals. Asking a spy for information outside
their position increases risk and reduces accuracy.

### Informants

Informants trade narrow access for silver, protection, revenge, advancement, or
relief from another pressure. Their usefulness can end when that need is met.

Long use changes them. They may invent information to preserve payment, conceal
facts that would end access, or develop loyalty toward people they betray.

### Counterintelligence

Seats protect themselves through divided knowledge, changed routes, seal
checks, false schedules, household observation, and deliberate tests.

Testing loyalty by feeding different stories to suspected people is effective
and corrosive. A ruler who uses it constantly creates a hall where nobody can
coordinate honest work.

### Intercepting Messages

Interception can reveal content, delay action, forge response, or merely tell
the target that a route is compromised. Killing the carrier is often less
useful than letting a changed message continue.

Material clues remain: disturbed wax, wrong cord, exhausted horse, unfamiliar
mud, a messenger arriving too clean, or phrasing that fits the ruler better than
the scribe.

### Information under Dread

Fear encourages reports about neighbors and suppresses reports about official
failure. Dread rulers receive many accusations and little reliable warning.

Subordinates learn to bring news that supports doctrine. Bad harvest becomes
heresy. Missing tribute becomes conspiracy. A dangerous road remains safe in
the ledger until an important person dies there.

### Public Knowledge and Secret Knowledge

Factions need some things known. Their seal, punishments, safe-conduct, market
days, and protection terms lose value in secrecy.

Other things gain value when narrow: hostage location, weak wall, disputed heir,
empty store, false genealogy, monster contract, or private alliance.

Political skill lies in deciding what must be believed widely and what must be
known exactly by few.

### Information Signs

Use:

- a messenger changing horses without entering the inn
- two versions of the same decree posted at one market
- fresh wax over an old seal impression
- pigeons absent from a loft before expected trouble
- servants forbidden to use one stair
- a road rumor repeating the same unusual phrase
- a guard who knows the order but not its stated reason

Information has already been handled before the players receive it.

## The Four Pillars as Lived Institutions

### Mandate

Mandate is the expectation that a command deserves obedience. It lives in
recognized succession, kept promises, accepted judgment, ritual witness,
customary restraint, and the belief that refusal would damage an order worth
preserving.

High Mandate appears as unforced anticipation. People prepare before the order
arrives because they trust the pattern. Low Mandate appears as verification.
Every messenger is questioned. Every due requires escort. Every captain asks who
else has agreed.

Mandate is damaged by broken terms, selective justice, exposed falsehood,
unlawful violence, failed protection, or succession nobody accepts.

It recovers through public acts whose cost falls upon the ruler: remission,
compensation, dismissal of a favored offender, kept surrender terms, or useful
service rendered without fresh extraction.

### Force

Force is the capacity to organize and sustain coercion. It includes bodies,
weapons, officers, drill, courage, remounts, replacement, and the willingness of
people to remain after the first injury.

High Force is visible in relief watch, maintained equipment, known command, and
units that arrive together. Low Force hides behind loud recruitment, mixed
weapons, absent captains, and retainers doing work levy should have answered.

Force is damaged by casualties, desertion, unpaid service, officer conflict,
lost arms, broken morale, or repeated use against the faction's own supporters.

It recovers through rest and pay. Replacement and drill rebuild capacity;
a successful defense restores trust. Fighters recover fully only where their
households still welcome them.

### Reach

Reach is the distance at which the faction's intention remains recognizable and
actionable. Roads, ferries, riders, boats, messengers, inns, allied households,
maps, patrols, and local discretion all carry it.

High Reach appears as coordinated arrival. Orders adapt to changed ground
without losing purpose. Low Reach appears as several honest people performing
incompatible versions of the same command.

Reach is damaged by broken crossings, killed messengers, hostile settlements,
route ignorance, false reports, exhausted animals, or expansion beyond the
network that sustains it.

It recovers through repaired roads, renewed safe houses, recognized tokens,
local appointments, and people trusted to interpret rather than repeat.

### Hearth

Hearth is the settled life that keeps power fed. It includes grain, labor,
workshops, animals, shelter, care, household reproduction, trust in ordinary
exchange, and the willingness to remain under the faction's shadow.

High Hearth is visible in repaired roofs, seed held back, apprentices learning,
and carts available without seizure. Low Hearth appears in cannibalized
buildings, delayed burials, empty byres, migrating households, and every skilled
person doing emergency work.

Hearth is damaged by extraction, war, disease, flight, broken mills, consumed
seed, forced labor, or rulers who treat dependents as a store that cannot empty.

It recovers through harvest, repair, relief, return, peace, secure trade, and
enough restraint that households invest in next year.

### Pillar Conflict

Factions often strengthen one pillar by spending another.

- Forced levy may increase immediate Force and damage Hearth.
- Terror may extend obedience and damage Mandate.
- A distant conquest may display Force while thinning Reach.
- Remission may restore Mandate while reducing Stores.
- Heavy road work may improve Reach and exhaust Hearth.

These exchanges should be visible in people and objects. Numbers summarize the
consequence after the fiction establishes it.

### Pillar Imbalance

**Mandate without Force** produces respected orders nobody can defend.

**Force without Mandate** produces compliance requiring constant presence.

**Reach without Hearth** produces impressive movement supplied by exhausted
places.

**Hearth without Reach** produces local plenty vulnerable beyond the next road.

A faction's imbalance tells the GM which offers it will make and which insults
it cannot tolerate.

## Peace, Pressure, Muster, and Campaign

### Peace as Work

Peace is the mode in which customary obligations still carry most acts. The
faction judges, repairs, collects, receives guests, and manages disputes before
they require armed demonstration.

Residents experience peace through predictable demand. They know when payment
comes, how long service lasts, and which office hears complaint. Quiet does not
mean fairness. It means the pattern is legible.

Peace begins to fail when exceptions accumulate around favored people, repairs
are deferred, or collection arrives without the reciprocal acts that once made
it due.

### Pressure

Pressure leans beyond accepted custom while stopping short of open campaign.
Roads close, hostages are requested, collections harden, spies work, patrols
multiply, and public speech narrows.

Pressure is often deniable. Every act has an administrative explanation. The
extra guard protects the bridge. The delayed market prevents disorder. The
hostage is an honored guest. The grain count corrects old arrears.

People recognize the mode through accumulation.

### Muster

Muster converts obligations into formed readiness. Messengers ride. Stores move.
Weapons leave chests. Households decide whom to send and what truth to tell
about absence, illness, or equipment.

Muster creates political sorting. Allies who spoke warmly may delay. Old rivals
may answer because the enemy is worse. Priests choose whether to bless. Wealthy
houses send substitutes. Poor houses send the person who should be cutting hay.

The host's future cohesion is already present in those choices.

### Campaign

Campaign turns political relations into roads, supply, occupation, and armed
timing. The host consumes faster than ordinary reciprocity can replenish.

Rear settlements experience campaign as carts, requisition, wounded people,
rumor, burial, absent labor, and officials who promise repayment after victory.
Front settlements experience it as forced choice between powers.

Campaign success can still destroy the faction's Hearth. Battlefield victory
does not repair that account.

### Moving between Modes

The change should have a visible trigger.

- Peace becomes Pressure when an accepted process is replaced by coercive
  exception.
- Pressure becomes Muster when bodies and stores are called into readiness.
- Muster becomes Campaign when formed force moves or occupation begins.
- Campaign returns toward Peace only after armies disperse, arrears are faced,
  roads reopen, and emergency authority surrenders some of what it took.

Rulers often declare peace before households experience it.

### Emergency Powers

Threat permits rulers to bypass consultation, seize stores, close roads, or
extend service. Such power may be necessary. It also gives officeholders reasons
to prolong emergency.

The end condition should be named when emergency authority begins. Without one,
restoring ordinary custom becomes a new political struggle.

### Seasonal Meaning

The same mode costs differently by season. Muster during harvest attacks Hearth.
Pressure during winter turns denied passage into hunger. Campaign during thaw
breaks roads and animals. Peace after a poor summer may require remission rather
than ordinary collection.

Political calendars are agricultural and bodily calendars.

## Forms of Faction Rule

### Petty Crowns

A petty crown claims more than one household can hold and less than a realm can
administer. Its ruler may be called king, queen, prince, protector, first elder,
or simply lord. The title matters less than the circuits that make it credible.

The ruler travels between halls because no chancery can govern the land in his
place. He hears disputes, confirms local officers, receives dues, exchanges
gifts, and lets distant dependents see that he still has teeth, scars, children,
and a memory.

A crown's treasure is distributed. Grain lies in village lofts. Weapons hang in
the halls of sworn households. Pack animals belong to people who can refuse to
lend them. The crown may command these things, but command must pass through
relations.

The political danger is absence. A ruler who does not travel becomes a rumor.
Local protectors begin settling matters without reference to the crown. Their
temporary discretion grows roots.

### Castle Lordships

A castle lordship grows around a defensible place and the country required to
feed it. Walls create an argument: the garrison protects the district, and the
district therefore owes the garrison food, labor, intelligence, and obedience.

The argument is strongest during danger and weakest when soldiers become the
danger. Every castle household contains people whose work touches the
countryside: bailiffs, millers, stable hands, woodcutters, drovers, collectors,
smiths, guides, hostages, and petitioners.

Castles magnify small administrative choices. If the garrison takes seed grain,
the next hunger begins behind its walls. If a gate officer humiliates carters,
trade chooses another road. If the lord repairs only the approach used by
soldiers, villagers learn whose safety counts.

The lordship's recurring question is whether protection remains a reciprocal
bond or has become rent charged by armed occupants.

### Clan Confederacies

A clan confederacy rules through assembled loyalties. It has no single body.
Each constituent clan brings its own elders, grievances, sacred obligations,
armed following, and private threshold for leaving.

Confederacies excel at gathering strength around a named insult. They struggle
with patient collection and uniform judgment. A leader must allow constituent
clans enough distinction to preserve their honor while preventing those
distinctions from becoming separate wars.

Councils perform much of the government's work. Seating order, speaking order,
the division of gifts, and whose version of an old event is heard first all
carry political meaning.

A confederacy usually breaks at the edge, not the center. A lesser clan is
neglected, pays twice, loses a disputed pasture, or sees another clan excused
from service. Its withdrawal then gives larger rivals permission to reconsider
their oaths.

### Militant Faiths

A militant faith claims that command descends through sacred truth. Its officers
may judge, collect, preach, investigate, heal, punish, and wage war under one
authority.

Faith gives wide Reach because ritual and doctrine travel in memory. It also
makes failures dangerous. A corrupt collector implicates more than himself. A
lost battle may become evidence that a rite was impure, a superior false, or the
faith abandoned.

Militant faiths govern calendars as much as territory. They name feast, fast,
penance, procession, burial, lawful union, forbidden knowledge, and the hours in
which work must stop. Control of time enters every household without a garrison.

Their internal politics concern interpretation. The officer who decides what a
defeat means may wield more lasting power than the captain who survived it.

### Druidic Orders

A druidic order may possess little taxable land and still govern access to
water, burial ground, healing, sacred groves, animal paths, and the rites that
settlements believe keep the year in order.

Its power appears seasonal. A village that ignores the order in summer may seek
it desperately when blight, strange birth, fever, or restless dead arrives.

The order's dispersed members can carry news and custom farther than armed
officers. That reach depends upon hospitality. A druid denied shelter loses not
only a bed but a point in the living road by which warnings travel.

Conflict arises when preservation and subsistence meet. A protected wood may be
the last source of winter fuel. A forbidden hunt may be the meal that keeps a
household alive. The order must decide which necessity can be honored without
making every prohibition negotiable.

### Merchant Leagues

A merchant league rules movement, measurement, credit, and confidence. Its
armed strength guards scales, bridges, contracts, warehouses, and people whose
names make distant promises credible.

League power is easiest to miss because it often appears as choice. A ferry is
available, but only at the league rate. Grain may be sold, but only after debts
are settled according to the league's tally. A village may reject its charter,
but then no bonded caravan stops there.

Its officers include weighers, brokers, warehouse keepers, caravan masters,
guards, money changers, interpreters, and witnesses. Corruption in any one
office can turn a remote promise into worthless marks.

A league fears broken trust more than a single lost cargo. It may spend blood to
punish a fraud whose material value was small because every other contract hangs
on the response.

### Free Companies in Service

A free company begins as organized force for hire. It becomes political when it
holds winter quarters, collects payment directly, judges camp disputes, grants
safe-conduct, or protects the same road long enough for residents to appeal to
its captain instead of their lord.

The company lives between contract and appetite. Discipline preserves its sale
value. Plunder supplements irregular pay. These needs pull in opposite
directions whenever a patron delays.

Camp followers are part of the institution: armorers, cooks, launders,
prostitutes, drovers, healers, sutlers, children, partners, creditors, and
prisoners. A captain who counts only fighters misunderstands what keeps the
banner moving.

Companies become accidental lordships when a weak patron grants collection
rights. Temporary control of a toll, mine, or district gives soldiers an income
they may prefer to wages and a place they may refuse to surrender.

### Hidden Societies

A hidden society governs through knowledge other people cannot safely admit it
possesses. It arranges debts, smuggling, murder, sanctuary, illicit worship,
stolen correspondence, or access to prohibited goods.

Secrecy does not remove administration. Someone recruits, tests, pays, carries,
records, punishes, and decides what each member is allowed to know. Cells may
protect the whole while encouraging local invention and private abuse.

The society's weakest point is often interpretation. An order passes through so
many concealed hands that nobody can tell whether its cruelty came from the
center, an ambitious intermediary, or an enemy forging signs.

Ordinary people may depend upon a hidden society without joining it. A smuggler
brings salt around a predatory toll. A secret priest buries the outlawed dead.
A blackmailer keeps a violent officer cautious. Such services complicate any
clean attempt to destroy the network.

### Dark Dominions

A dark dominion treats dread, supernatural force, or systematic degradation as
ordinary instruments. It may still keep accounts, repair bridges, reward
service, and enforce limits among its own agents. Competent evil is more durable
than random cruelty.

Subjects learn fine distinctions within danger. They know which collector can
be delayed with gifts, which patrol obeys written orders, which shrine offers
real sanctuary, and which accusation cannot be answered once spoken.

The dominion's public doctrine usually explains suffering as necessity,
purification, inherited guilt, or the price of protection. Its agents need not
believe every word. They need the doctrine to distribute responsibility until
no one person feels able to refuse.

Its recurring weakness is the cost of fear. Fear secures immediate compliance
while destroying candid reports. The ruler receives pleasing lies until an
empty granary, vanished district, or mutinous garrison supplies the correction.

### Mixed Rule

Most lasting factions combine forms. A castle lord may marry into a clan,
receive temple sanction, borrow from merchants, and retain a company whose
captain controls the outer villages.

Mixed rule creates resilience because one bond can survive another's failure.
It also creates competing chains of command. The priest, bailiff, clan elder,
creditor, and captain may each issue a lawful demand upon the same cart.

For play, identify the two forms that matter most. Their disagreement supplies
the faction's internal politics.

## Political Traditions of the Kin

### Humans: Rule Made during Ruin

Human authority in the Ravenlands carries the memory of settlement, conquest,
plague, religious domination, and long enclosure by the Blood Mist. Human
villages therefore recognize many incompatible grounds of rule at once.

A family may honor an old royal claim, obey a Rust collector, conceal a Raven
rite, hire an orc guard, and seek elven healing without considering itself
inconsistent. Survival taught layered allegiance.

Human rulers often claim restoration. The word is politically useful because
every listener restores a different past. One hears a lost crown; another, a
free village or old faith. Others hear safe roads and lawful markets, or the
years before a hated family rose.

The dangerous human talent is converting emergency into office. A war leader,
grain keeper, or shrine defender remains in charge because the next emergency
is already visible.

### Elves: Stewardship across Mortal Generations

Elven politics is shaped by lives, memories, and bodily truths that do not fit
human succession. A promise made to a human grandparent may feel recent to the
elf who spoke it and a remote family tale to the descendants now asked to honor
it.

Elven authority favors custodianship, precedence, and witness. The holder of a
place may be understood as its present keeper rather than its owner. Human
neighbors can mistake patience for surrender and delayed action for
forgetfulness.

Ruby, body, and identity make injury politically difficult. Theft or violence
may threaten more than property or flesh. Outsiders who treat such matters as
ordinary compensation can deepen an offense while attempting settlement.

Elven factions divide over contact. Withdrawal preserves what remains. Action
recovers what was taken. Alliance risks dilution and betrayal. Each position
can call itself faithful stewardship.

### Half-Elves and Elvenspring: Translation as Power

People who move between elven and human relations often become interpreters of
custom rather than merely language. They know which human urgency sounds like
bad faith to an elf and which elven delay feels like contempt to a starving
village.

That competence grants influence and attracts suspicion. Every translation can
be accused of favoring one inheritance. Intermediaries may become indispensable
while never being admitted fully to either council.

Elvenspring political authority frequently grows through service: healing,
woodcraft, ritual knowledge, patrol, teaching, and the maintenance of ties no
single settlement can keep alone.

Their danger is permanent mediation. Others may praise their ability to bridge
worlds while denying them the right to decide where either road leads.

### Dwarves: Work, Clan, and the Weight of Annals

Dwarven rule joins clan obligation to made things. Roads, halls, walls, forges,
archives, waterworks, and measured claims supply visible evidence that a line
has kept faith with the world.

Authority speaks through competence. An elder who cannot judge stone, labor,
stores, or the probable cost of a promise may retain title but lose the quiet
obedience on which execution depends.

Written and recited annals enlarge political memory. A present compromise must
answer prior settlements whose terms were preserved by specialists. This makes
dwarven law durable and gives rival readers ample material for dispute.

The Gathering at Stonegarden, held once every two years, permits clans to renew
peace through rite, contest, bazaar, record, and carefully staged rivalry.
Champions display more than individual excellence. Their conduct becomes an
argument about the clan that trained, equipped, and selected them.

Dwarven politics becomes bitter when completion itself is disputed. One clan
calls a work sufficient and claims its due. Another sees an abandoned promise
embedded in every unfinished course of stone.

### Orcs: Freedom beneath the Burden of History

Orc political life is shaped by enslavement, imposed service, dispersed kin,
and the continuing need to decide what freedom requires after liberation.

Some clans value force because nobody else will secure their autonomy. Others
value councils and distributed leadership because concentrated command resembles
the institutions that once owned them.

Spoils, weapons, grazing, shelter, and recognition must be distributed in ways
fighters regard as earned. A leader who hoards may be strong for one season and
alone in the next battle.

Orc claim-making can sound blunt to outsiders because old dispossession leaves
little patience for refined language that conceals possession. Yet clan law may
contain precise histories of who sheltered whom, who broke captivity, and which
debt cannot be priced.

No single orc polity represents the kin. Attempts to treat all clans as one
negotiating body repeat the outsider's habit of seeing a people only as a force
to manage.

### Halflings: Domestic Reputation and the Politics of Descent

Halfling public life rests upon households that present comfort, continuity,
and respectable fertility as evidence of moral fitness. Meals, gardens,
marriages, and children's accomplishments become political statements.

Matchmaking joins property, reputation, religious hope, and fear surrounding
the birth of goblin children. Ambitious parents may turn courtship into a dense
campaign of tutoring, gifts, ancestry claims, whispered defects, and managed
encounters.

A family can injure a child while insisting every pressure protects the child's
future. Failure is then concealed because public acknowledgment threatens
siblings, marriage prospects, and the parents' standing as judges of others.

Halfling offices may look gentle while exercising sharp exclusion through
invitations, meal seating, credit, introductions, and the circulation of family
knowledge.

The kin's political shadow is the person passed away. Any settlement order built
upon hidden goblin kin contains a truth capable of breaking households and
making new ones.

### Goblins: Affiliation Chosen and Tested

Goblin politics grows among bands, tribes, foster relations, cunning leaders,
shared dangers, and the practical knowledge required to live beyond halfling
respectability.

Membership may depend more upon participation than descent. The person who
keeps watch, shares meat, honors routes, and answers danger becomes kin through
repeated acts.

Leadership is often immediate and competence-sensitive. A guide commands on a
dangerous path; a hunter decides the approach; a speaker handles a settlement;
an elder remembers which apparent gift once became a chain.

Outsiders mistake flexible authority for absence of order. They learn otherwise
when a whole band refuses them with one mind because they insulted the person
whose quiet work held it together.

Relations with halflings carry private knowledge, anger, tenderness, mockery,
and the possibility of recognition. A political agreement between the kin may
become a family crisis before it becomes a treaty.

### Wolfkin: The Pack against Hunt-Mad Solitude

Wolfkin society treats belonging as protection of the mind as well as the body.
Prolonged isolation carries the feared possibility of becoming hunt-mad: the
self narrows toward quarry, pursuit, killing, and feeding until social identity
is no longer reachable.

Pack discipline therefore has psychological weight. Shared sleep, grooming,
argument, scent, work, and repeated response to a known voice keep a wolfkin
located among persons.

Exile can be understood as a terrible sentence even where no execution is
intended. A lone wolfkin may fear the open forest as a human fears a closing
cell. Every direction offers room to run and no person to call him back.

Leadership must balance autonomy against connection. Too much coercion makes
the pack a prison. Too little attention allows a distressed member to drift
beyond ordinary thought while companions tell themselves he wanted solitude.

The death of a pack is both bereavement and an emergency of identity. Other
wolfkin may take in the survivor through formal watch, shared tasks, sleep near
the hearth, and patient interruption of the first dangerous withdrawals.

### Kin Difference inside One Settlement

Mixed settlements do not operate by averaging customs. They create negotiated
interfaces.

A dwarven written term may stand beside a goblin witnessed performance. A
human office may collect from halfling households through family brokers. A
wolfkin watch may require paired duty that human officers misread as waste.

Political conflict often begins at the interface: who counts as kin, what
constitutes consent, when a promise descends, whether exile is survivable, and
which form of evidence can close a grievance.

Good local law names the interface. Bad local law assumes everybody meant the
same thing.

## The Great Powers without Campaign Spoilers

### A Spoiler-Safe Method

This section describes public reputation, ordinary contact, and institutional
pressure. It does not state concealed campaign agendas, secret identities, or
future revelations.

Treat every great power as three things at once: the story told about it, the
officer encountered locally, and the material demand that reaches a household.
These may contradict one another without any being false.

### The Rust Brothers

The Rust Brothers offer order through submission, doctrinal certainty, iron,
investigation, and an administered fear of worse powers. Their public presence
is recognizable before a word is spoken: deliberately rusted weight, gaunt
discipline, smoke, metal striking stone, and bodies trained to endure discomfort
as proof of election.

Settlements experience the Order through tribute, searches, controlled
knowledge, religious correction, hostage pressure, selective torture, and
directed execution. It generally seeks submission that continues producing,
not empty ground.

The local Brother may be punctual, sober, brave against monsters, and
unredeemably committed to a system that degrades the people it claims to save.
Competence makes the system harder to dismiss and more dangerous to depend upon.

### Raven Networks

Raven-aligned power is dispersed through healers, witnesses, shrines, household
rites, hidden teachers, and memories of obligation. It may possess great
Mandate where it cannot safely display Force.

Its agents often arrive as people performing necessary work. A burial is
completed. A fever is treated. An oath is remembered. A child is carried to a
safer house. Political organization grows from the trust made by those acts.

The network's weakness is exposure and uneven coordination. One village may
believe help is near because a sister passed last season. The sister may have
three districts already calling for the same hands.

### Eastern Hosts and Claimants

Eastern powers can gather around land, vengeance, protection, lineage, and the
promise that the old confinement need not determine the future. Riders and
exiles give such hosts reach while complicating their Hearth.

Their public language emphasizes common enemies. Beneath it lie arguments over
whose loss matters, who receives recovered land, which kin supplies the
fighters, and whether victory restores an order or creates one.

A claimant becomes credible when people accept immediate costs for a future
settlement. Every unfulfilled grant and unpaid household then becomes part of
the claimant's political debt.

### Dwarven Seats

Dwarven strongholds project endurance, stored competence, and the possibility
of force descending from defended heights. Lowland neighbors may seek their
craft and fear their patience.

Public dwarven politics turns upon clan, old construction, inherited claim,
and disagreement over how directly to enter the struggles of shorter-lived kin.
Even apparent inaction may contain extensive preparation and internal dispute.

Foreign envoys who ask only whether the dwarves will fight miss the prior
questions: under whose wording, for which completion, supplied by whom, recorded
where, and with what obligation after victory.

### Elven Orders

Elven powers may present themselves as wardens, hunters, custodians, or
recoverers rather than rulers. Their actions nevertheless govern access to
forest, ruby, ruin, sanctuary, and dangerous knowledge.

Some work through relationships and preservation. Others answer theft and
corruption with mobile violence. Human settlements may encounter both and call
their disagreement inconsistency.

Elven time makes warning difficult. A trespass repeated for three human
generations may remain one continuing offense to the witness who first forbade
it.

### Orc Courts and Confederacies

Orc powers seek security, recognition, resources, and forms of greatness not
defined by former masters. Courts may adopt splendor precisely because outsiders
expect only camps and raiders.

Their public ambition can attract mockery. Mockery is politically expensive
when ceremony is being used to turn scattered force into an institution.

Deal with each clan, court, and band as a political body with its own history.
The phrase “the orcs” usually signals that a speaker has not done the necessary
work.

### Mercenary Powers

Mercenary companies become great powers at the seam between war and rule. They
possess organized force, movement, internal law, and names capable of carrying
credit.

A company's public agenda may be no more mysterious than payment and continued
employment. Secondary interests arise from winter quarters, marriages, old
employers, burial grounds, wounded veterans, and collection rights.

The question is when a contract-holder becomes a resident power. Villagers may
answer long before the captain does.

### Public Rumor as Political Terrain

Rumor about a great power should contain one true observation, one inherited
fear, and one interested distortion.

A ferryman's story may accurately describe an officer's demand, repeat a
grandparent's terror, and exaggerate a rival road's danger to keep traffic on
his own crossing.

Do not correct rumors from above. Let contact reveal which part has weight.

## Succession, Regency, and Divided Houses

### Succession Begins before Death

Succession starts when people alter conduct in expectation of a vacancy. Gifts
change direction. Officers preserve copies. Heirs are invited to witness.
Creditors shorten terms. Marriage talks accelerate. Old enemies become polite.

A healthy ruler can create succession politics by falling ill, failing to
travel, losing a battle, remaining childless, or favoring one heir in public.

The crucial question is not who has the best abstract claim. It is which
obligations continue through the claimant.

### Forms of Claim

A claimant may appeal to descent, designation, election, marriage, conquest,
adoption, sacred sign, possession of the seat, custody of an heir, or completion
of a required deed.

Claims become strong by accumulation. A weak blood claim with the treasury,
garrison, burial rite, and widow's consent may defeat a stronger genealogy.

Every claim should name the people whose status improves if it succeeds. They
are its working body.

### The Deathbed

The ruler's last hours gather witnesses whose memories will soon disagree. Pain,
fever, magic, fear, and interested attendants complicate any final declaration.

A deathbed word may be binding, invalid, sacred, manipulated, or all four to
different audiences.

The person who controls access controls which reconciliations occur and which
instructions receive witnesses.

### The Body and Burial

Possession of the body can matter as much as possession of a seal. Burial
confirms identity, permits public mourning, displays ritual legitimacy, and
creates a place around which memory gathers.

A missing body feeds rival stories. A hurried burial suggests fear. A delayed
burial may preserve evidence while violating religious duty.

Funeral seating, gift distribution, watch order, and the persons permitted to
touch the dead are early votes in the succession.

### Minority and Regency

A child heir concentrates Mandate while lacking the practical capacities to
exercise it. The regent governs in another person's name and therefore has both
authority and an approaching end date.

Good regents preserve the heir's future household, educate the child, maintain
relationships, and leave records clear enough to surrender power.

Bad regents need not plan usurpation. They merely make every institution depend
upon themselves until handing it over appears irresponsible.

The heir grows among adults whose livelihoods depend upon what kind of ruler
the child becomes.

### Widowhood and the Surviving Spouse

A surviving spouse may hold keys, household loyalty, marriage ties, private
knowledge, and the trust of dependents. Calling that person merely a widow or
widower can conceal the most competent political actor in the seat.

Remarriage may stabilize an alliance, threaten an heir, invite outside control,
or give the survivor necessary protection.

Mourning customs determine how openly the spouse can act. Quiet withdrawal may
contain intense government through chamber servants and kin.

### Sibling Rule

Shared inheritance avoids immediate exclusion and creates continuous ambiguity.
Brothers, sisters, spouses, and cousins may divide offices, regions, income, or
ritual duties.

The arrangement works while subjects know whose command applies. It fails when
each ruler grants exceptions in the other's sphere.

Rivals often attack shared rule by obeying both. They solicit contradictory
orders, then claim innocence.

### Election and Acclamation

Election may occur through formal votes, clan consensus, armed assembly,
priestly recognition, or the crowd's acceptance of a name at the right moment.

Acclamation is not spontaneous merely because it is loud. Supporters prepare
positions, phrases, banners, weapons, and the order in which respected voices
will speak.

A defeated candidate may accept the outcome in exchange for office, marriage,
compensation, or explicit protection. Excluding every rival produces purity and
future rebellion.

### The Seat during Vacancy

Keys, gates, stores, archives, stables, kitchens, and messenger animals require
daily decisions while great people debate legitimacy.

The household officer who keeps bread moving can prevent a constitutional
crisis from becoming a riot. That officer can also choose which claimant's
orders are copied and which are delayed for clarification.

Vacancy reveals the politics of maintenance.

### Succession Debt

Claimants promise lavishly because future authority seems able to pay. Victory
arrives with offices pledged twice, land granted before survey, remissions that
empty stores, and fighters expecting immediate reward.

The first year of a new rule is therefore a test of disappointment management.
Punishing former supporters may restore the treasury and destroy Mandate.

### Divided Houses

A house can split without open war. Members attend separate meals, maintain
separate guards, patronize rival shrines, and send contradictory letters under
the same emblem.

Dependents suffer first. A miller cannot know which instruction protects him.
A servant's ordinary movement becomes suspected espionage.

The division becomes public when outsiders can profit by it without immediate
punishment.

### Settlement Response to a Disputed Succession

Settlements delay, divide payments, request confirmation, hide stores, and ask
each claimant to renew protection.

Such caution may be called treason by whoever expected automatic obedience. It
is often the rational response to being punished later for obeying the loser.

Adventurers may be asked to carry a declaration, secure a witness, escort an
heir, recover a seal, guard a funeral, or learn which garrison will open its
gate. None is a neutral errand.

## Dependents, Servants, and People without a Seat

### The Political Population below the Council

Rule is carried by people who rarely speak in council: servants, tenants,
herders, apprentices, ferrymen, camp followers, fosterlings, debtors, refugees,
prisoners, healers, scribes, cooks, and the kin of executed people.

They do not stand outside politics. They possess constrained choices inside it.
They can hurry, misunderstand, warn, conceal, remember, spoil, delay, misdirect,
or leave.

An order becomes material only through their labor.

### Household Servants

Service creates access without equality. A chamber servant hears sleep, illness,
private argument, and unguarded names. A kitchen worker sees appetite, guest
order, fasting, poison fear, and the true size of stores.

Long-serving people may be treated as family until inheritance, accusation, or
marriage reveals the limit of that language.

Servants develop ethics of selective knowledge. Telling everything destroys the
household. Telling nothing permits abuse. Survival lies in judging which secret
belongs to the office, the family, the victim, or the grave.

### Tenants and Bound Cultivators

People working land under another's claim negotiate through rent, labor days,
grazing access, seed loans, repair duty, and customary exceptions.

Their political leverage is seasonal. Collective delay during plowing or
harvest can injure a lord more than a petition, but the same delay endangers
their children.

Flight is possible where land and protection can be found elsewhere. A lord who
calls every departure theft admits that residence has become captivity.

### Apprentices and Retainers in Training

An apprentice belongs partly to a birth household and partly to the master's
working household. Discipline, debt, secret technique, and future independence
all pass through this relation.

Masters may use apprentices as political pledges. Training the child of an ally
binds households. Refusing advancement can keep a skilled dependent cheap and
obedient.

The apprentice knows where quality is feigned, whose order was rushed, and what
material vanished after dark.

### Fosterlings

Fosterlings are honored dependents and living guarantees. They may love the
house that holds them, resent the purpose of their residence, or fear returning
to a birth family now half-strange.

They learn accents, loyalties, routes, and private weaknesses on both sides of
an alliance.

When relations fail, the treatment of fosterlings reveals whether the original
bond had any moral substance beyond convenience.

### Debtors

Debt narrows future choices. A debtor may owe silver, grain, labor, protection,
blood compensation, or the return of help once received during hunger.

Creditors exercise rule by deciding which failure counts as misfortune and
which as defiance.

Public remission can purchase loyalty. Public seizure can warn others. Quietly
extending a loan may create a deeper obligation than either.

### Refugees and Guests Who Cannot Leave

Hospitality assumes departure. Refugees make the guest relation endure beyond
its comfortable form.

Hosts begin by sharing. Then come questions of fuel, grazing, marriage,
sanitation, watch duty, worship, and whether newcomers may speak in assembly.

Refugees possess information, skills, kin ties, and grievances. Treating them
only as mouths wastes their capacities and makes predatory recruiters attractive.

### Prisoners and Captives

Captivity has political uses beyond punishment. Prisoners can be exchanged,
ransom can finance settlement, testimony can expose a superior, and decent
treatment can prepare peace.

Captives also require food, guard labor, shelter, and decisions about family
contact. A faction that takes more prisoners than it can maintain invites
starvation, disease, escape, or massacre.

The guard-prisoner relation creates knowledge and can produce shameful
intimacies. Both may understand that policy will soon demand an act neither
chooses alone.

### Outlaws and the Unseated

An outlaw lacks recognized protection, not human relations. Kin may feed him,
traders may use him, hidden shrines may bury his dead, and a rival faction may
grant conditional refuge.

People without a seat build portable politics: passwords, caches, shared routes,
debts remembered without writing, and harsh rules against conduct that would
turn every shelter into a target.

They can become a faction before acquiring a place.

### Camp Followers

Moving forces draw a settlement behind them. Camp followers repair and feed the
host. They trade and nurse, then carry or bury those who cannot march. Some
entertain or inform; others steal. Children are raised inside the host's shadow.

Commanders who deny their existence lose control of prices, waste, disease,
intelligence, and movement.

Camp followers may have no voice in war aims while bearing the same road,
hunger, and defeat.

### People with Stigmatized Bodies

Visible difference can be made into religious proof, entertainment, warning, or
an excuse for exclusion. Factions may recruit people marked as strange because
ordinary society left them few safer patrons.

Protection offered by a cruel institution can still be real to the protected
person. That fact explains loyalty without cleansing the institution.

A ruler who displays unusual bodies as signs, monsters, or chosen servants turns
persons into political language.

### Children

Children carry names, claims, grudges, foster bonds, and future marriages before
they understand them.

They also observe adult contradictions. A child sent from the room may still
know which guest made everyone quiet and which cupboard was opened afterward.

Political violence against a household reshapes the loyalties of people who
will be adults when present victors are old.

### The Right to Leave

The practical right to leave depends upon road safety, portable property,
welcome elsewhere, family agreement, health, season, and whether a lord regards
departure as lawful.

Freedom stated without those conditions may be ceremonial.

Ask what a person must abandon to refuse an order. That cost measures the order's
coercive weight.

## Feud, Peace, and the Uses of Restraint

### Feud as a Relationship

Feud is not continuous killing. It is a durable relation in which injury,
answer, and the authority to answer remain unsettled.

Periods of ordinary trade, shared worship, intermarriage, or mutual defense may
occur inside a feud. These contacts create both paths to peace and fresh points
of betrayal.

### The First Injury

Communities rarely agree where a feud began. Each side starts its account with
the last injury it regards as unprovoked.

Older causes may include disputed land, failed compensation, marriage insult,
abandoned protection, wrongful judgment, theft of a sacred object, or a killing
committed under an authority the other side never recognized.

Seeking the beginning can clarify structure while making settlement harder.

### Who May Answer

Feud law constrains legitimate targets. Kin, oath companions, officeholders,
property, herds, and named champions may stand within or outside the permitted
circle.

Violating these limits widens participation. Killing a messenger or child adds
a victim. It also tells neutral people that restraint offers no protection.

### Compensation

Compensation prices responsibility, not affection. No payment makes a dead
person equivalent to silver or cattle. It allows the injured side to accept a
publicly measurable substitute for further answer.

The sum must reflect status, dependence, intent, and the ability of the offender
to pay. An impossible demand is a declaration that peace is not presently
offered.

Payment by kin distributes guilt and gives the offender's household reason to
restrain future conduct.

### Sureties

A surety pledges property, standing, or body for another person's compliance.
This converts private trust into a public risk.

Good sureties can stop violence because both sides know a respected third party
will suffer from breach.

Bad sureties are named people with no power over the person they guarantee.

### Truce

A truce suspends selected acts for a time or purpose. Harvest, burial,
pilgrimage, market, marriage, monster defense, and negotiation may each justify
one.

Its terms should name place, duration, permitted weapons, routes, companions,
and the process for accidental contact.

Ambiguity invites the person who wants breach without blame.

### Sanctuary

Sanctuary moves a person temporarily under another authority. The shrine,
household, or settlement that grants it stakes Mandate upon preventing immediate
violence.

Sanctuary does not necessarily erase judgment. It creates time for judgment to
occur without pursuit deciding the case first.

Dragging a person from sanctuary attacks the protecting institution.

### Ritual Humiliation and Submission

Peace may require kneeling, surrendering a weapon, carrying a yoke, public
confession, bareheaded approach, or service at a grave.

Such acts work when they satisfy the injured side's need for acknowledged order
without making the submitting party socially dead.

Humiliation pursued past that point plants the next feud.

### Marriage across Feud

Marriage can create kin who have standing in both camps. It can also place a
spouse inside a hostile household under unbearable expectations of proof.

The union supports peace only if property, residence, children, worship, and the
right of return are negotiated honestly.

Calling an unwilling bride or groom a bridge disguises captivity as diplomacy.

### Shared Work

Repairing a bridge, clearing irrigation, hunting a dangerous beast, or burying
unclaimed dead creates bounded cooperation.

Shared work does not require reconciliation. It lets people accumulate evidence
that restraint is possible.

The tools, meal, and division of labor can matter more than a grand speech.

### The Peacemaker's Risk

Mediators hear admissions neither side wants public. They can be accused of
sympathy merely for understanding the enemy's claim.

A peacemaker needs protection from both parties and a future after the case.
Otherwise only the naive accept the role.

### Spoilers of Peace

Some people lose status, income, or protection when feud ends: raiders,
bodyguards, hardline priests, smugglers, ambitious cousins, and patrons who made
dependents through danger.

They need not oppose peace openly. A delayed message, missing animal, insulting
song, or conveniently unguarded meeting may suffice.

### Remembered Restraint

Communities remember the enemy who could have killed and did not. Such memory
does not erase grievance. It creates a usable precedent.

When designing a feud, include at least one act of restraint. It gives players
something to enlarge besides the body count.

## Occupation and Collaboration

### Occupation Begins with Routine

Occupation is made through keys, watches, food issues, curfews, quarters,
inventories, road papers, and the replacement or supervision of local judgment.

The banner over the hall matters. The person holding the mill key at dawn
matters sooner.

### Types of Occupation

A punitive occupation extracts payment and leaves after submission.

A strategic occupation controls a crossing, road, height, mine, shrine, or
storehouse needed for continuing war.

An annexing occupation intends to replace political identity and make temporary
command ordinary.

A protective occupation claims to shield residents from another enemy and may
be welcomed, feared, or both.

Each type creates different incentives for repair, local recruitment, and
departure.

### Quartering

Soldiers housed among civilians consume space, fuel, food, privacy, and sleep.
Even paid quartering changes household authority.

Hosts learn which soldier steals, which pays, which can carry a sick person,
which writes home, and which reports private speech.

Commanders prefer concentrated quarters for discipline. Dispersal feeds troops
and spreads resentment.

### Requisition

An inventory turns lived goods into military categories: grain, animal, cart,
cloth, iron, rope, labor.

Receipts may promise later payment. Their credibility depends upon whether any
prior receipt was honored and which authority will exist after the campaign.

Seizing breeding stock or seed attacks the next year. An occupier who intends to
remain must decide whether immediate need outweighs future revenue.

### Curfew and Movement

Curfew simplifies military watch by criminalizing ordinary necessity. Birth,
illness, animal trouble, fire, secret worship, courtship, and latrine use do not
respect the horn.

Permits create brokers. The clerk who can issue one acquires power over work,
family contact, and survival.

### Local Officers

Occupiers need residents who know boundaries, debts, stores, paths, and names.
They may retain existing elders, appoint rivals, elevate outsiders, or govern
through military interpreters.

Keeping old officers preserves competence and implicates them in the new rule.
Replacing them demonstrates change and destroys local knowledge.

### Collaboration as a Spectrum

Collaboration may mean ideological support, career ambition, survival, protection
of dependents, settlement negotiation, coerced labor, personal revenge, or the
belief that cooperation reduces harm.

These motives can coexist in one person.

Calling every accommodation treason makes occupation impossible to survive
without guilt. Calling every accommodation necessity erases deliberate cruelty.

### The Broker

A broker translates between occupier and occupied. He secures remissions,
passes names, arranges labor, warns families, selects targets, and becomes rich
or exhausted at the seam.

Residents judge results they can see. The occupier judges obedience. The broker
may satisfy neither.

### Occupation Courts

Military courts prioritize order, supply, and security. Local courts know
property, marriage, injury, and inherited obligation.

Parallel jurisdiction creates opportunities and traps. A litigant chooses the
court likely to favor him, then discovers the losing side regards the judgment
as foreign force.

### Sexual and Domestic Power

Occupation enters intimacy through coercion, bargaining, attachment,
prostitution, assault, marriage, and unequal access to food or protection.

Do not reduce every relation to one category. Do not use complexity to conceal
coercion where refusal carried hunger, punishment, or danger to kin.

Children born during occupation may become beloved family members and targets
of public shame at the same time.

### Religious Policy

An occupier may suppress rites, appropriate shrines, demand prayers for victory,
or tolerate worship in exchange for public loyalty.

Religious officers can mediate daily life while lending sacred appearance to
foreign command.

Martyrdom may strengthen resistance. Administrative interference often wounds
more quietly and more broadly.

### Collective Punishment

Fines, hostage-taking, demolition, grazing bans, and food seizure make a
community police itself.

Such punishment can produce short-term information. It also gives uninvolved
people a reason to desire the occupier's defeat.

A restrained occupier punishes named acts through stated process. An insecure
one punishes proximity.

### Leaving

Occupation ends unevenly. The main force departs while creditors, mixed
families, local recruits, graves, damaged works, and secret agreements remain.

Departure orders should address keys, prisoners, stores, records, weapons,
arrears, and protection for local servants. Most do not address all of them.

The vacuum between last patrol and accepted successor invites revenge.

### Accounting after Occupation

Communities ask who profited, who informed, who fed prisoners, who took a house,
who prevented a worse act, and who now claims to have resisted all along.

Evidence is partial. Everyone has a story shaped by fear and future need.

Justice must distinguish degrees without pretending complete knowledge.

## Resistance beneath Ordinary Work

### Refusal without a Banner

Resistance often begins as work done slowly, badly, elsewhere, or for someone
the ruler did not intend to benefit.

A cart axle fails. A grain count is honest in the wrong direction. A guide takes
the safe long road until pursuit is useless. A cook feeds a prisoner from the
bottom of the pot.

These acts remain politically ambiguous enough to survive inquiry.

### Concealment

Households hide grain and weapons. People disappear into other roofs;
books and icons go into walls. Letters and lineages may be entrusted farther
away.
Effective concealment uses ordinary architecture and habits rather than a single
clever compartment.

A secret known by every villager is protected through mutual dependence, not
ignorance.

### False Compliance

People attend the rite, repeat the words, display the required mark, and continue
another practice at home.

False compliance protects bodies while allowing authorities to report success.
It may endure for generations and change both the public and hidden traditions.

### Information Resistance

Residents can drown an authority in useless truth. Every direction is accurate
but irrelevant. Every name belongs to three people. Every incident is explained
through an old quarrel.

Silence attracts pressure. Excess detail exhausts it.

### Material Resistance

Tools disappear before requisition. Animals are moved to distant grazing. Seed
is mixed with inferior grain. Roads are allowed to deteriorate where patrols
need them most.

Material resistance carries collective cost. The broken bridge also delays a
midwife.

### Sanctuary Networks

Safe houses require food, waste removal, messages, medical care, and rules for
when a guest must move.

Networks fail when too many people know the whole route or when romantic ideas
of secrecy ignore the labor of keeping someone hidden.

### Songs, Jokes, and Names

Mockery reduces the ruler's control over meaning. A grand title becomes a farm
animal's name. An official hymn acquires a second verse. Children repeat what
adults can deny teaching.

Authorities may fear laughter because punishment confirms that the joke found
its mark.

### Ritual Resistance

Burial, fasting, forbidden feast, dress, gesture, and the refusal to swear can
preserve a community's moral boundary.

Ritual resistance is powerful where it is repeatable. One heroic act inspires.
A weekly custom organizes.

### Flight

Leaving denies labor, revenue, recruits, and the visible acceptance of rule. It
also abandons fields, graves, elders, and people unable to travel.

Organized flight needs destination, guides, stores, rear warning, and a story
that makes another settlement willing to receive the group.

### Armed Resistance

Weapons change the authority's response and the risks borne by unarmed
neighbors. Fighters need intelligence, shelter, food, medical care, replacement
equipment, and limits on what they will do to obtain them.

A resistance that extracts through fear can reproduce the rule it opposes.

### The Politics of Rescue

Outside adventurers may want to save a settlement whose residents have built a
careful accommodation. A dramatic attack can expose hidden people and destroy
months of quiet protection.

Ask residents what kind of help they can survive.

### Success

Resistance succeeds in degrees: one person escapes, a demand is reduced, a rite
survives, an officer is removed, a district becomes ungovernable, a rival grants
protection, or occupation ends.

Victory creates its own accounting. People who resisted differently must decide
whether they can live under one peace.

## Adventurers inside Political Life

### Why Factions Notice Them

Adventurers move armed, cross boundaries, enter ruins, possess rare knowledge,
accumulate treasure, survive violence, and form loyalties outside ordinary kin.
These traits make them useful and difficult to classify.

A faction wants to know who can answer for them.

### Hospitality as Assessment

The first feast, stable place, sleeping arrangement, and escort are tests. The
host observes appetite, manners, weapons, kin relations, religious signs, and
which adventurer speaks for the others.

Generosity creates a debt before any explicit request.

### Gifts

A political gift should be useful, visible, and difficult to accept without
meaning. A horse requires fodder. A cloak bears colors. A weapon belonged to a
dead retainer. A key implies custody.

Selling the gift may insult. Keeping it may identify allegiance.

### Employment

Factions hire adventurers for tasks requiring deniability, mobility, unusual
skill, or expendability.

The stated task may be accurate while omitting the political consequence. Escort
a witness, and one claimant loses the ability to deny her. Clear a ruin, and a
lord's border moves around the newly usable road.

### Traveling under Safe-Conduct

A safe-conduct is a faction's promise about its own agents, not a charm against
all danger. Rivals may honor it from custom, fear, reciprocity, or not at all.

The document or token reveals Reach. If distant guards recognize it, the
faction's name travels. If they laugh, the party has learned something valuable.

### Standing

Reputation is local and relational. Saving a village from a monster may impress
residents and anger the collector who used the threat to justify tribute.

Record standing with the people who interpret the deed, not with an abstract
world.

### Treasure as Political Material

Recovered wealth attracts claims from landholders, descendants, temples,
craftspeople, creditors, and people who died helping the expedition.

Spending changes local prices and dependencies. A chest of old silver can fund a
wall, hire every spare hand away from harvest, or make the party a faction before
it intended to become one.

### Strongholds

An adventurer stronghold establishes a seat. Neighbors then ask about roads,
grazing, fugitives, judgment, market access, monster defense, worship, and whose
claim the builders acknowledge.

The stronghold cannot remain merely private once other households depend upon
its mill, wall, healer, forge, or patrol.

### Neutrality

Neutrality requires practice. The party must refuse some gifts, apply known
rules, protect messengers, and accept that both sides will still test it.

Declaring neutrality after taking one side's food and road papers persuades few
people.

### Becoming Officers

Titles grant access and duties. A sheriff must hear unpleasant cases. A warden
must answer raids during poor weather. A court magician inherits blame for magic
he did not cast.

An office that adds status but changes no adventure remains decorative.

### Being Used as a Sign

Rulers display adventurers to imply alliances, blessings, monster mastery, or
access to distant wealth.

Attendance can communicate support even when the party says nothing. Refusal can
communicate hostility even when none was intended.

### Leaving Consequences

Adventurers depart after the decisive scene. Residents remain with broken doors,
new enemies, unpaid promises, exposed collaborators, and the story of what
happened.

Before closing a political adventure, name who must live with its result.

## Rendering Politics in Play

### Begin with a Demand

Introduce a faction through something it wants done: grain counted, a person
handed over, a road opened, a marriage witnessed, a shrine closed, a patrol
fed, or a corpse surrendered.

The demand makes authority visible and gives players a point of contact.

### Show the Chain

Place at least three people between decree and consequence: the superior who
wants an outcome, the officer who interprets it, and the resident who must
comply.

Each has room for judgment. Politics lives in those rooms.

### Give Every Power a Competence

Even a hated faction should do something its dependents genuinely value. It may
keep a dangerous road, maintain grain measures, punish predatory soldiers,
preserve records, or answer monsters.

Removing it then creates work rather than automatic relief.

### Give Every Power a Price

Protection consumes labor. Justice favors a social order. roads channel tolls.
Relief creates dependence. Sacred certainty excludes somebody.

Name who pays and when.

### Use Repeated Faces

Political scale becomes comprehensible through recurring people: one collector,
one ferryman, one household elder, one rival agent, one servant who carries the
unofficial truth.

Their changing conduct displays the faction's condition better than a lecture.

### Put Procedure on Stage

Councils, oath-taking, inventories, hostage exchange, compensation, charter
reading, levy selection, and burial are playable scenes.

Procedure creates positions for characters who are not the loudest speaker.
Someone can notice a false seal, comfort a fosterling, challenge a measure, or
remember an omitted witness.

### Make Material Facts Legible

Show wet grain, a tired remount, a repaired gate, smoke from a distant kiln, the
empty chair of a levy fighter, and the clerk's cracked fingers.

These details explain Pillars without naming numbers.

### Let People Hold Mixed Judgments

A villager may hate the tithe, trust the collector's weights, fear the faction's
fall, and shelter its enemy.

Contradiction grounded in experience is political understanding, not confusion.

### Keep Secrets Uneven

Decide who knows the fact, who suspects it, who benefits from disbelief, and
what evidence could travel.

A secret held by one villain is fragile. A secret supported by many ordinary
interests can structure a district.

### Advance Consequences by Season

Return after sowing, harvest, winter, or thaw. The same decree may become hunger
or a new office. Elsewhere it repairs a road and empties a house. A marriage
changes; a grievance remains.

Seasonal return gives political acts weight without forcing every consequence
into the next scene.

### Questions for a Settlement

When preparing local politics, answer these questions.

- Who is allowed to command a household, and who actually persuades it?
- Which protection is remembered as genuine?
- What payment was once reciprocal and now feels extracted?
- Who keeps the record that outsiders would seek?
- Which person connects this place to a distant faction?
- What necessity makes residents tolerate a hated officer?
- Which hidden practice would punishment strengthen rather than erase?
- What work stops first if conflict escalates?
- Who cannot safely leave?
- What act of restraint still makes peace imaginable?

### Questions for a Faction

When preparing a power, answer these questions.

- What does it do competently every season?
- Which households convert its orders into work?
- What does it promise in exchange for obedience?
- Which Pillar is being quietly consumed?
- What succession question is already changing conduct?
- Who benefits from emergency?
- Which dependent knows the institution's true weakness?
- Which rival can offer a credible alternative rather than mere destruction?
- What would victory require the faction to become?
- What obligation survives even if its ruler dies tonight?

### Political Anecdote: The Two Receipts

At a western grain village, a Rust collector issued a proper receipt for six
sacks and quietly took a seventh for his patrol. The elder kept both the marked
board and the memory of the unmarked sack.

Years later, a claimant promised to honor all unjust exactions. The marked six
were easy. The seventh required the testimony of people who had survived by
pretending it never existed.

The case was not about one sack. It asked whether the new power valued clean
accounts more than the dangerous truth beneath them.

### Political Anecdote: The Empty Watch

A clan chief ordered twelve households to keep a hill watch. Eleven sent people.
The twelfth had lost two adults during harvest and sent food instead.

The chief accepted the food for three seasons. A rival later called the household
cowardly and the chief weak.

To punish the household would deny the remembered mercy that made others love
the chief. To excuse it without explanation would let every rich house purchase
absence. Judgment required naming why this exception did not become a price.

### Political Anecdote: The Bridge Widow

A castle repaired a bridge with forced labor after its keeper died in a raid.
The keeper's widow continued collecting the old maintenance gift, though the
lord now claimed the toll.

Travelers paid twice because neither collection was safely refused. When the
adventurers were asked to settle the dispute, they found the widow feeding the
orphaned children of laborers killed in the repair.

Any clean answer moved hunger from one household to another.

### Political Anecdote: The Guest at the Lower Table

An orc envoy was welcomed to a human hall and seated below a young local lord.
The host believed age and office justified the order. The envoy saw a court
claiming alliance while displaying inherited contempt.

The translator could preserve the feast by explaining either custom. She could
not make both meanings disappear.

### Political Anecdote: The Last Key

During a disputed succession, every claimant held a seal. Only the old
storekeeper held the key to the winter grain.

She refused to open for any single claimant and issued food under the dead
ruler's previous household allowance. Armed supporters called this obstruction.
Servants called it the reason the seat survived long enough to choose.

### Closing Principle

Power in the Ravenlands is the repeated ability to turn words into burdens,
protection, movement, and memory. Titles explain why people ought to comply.
Institutions reveal how compliance is obtained. Households show what it costs.

Keep those three levels visible, and faction procedure will produce lived
politics rather than distant banners.
