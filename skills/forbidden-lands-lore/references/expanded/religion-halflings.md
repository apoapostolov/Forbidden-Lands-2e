# Halfling Religion of the Ravenlands

Halflings show Eor a public village face and keep her moon practice concealed
beneath it. Cradles and exchanged children shape marriage and childhood training.
Penita repentance reshapes hospitality; intoxicants and shame protect the lies
beneath it. Eor, the divided kin, and cradle customs are manuscript facts.
Halfling belief in the child-making power of a proper match is an established
campaign truth. Detailed rites are grounded setting inference.

---

## Eor

### Moon, Deception, and the Divided Kin

Eor is the moon goddess of lies, hidden meanings, jealousy, and shame. Her blue
moonstones gave rise to the beings later divided into halflings and goblins.
She turns her face away each month. Both halves worship her: goblins openly,
halflings behind domestic respectability.

Eor's faithful do not teach that every lie is holy. A useful lie reveals which
truths are dangerous, who claims the right to know, and what the speaker will
risk to preserve. Goblins praise deception that secures freedom, prey, or kin.
Halflings often bless the lie that keeps the cradle and the household facade
apart. Penita priests call both practices symptoms of a division that must end.

The moon's change gives the cult its moral grammar. No face is complete at all
times. What is hidden still shapes what is shown. Shame can protect a boundary,
but shame fed too long becomes cruelty toward the person who exposes it.

### Cradle Faith and Daily Practice

The hidden cradles are the strongest Eor institutions. Halfling and goblin
mothers, earth mothers, and shamans cooperate where public custom insists the
half-kin remain separate. Fathers guard outside and accept children without
certainty of descent. The rite makes social parenthood stronger than proof of
blood while concealing the fact from much of daily life.

Halfling households keep harmless-looking lunar customs: round loaves cut only
after moonrise, shutters closed during certain births, blue stones hidden in
garden walls, and a cup left under the table rather than on it. Goblins turn
similar acts outward with moon-painted masks, stolen silver, and sacrifices
made where the sky is visible.

An object taken by a goblin may be shown to Eor before use. The thief names who
believed they owned it and how the taking was done. A dull theft from someone
unable to resist brings little honor. A theft that exposes hoarding, false
security, or a broken bargain becomes a story.

### Rites and Ceremonies

**The Cradle Exchange** transfers newborns to mothers of their visible half-kin.
No public record names the biological mother. Witnesses remember health,
feeding, and obligations instead. The secrecy protects the children and gives
cradle keepers immense power over identity.

**The Two Faces** occurs at full and dark moon. At full moon, a person states a
public reputation they must maintain. At dark moon, before a smaller trusted
circle, they name what the reputation conceals. The second statement does not
automatically excuse the first.

**Eor's Misrule** permits a bounded night of exchanged clothing, false names,
borrowed offices, riddling judgment, and thefts that must be returned by dawn.
The rite exposes resentment without promising safety. A powerful halfling may
learn what the village says when the speaker's face is hidden.

**Penita Seeking** sends moon-priests after the lost blue stones. Before leaving,
a seeker names which present kinship they are willing to undo for restoration.
Many cannot answer. Goblins oppose the quest because reunion defined by Penita
may erase the lives the divided kin have built.

### Anathemas

Eor worship condemns betraying a cradle to outsiders, claiming certainty of
fatherhood to seize a child, using a protected confession from the dark-moon
rite as common gossip, and telling a lie that places all risk on someone who did
not consent to carry it.

Halfling orthodoxy may treat public admission of goblin kinship as dangerous
indecency. Goblins treat denial of a halfling in mortal danger as the worse
offense. Penita condemn deliberate destruction of a moonstone or refusal even
to consider reunion. The three positions cannot all govern the same crisis.

## The Public Face and the Hidden Moon

Halfling religion is organized around two audiences. Neighbors see a tidy
village, generous table, mild joke, clean path, and harmless lunar custom.
Cradle keepers, moon-priests, and trusted kin know that goblin children are born
to halfling mothers, halfling children to goblin mothers, and every respectable
line depends upon concealed exchange.

The public face protects settlements from suspicion and protects adults from
questions they have agreed not to answer. It also gives elders a weapon. A
person can be declared ill-mannered, dark-born, or unsafe for speaking about a
truth everyone relies upon.

Eor is approached through concealment because the halflings believe exposure
can destroy what truth alone cannot protect. Their central religious question
is jurisdiction: who has a right to know? The answer is seldom “everyone,” even
when secrecy enables harm.

## Religious Authority

Village hosts control public observance. They choose festival rooms, settle
table order, distribute gifts, and decide which embarrassment becomes a joke
rather than a charge. Their authority appears informal because hospitality is
its instrument.

Cradle keepers hold the harder power. They coordinate births and fosterage.
Health determines when travel is possible; exchanged infants make the route
sacred. The silence of fathers protects it. A keeper may know the living
connections between several villages and goblin bands. No ordinary elder can
replace that knowledge quickly.

Moon-priests interpret Eor's phases, protected lies, and rites of disclosure.
Penita priests form a visible or concealed reform movement. They seek the lost
moonstones and restoration of the divided kin, but disagree over whether
present halfling and goblin lives are wounds to heal or identities to erase.

## Daily Household Practice

The front room receives guests. The back room keeps what guests should not see.
The division becomes religious architecture. A household without a
private space may use a locked chest, false floor, neighboring burrow, or a
trusted tavern room.

Round bread belongs to the visible moon. A portion cut before serving may be
wrapped and placed below the table for the hidden half. Poor households cannot
waste bread for symbolism; someone eats it later, often a person whose work
must remain unmentioned.

Sweeping is both cleanliness and control of evidence. The person with the broom
decides which ash, hair, broken object, footprint, or spilled powder remains.
“Swept for Eor” can mean the room is ready, the quarrel is closed, or proof has
been destroyed.

Taverns function as neutral rooms because no household reveals its back room.
Deals, courtship, discipline, and religious argument happen over food someone
else prepared. The innkeeper learns who cannot safely visit whom.

## Intoxication and the Quaint Vice

Tobacco and ale are presented as comfort. Spirits and narcotic plants are
presented as hospitality or village charm. Under those names, dependence hides
in plain sight. A person who drinks at every feast can be called sociable until
stores, work, or children suffer.

Eor's **Soft Cup** allows a person to refuse alcohol without naming illness,
pregnancy, oath, or recovery. The host fills an opaque cup with water or thin
tea and treats it as ale. Revealing the substitution is a breach because the
lie carries no risk for anyone else.

The same custom can conceal poisoning or coercion. Responsible hosts keep one
sober pourer and mark the vessels by touch. A village that laughs at every
collapse has made intoxication into a screen for power.

## Birth, Cradle, and Fosterage

Pregnant women leave for a hidden cradle before birth. Fathers of both half-kin
guard outside and accept uncertainty. Inside, halfling earth mothers and goblin
shamans work together without the public hierarchy maintained elsewhere.

Children are assigned to mothers of the same visible half-kin. Health, milk,
existing losses, travel safety, and the number of waiting households influence
the exchange. It is not a simple trade of one child for another. A birth season
may leave one mother with no infant, another with twins, and several adults
bound to a secret they experience differently.

At **Closing the Cradle**, every departing mother names what she will tell and
what she will never claim to know. The keeper states the help owed if a child
later shows goblin traits, nocturnal habits, or a resemblance no one can explain
publicly.

Fosterage continues beyond infancy. A halfling child who cannot remain safely
in one village may move under the explanation of apprenticeship, sickness, or
inheritance. The cover story creates real obligations. A false aunt who raises
the child becomes an aunt in every matter that keeps the child alive.

## Marriage, Matchmaking, and the Desired Child

Halflings commonly believe that a properly chosen marriage guarantees the birth
of a halfling child. Matchmakers consider temper, health, and occupation. They
weigh household reputation against the conduct of grandparents. The lunar date
and the visible children born to related couples also enter the calculation.
The theories disagree, yet they share one promise: the right families can keep
goblin birth from their own cradle.

Cradle secrecy protects the promise from evidence. A goblin child is carried to
goblin kin, the public account records a loss or a different birth, and the next
matchmaker studies a lineage from which every contrary result has been removed.
Some cradle keepers understand this. Others believe the hidden birth proves that
one factor was overlooked. Both keep silent. To publish their knowledge would
expose living children and break the institution that moves them to safety.

### The Counting of Houses

Serious courtship begins with household histories rather than the wishes of the
prospective partners. Matchmakers keep private books of births and illnesses.
Broken engagements and debts explain failed negotiations. Fosterage is recorded
beside the supposed success of earlier unions. A family unable to show a book
may recite its account before witnesses.

Every halfling child strengthens the line. A goblin child leaves no mark except
an unexplained confinement, a paid journey, or a season in which the midwife
refuses questions.

At **The Counting of Houses**, the two families lay out tokens for the children
they publicly claim. They then bargain over residence, stores, apprenticeship,
care of parents, cradle travel, and the standing of future children. The young
couple may speak, but a refusal can be described as waste of a good joining and
an injury to people not yet born.

A skilled matchmaker can stop a feud by binding two houses. The same keeper can
invent a hereditary taint, suppress a goblin birth in one line, or reveal one in
another. A few become richer than village hosts while continuing to call their
work neighborly concern.

### Childhood under the Marriage Table

Ambitious parents begin preparing a child long before courtship. Tutors teach
figures, letters, music, table service, family memory, a profitable trade, and
the exact answers favored by desirable households. Food, posture, friendships,
clothes, sleep, and harmless mistakes become evidence for or against the future
match. A child may reach adulthood able to host twelve guests and unable to
choose where to walk without permission.

Play is permitted when it teaches grace, bargaining, memory, or skill. A fall,
scar, rumor, private friendship, or evening spent beyond the known path can
reduce a family's claims. Parents who believe a poor choice may produce a goblin
call surveillance protection. They hire better tutors, forbid rough work, and
arrange rehearsed meetings with families they hope to impress. The expense can
consume a modest household's seed grain.

Children learn the stakes without being told the cradle truth. They hear that a
careless appetite can shame grandchildren, that an unsuitable affection can
darken the moon over a house, or that one public outburst spoiled an elder
sibling's prospects. Affection becomes another lesson to perform. Siblings may
be ranked by marriage promise before either is old enough to understand why.

### Ambition, Failure, and Blame

Marriage rivalry is fought through invitation, apprenticeship, gossip, debt,
and the control of birth accounts. A household may ruin a rival by spreading a
story of night wandering or goblin resemblance. Wealthier parents buy a scarce
tutor's entire season so no competitor can learn from them. Engagements are
broken after one family has already paid for clothes, gifts, and repairs to the
future room.

Where land, office, or an exceptionally favored line is at stake, the struggle
can become bloody. A witness takes a dangerous road alone. A rival eats from the
wrong dish. A birth keeper is threatened until her memory improves. Such acts
are condemned in public because open violence would spoil every household
involved. In private, the surviving family may still enjoy the match.

When a goblin child is born, the doctrine protects itself by locating fault.
One partner concealed a habit. A grandmother lied about a cousin. The moon was
counted from the wrong night. The bride ate forbidden food, the groom kept bad
company, or a rival placed a goblin token beneath the bed. The child disappears
into the cradle network while blame remains in the halfling house.

Parents often turn that blame upon an older child. Years of tutoring become
proof of sacrifice, and sacrifice becomes a debt no child can repay except by
accepting the next approved union. Some flee. Some make excellent marriages and
raise their own children under the same watch. Some seek Penita because reunion
promises an end to the birth that no match can control.

### Doctrine and Dissent

Village orthodoxy calls a well-matched household a truthful lie: two public
lives arranged so carefully that Eor need not show the hidden face. Matchmakers
quote successful lines as proof and treat cradle keepers' silence as sacred
confirmation.

Dark-moon households answer that Eor divided the kin before any living marriage
was made. They may still arrange matches for property and peace, but refuse the
claim that a child's visible half rewards parental virtue. Their children are
often judged unsuitable, which proves how quickly theology becomes a tool of
the marriage table.

Penita priests use the contradiction as an argument for reunion. If the blue
stones are restored, they say, no mother will bargain against half her own kin.
Opponents answer that Penita would cure coercive marriage by dissolving both the
halfling and the goblin lives built around the cradle.

## Naming and Adult Standing

A public name presents good temper, household relation, trade, or an admired
ancestor. A moon-name is known inside the family or cradle network and may
record the circumstances of exchange. Some adults never learn it.

At **Keeping a Room**, an adolescent hosts a meal with one expected thing
missing. The guests must decide whether to ask. The candidate must decide when
concealment protects the gathering and when failure to speak becomes fraud.
The rite tests food, hospitality, and judgment together.

Adult standing includes keeping another person's harmless secret without using
it as social currency. A candidate who knows everything and cannot stop hinting
at it is considered unsafe.

## Partnership and Household Truth

Courtship takes place in public rooms. Partnership requires entry into the back
room. Each person discloses debts, dependents, substances kept, cradle duties,
and which truths cannot be shared with either family.

At **The Closed Shutter**, partners sit through one moonrise without opening the
room to interruption. They tell one truth that must change household practice
and one matter that remains private. A protected silence cannot cover violence,
stolen stores, or danger to a child. Halfling communities argue constantly
about that boundary.

Partnership with another kin can threaten cradle secrecy. Some villages accept
the outsider through an oath. Others demand permanent ignorance. A halfling
who loves a human may be forced to choose between honest marriage and the
network that protects every local birth.

## Death and the Two Accounts

The public funeral names work, humor, hospitality, and visible family. A smaller
night gathering names fosterage, goblin kin, hidden aid, addiction, exclusion,
and injuries the public face concealed.

At **Turning the Plate**, the dead person's place is set once with its clean
side upward and once with the worn underside upward. Claims unresolved in the
second account determine who receives hidden objects and who must warn the
cradle network.

A respectable burial can be false without being empty. The public
account may protect living children. It may also protect an abuser whose victims
are told that speaking would endanger the cradle. Eor offers no easy rule that
makes secrecy innocent.

## Halfling Readings of Eor

### Village Orthodoxy

Orthodox villages teach that Eor's shame protects the divided kin from repeating
their first deceit. Halflings should show the mild face, keep disorder inside,
and prevent outsiders from using the old punishment against them. The doctrine
maintains peace and traps people inside reputations they cannot survive breaking.

### Penita

Penita call concealment a penance that became an idol. They seek the blue
moonstones and reunion. Before a search, the priest must name who controls the
restored body, whose village disappears, and whether goblins consent. Priests
who refuse these questions want purity more than kin.

### Dark-Moon Households

Some households honor the goblin half openly inside the family. They teach
tracking, night travel, theft stories, and forest signs. Neighbors often use
them for unpleasant work while questioning their respectability.

## Relations with Goblins

Halflings depend on goblins at the cradle and deny them at market. Goblins may
watch roads, return stolen children, punish a threat, or observe a halfling
family for years without receiving acknowledgment.

A halfling who accepts goblin aid publicly risks exposing the shared kinship. A
person who refuses it may die for a facade. This is one of the religion's
hardest tests because either choice can endanger other people.

Goods passed between the half-kin often move through staged theft. The goblin
“steals” medicine left under a marked bush. A halfling “finds” meat at the edge
of a field. The fiction preserves separation and gives both parties deniability.

## The Village Year and the Moon behind It

Public festivals follow field, pantry, marriage season, and market. Hidden
observance follows moon, cradle travel, and the nights when goblin kin can reach
the village without crossing a lit road. The two calendars are kept by different
people and made to look accidental when they meet.

At **Setting the Long Table**, households contribute food in proportion to what
neighbors believe they possess. A family with concealed debt may borrow simply
to avoid appearing poor. The host seats guests by public standing while leaving
one place near the service door for whoever carries news from the cradle road.

Spring cleaning includes **Opening the Back Room**. Only household members and
one trusted witness enter. Stores are counted, hidden bottles found, birth tokens
checked, and objects belonging to fostered children separated from common
inheritance. The door opens after every dangerous item has acquired a keeper.

On the darkest autumn moon, respectable homes extinguish the front-room lamp
early. Food wrapped beneath the table is carried to a wall gap, garden mark, or
forest edge. Most villagers say it feeds foxes. Goblin relatives know which
parcel contains salt, medicine, or a warning rather than leftovers.

Winter weddings are favored because stores, bedding, and family temper can be
inspected under pressure. A match that looks excellent during harvest may fail
when both households must share one smoky room. Matchmakers call this prudence
while arranging enough winter visits to leave the young couple no privacy.

## Charity, Debt, and the Pleasant Table

Halfling charity is delivered as hospitality whenever possible. A hungry person
is called a guest. An indebted cousin is asked to help with supper. A child from
an unsafe home becomes an apprentice who happens to sleep upstairs. The fiction
protects standing and makes aid easier to withdraw without public judgment.

At **The Covered Basket**, food, fuel, medicine, or coin passes through an
intermediary who removes the giver's mark. The recipient owes no declared thanks.
The intermediary remembers enough to stop several wealthy households from
claiming the same gift.

Aid can become possession. Hosts decide dress, speech, work, courtship, and which
version of the recipient's need may be told. A person kept alive for years can
be introduced as proof of generosity while never being allowed to leave with
stores of their own.

Village debt is often recorded as meals and favors before coin. “We kept your
mother's table” may refer to three weeks of food and be used to claim a marriage
vote thirty years later. Wyrm keepers sometimes force the household to state a
term. Eor traditionalists prefer a debt whose exact edge remains hidden.

Taking food from a public feast is permitted. Hoarding it while a named guest
remains hungry is not. The distinction becomes bitter when a family hides food
for goblin kin whom the public table refuses to recognize.

## Judgment, Gossip, and the Cost of a Good Name

Halfling judgment begins before any hearing. Invitations stop. A chair is placed
farther from the hearth. Credit becomes cash. Children are called home when a
particular adult approaches. By the time elders speak, the village may have
already punished an entire household.

At **The Two Tellings**, accuser and accused each describe the event once in the
front room and once before a smaller dark-moon circle. The first account protects
public standing. The second admits facts that cannot safely be repeated. A
keeper then decides which consequences can be justified without revealing the
protected truth.

The procedure can protect a cradle and leave an innocent person publicly blamed.
It can also let an offender invent a sacred secret no one may challenge. Some
villages require one witness who holds no marriage tie to either house. Finding
such a person in a small settlement is its own judgment.

Gossip is not dismissed as empty speech. It moves marriage, credit, access to
children, and the willingness to open a door at night. A corrected story must
travel through the same kitchens and taverns as the first. A notice nailed to a
hall does not reach the people who made the punishment real.

Eor priests distinguish a concealed truth from a planted falsehood. The first
limits access. The second recruits other people into harming someone. Wealthy
families prefer not to recognize the difference when rumor works in their favor.

## Old Age, Dependency, and the Hidden Burrow

The public village praises grandparents, recipes, family tales, and the wisdom
of a long table. Actual age can bring confusion, shaking hands, anger, wandering,
and work no household wants guests to see.

Some elders move into back rooms or concealed burrows with disfigured people,
unwanted dependents, and those whose habits disturb the facade. The place may be
warm, well provisioned, and full of company. It may also be a prison beneath a
house that tells visitors the elder is resting elsewhere.

At **Passing the Key**, an aging householder gives another person access to
stores, marriage books, hidden drink, cradle tokens, and the back door. Two
witnesses inspect what was transferred. Without the rite, an heir can claim the
elder lost judgment whenever an inconvenient promise appears.

Care rotates by meal or night among households that share descent. Rich families
pay poorer kin and call the labor affection. A caregiver who reveals neglect may
be accused of breaking household confidence. Eor's protected lie can preserve
dignity or keep a bruised elder out of sight.

The dead person's hidden-room companions have standing at the night funeral even
when no public kinship is admitted. Excluding them lets the clean house inherit
objects, debts, and memories it refused to tend.

## Penita Houses and the Labor of Reunion

Penita priests search for stones while maintaining lodging, maps, copied
accounts, goblin contacts, and routes between cradles. Someone must bake, mend,
translate, and answer the families endangered by each expedition.

Before **Opening the Blue Road**, a seeker deposits a personal moon token and
names who may stop the journey if a cradle is threatened. The right to stop is
given to someone staying home. Otherwise every risk can be justified by the
stone supposedly waiting ahead.

Penita houses collect testimony from halflings and goblins who desire reunion,
fear it, or reject it. The accounts are kept separately from marriage books.
Orthodox elders sometimes steal them to identify dissent. Goblin bands sometimes
burn them to prevent any priest from counting consent that may later be used
against the kin.

The sect divides over the restored being. One school expects a new person who
inherits both histories and owes protection to both kin. Another speaks as if
the original kin has merely been sleeping and present lives are errors. A third
would keep the stones apart until every living cradle network agrees, a condition
that may never be met.

Failure changes seekers. A priest may return with no stone, a dead companion,
and knowledge of a goblin family their village denies. Orthodoxy calls the
journey proof of dangerous obsession. Penita asks whether the returned knowledge
creates a duty even when the relic was false.

## Relations with Other Faiths

Raven worship understands public promise and distrusts the hidden witness. A
Raven Sister may attend a dangerous birth and accept a false public account to
protect the infant. A Raven judge later asked to settle inheritance may refuse
to honor the same concealment.

Wyrm keepers fit halfling concern with continuity, record, and domestic claim.
Their written archives threaten a society whose safest truths are divided among
people. A Wyrm priest who copies a cradle account has preserved it and made it
stealable.

Flow governs wells, washing, brewing, and the clean vessels respectable homes
display. Poison hidden in a shared cup violates both hospitality and Flow. A
village may still blame a troublesome servant rather than expose the marriage
plot behind it.

Clay appeals to gardeners, potters, midwives, and households living with a form
that public doctrine calls wrong. Dark-moon believers use unfired figures for
children sent through the cradle. They dissolve the figure only after news of
safe fosterage arrives.

Rust Brothers admire orderly villages because account books, host authority,
and fear of scandal make control cheap. They condemn Eor while recruiting
matchmakers and innkeepers as informants. A halfling elder may cooperate to
protect the village and discover that every protected secret is now tribute.

## The Blood Mist and the Closed Village

The Blood Mist rewarded the public face. Outsiders could not arrive after dark,
birth journeys needed careful timing, and each village could maintain its own
account without comparison. A respectable line accumulated generations of
proof because contrary witnesses lived beyond a lethal road.

Cradle travel became more dangerous and more sacred. Mothers left earlier,
stayed longer, and sometimes returned with a child already several weeks old.
Explanations of fever, difficult labor, or care by a distant aunt grew elaborate
enough to become family history.

The Mist also trapped people inside abusive homes. A person unable to cross the
road before dusk could not seek another village, goblin kin, or an order willing
to contradict local elders. Hospitality became compulsory dependence.

After the Mist lifted, marriage books met one another. Dates failed to match.
Two villages claimed the same foster aunt. Goblins appeared with names known
only from back-room stories. Some elders burned records before the first open
road market.

At **Receiving the Far Guest**, a village hosts someone whose relation was
maintained only through secret parcels. The guest is introduced by a harmless
public connection. At night, the household decides whether the old truth may
enter the front room. The lifted Mist opened distance faster than custom opened
speech.

## Shrines, Moonstones, and Dangerous Keepsakes

Halfling shrines are built into useful domestic things: the bread shelf, well
cover, garden wall, lintel, sewing box, cellar step, or cupboard used for guest
cups. A visitor sees good keeping. The household knows which scratch, blue chip,
or hidden hollow belongs to Eor.

At **Cleaning the Face**, the visible object is washed and repaired while the
concealed token is inspected separately. Damage to the public object may be
ordinary wear. Damage found only inside can mean intrusion by someone who knew
where to look.

Supposed moonstones attract Penita, goblins, collectors, thieves, and elders
afraid of what reunion would expose. Most blue stones are glass, shell, mineral,
or family fraud. A false relic can still have carried marriages and pilgrimages
for generations.

At **Testing the Blue**, no magical trial is accepted alone. The keepers ask
where the stone came from, who paid for the journey, which cradle was endangered,
and who gains authority if it is declared genuine. A miracle without custody is
a quarrel with light around it.

Destroying a false relic may ruin people who organized life around it. Preserving
it without disclosing the test makes a known lie recruit new believers. Penita
houses divide over which harm Eor requires them to carry.

## Magic, Omens, and the Convenient Hidden Meaning

Halflings read coincidence through household consequence. A broken plate before
a wedding matters because someone must replace it, explain it, and decide whether
the crack reveals concealed anger. The omen begins in work.

Moon-priests use dreams, phases, blue light, protected speech, and sometimes
sorcery. At **Telling after Breakfast**, a dream is not interpreted until the
sleeper has eaten and heard what occurred in the house overnight. Hunger, drink,
smoke, and overheard quarrel are allowed to explain it before Eor does.

A prophecy that flatters one marriage line is entered in the matchmaker's book
with the priest's payment beside it. Families often remember the omen and forget
who bought the feast at which it was spoken.

Magic used to conceal a room, birth, addiction, or fugitive protects life and
can preserve abuse. Dark-moon teachers ask who may end the concealment. A spell
with no person authorized to lift it turns emergency secrecy into permanent
rule.

Demons bargain through shame with unusual ease. They offer the perfect child,
lost moonstone, clean family line, or secret erased from every witness. Eor's
faithful know that removing the witness does not remove what the household did.

## Fault, Repair, and Anathema

Ordinary faults are repaired through replacement, hospitality, a corrected
story told to everyone who relied on the first, or private admission before the
same witnesses who kept the secret.

Grave breaches include betraying a cradle, claiming biological certainty to
seize a child, exposing protected kinship for profit, hiding danger to a child
under household privacy, forcing intoxication while calling refusal impolite,
falsifying a birth account to compel a marriage, punishing a child for the
visible half of a later newborn, and making another person carry every
consequence of one's lie.

A protected lie expires when its burden changes. The person who told it must
reassess who now pays. Halfling elders dislike this principle because old lies
support property, marriage, and standing across entire villages.

## Evidence at the Table

Show halfling worship through a moon token mortared inside a garden wall, a
cup under rather than on the table, a birth journey explained too carefully,
an inn room used by people who never visit one another's homes, or a goblin
warning dismissed in public and obeyed after dark. A marriage book with three
seasons missing, a child reciting table answers while other children play, or a
tutor paid to leave before a rival's lessons begin reveals the domestic cost.

Religious disputes should concern who may know a birth, whether a protected lie
still protects anyone, what Penita restoration would destroy, or which person
has been hidden so the village may keep calling itself pleasant. Marriage adds
another question: who is being asked to surrender a life so that a household
can claim control over the face of its next child?
