# Sandbox and Freeform Campaign Design

Use this reference to create material that remains useful when players ignore
the expected route. A sandbox is not a map without a plot. It is a network of
places, actors, resources, dangers, and incomplete information that changes in
response to player choices.

## Core Principle

Prepare situations with momentum, not scenes with an order.

A playable situation contains:

- something valuable, threatened, or changing
- at least two actors who want incompatible outcomes
- constraints that prevent an easy settlement
- signs the players can notice
- several ways to intervene
- consequences if the players act, fail, bargain, or leave

The GM should know what happens without the adventurers, not what the
adventurers must do.

## The Campaign Engine

Build six connected layers:

1. **Land:** routes, seasons, terrain, and travel costs shape access.
2. **Places:** settlements and sites offer needs, tools, dangers, and leverage.
3. **Actors:** people and factions pursue aims with limited means.
4. **Pressures:** hunger, weather, law, war, monsters, faith, and debt force
   movement.
5. **Signals:** rumors, prices, refugees, tracks, patrols, rites, and absences
   reveal change.
6. **Consequences:** choices alter safety, allegiance, scarcity, knowledge, and
   future opportunities.

If a layer does not affect another layer, it is background rather than a
campaign engine.

## Campaign Scale

### Hex or route

Give each traveled area:

- one reason to enter
- one reason to avoid or prepare
- one sign of a larger pressure
- one local resource or relationship
- one change that can occur over time

Not every hex needs an encounter. Travel choices need differences in time,
exposure, provisions, information, and destination state.

### Settlement

A useful settlement has:

- a material need
- a source of status or authority
- a local custom that changes behavior
- three interests that cannot all be satisfied
- someone who benefits from the current problem
- one resource unavailable elsewhere
- consequences for violence, theft, broken oaths, and generosity

Do not make the settlement a quest kiosk. Let requests emerge from interests and
let the adventurers propose their own bargains.

### Adventure site

Build a site as a stateful place:

- at least three entrances, approaches, or access methods
- occupants with routines and reactions
- hazards that can be observed or manipulated
- rewards with different owners and meanings
- information that changes decisions elsewhere
- a state change if disturbed and abandoned

Avoid rooms whose only function is combat. Each important area should offer
information, leverage, passage, danger, or transformation.

### Region

A region needs:

- a central pressure visible in ordinary life
- several competing explanations or responses
- movement of people, goods, monsters, and news
- a reason distant locations affect one another
- a change horizon if no one intervenes

Do not require every location to serve one metaplot.

## Factions as Playable Actors

Define each faction with:

- **aim:** the change it pursues
- **need:** what it lacks
- **assets:** people, places, knowledge, legitimacy, and force
- **constraints:** oaths, shortages, rivals, taboos, geography
- **method:** what it normally does
- **tell:** what players observe when it acts
- **fault line:** internal disagreement
- **offer:** what it can credibly give the adventurers
- **red line:** what causes retaliation or rupture

Faction action should consume assets, expose methods, or create new enemies.
Never advance an invisible clock without placing evidence in the world.

### Pressure tracks

Use a track only when stages create distinct fictional states. Write each stage
as:

`action → visible sign → changed opportunity → new risk`

Advance because time passes, a faction succeeds, the players expose weakness,
or a resource changes hands. Do not use tracks to force a scheduled climax.

## Rumors and Information

A rumor should help a player choose a destination, preparation, ally, or risk.
Write:

- a concrete claim
- a source with interests and limits
- one true element
- one uncertain, mistaken, or biased element
- a way to investigate

Use rumor clusters. Three independent clues should support any fact required for
meaningful progress. They need not repeat the same words or require the same
skill.

### Information states

Track information as:

- **unknown:** no actionable sign
- **suspected:** enough to investigate or prepare broadly
- **actionable:** enough to make a targeted choice
- **proven:** confidence earned through evidence or direct experience

Failed rolls should change cost, timing, exposure, or confidence. Do not make a
single failed roll erase the only path to necessary information.

## Hooks Without Rails

A strong hook states:

- what is happening
- why it matters now
- what can be gained or protected
- who is already acting
- what remains unknown

It does not prescribe a solution.

Offer hooks through changes the characters can notice: an empty market stall,
an altered toll, a funeral without a body, armed shepherds, a shrine with fresh
iron nails, or a road suddenly busy in the wrong direction.

## Consequence Design

Consequences should change one or more:

- access
- attitude
- allegiance
- price or scarcity
- route safety
- legal status
- time pressure
- knowledge
- ownership
- ecological state
- faction capacity

Use proportional, connected consequences. A broken oath damages the
relationships and institutions that depended on it; it does not summon unrelated
punishment.

### Failure that keeps moving

On failure, choose a change:

- success is delayed and another actor moves
- the goal is achieved at a cost
- the party gains incomplete or compromised leverage
- danger becomes immediate
- a relationship worsens
- a resource is depleted
- the location changes before another attempt
- a new choice replaces the blocked approach

Do not guarantee success. Guarantee only that the situation responds.

## Emergent Story

Story emerges when players remember:

- what they wanted
- what they risked
- who opposed or helped them
- what changed because of the choice
- what unfinished consequence follows

After each session, update:

1. Which goals advanced or changed?
2. Which actors learned something?
3. Which assets moved or were lost?
4. Which promises, injuries, debts, and grudges persist?
5. Which visible signs appear next?
6. Which two or three opportunities are now reachable?

Recurring consequences create arcs without prewriting endings.

## Freeform GM Procedure

When players attempt something unprepared:

1. Clarify intent and desired effect.
2. Establish fictional position: access, tools, time, opposition, and danger.
3. Decide whether the outcome is certain, impossible as stated, or uncertain.
4. If uncertain, state the risk and relevant procedure before commitment.
5. Resolve once at the level of the meaningful question.
6. Change the situation.
7. Show the change through concrete fiction.

Do not call for a roll when ordinary competence and adequate time settle the
question. Do not split one intent into many rolls unless each introduces a new
decision or danger.

### Adjudication hierarchy

Prefer:

1. established manuscript rule
2. close existing procedure
3. simple skill roll with explicit stakes
4. temporary ruling recorded for later review

Do not create a permanent subsystem during live play.

## Player-Authored Direction

At the end of a session ask:

- What are you trying to accomplish next?
- Which rumor or relationship matters?
- What are you willing to risk?
- What preparation do you want to make?

Prepare the consequences and opposition around stated intent. Keep unrelated
actors moving lightly so the world remains alive without invalidating plans.

Support character goals with world resistance, not by replacing them with a
more important GM plot.

## Balance in an Open World

Do not balance every encounter to the party. Instead:

- telegraph relative danger
- provide retreat, negotiation, stealth, research, and diversion options
- let preparation materially change odds
- make powerful threats geographically or behaviorally legible
- reward information and restraint
- distinguish dangerous from unavoidable

Players can choose unequal challenges when they can assess and manage the risk.

## Prep Economy

Prioritize material with high reuse:

- reaction procedures
- faction sheets
- rumor tables
- encounter motives
- site states
- names, goods, prices, and physical details
- consequence menus

Avoid long hidden histories that do not alter present choices. Convert history
into claims, ruins, customs, rights, taboos, and conflicts.

## Sandbox Failure Modes

- **Static world:** nothing changes without the party.
- **Quantum content:** every road leads to the same prepared scene.
- **Plot armor:** key actors cannot be defeated, persuaded, or ignored.
- **Hook flood:** many rumors, none with enough information to choose.
- **Punitive autonomy:** declining a hook produces arbitrary disaster.
- **Opaque danger:** threats are lethal but cannot be assessed.
- **One-skill world:** one character's specialty controls all navigation,
  information, or diplomacy.
- **Consequence inflation:** every choice decides the fate of the region.
- **Prep exhibition:** content exists to be shown rather than interacted with.

## Quality Gate

- [ ] The situation develops without a scripted player route.
- [ ] At least two actors have incompatible aims and real constraints.
- [ ] Players can observe pressure before suffering its full consequence.
- [ ] Every essential fact has multiple clue paths.
- [ ] Sites support several approaches and change when disturbed.
- [ ] Failure produces a changed situation and another decision.
- [ ] Faction action leaves visible evidence.
- [ ] Travel and logistics alter strategic choices.
- [ ] Consequences connect to the action that caused them.
- [ ] The material helps the GM answer unplanned choices quickly.
