# Player Psychology and Table Experience

Use this reference to predict how a rule will feel, what behavior it will teach,
and which players will pay its hidden costs. It complements probability and
synergy analysis; it does not replace them.

## The Experience Model

Evaluate the rule through this chain:

`promise → information → choice → commitment → uncertainty → outcome → attribution → learning`

- **Promise:** What does the player believe this option lets them be or do?
- **Information:** What can they know before committing?
- **Choice:** Which alternatives are credible?
- **Commitment:** What do they risk or forgo?
- **Uncertainty:** What remains unresolved after good judgment?
- **Outcome:** What changes in the fiction and rules?
- **Attribution:** Can they tell why the outcome happened?
- **Learning:** Can they make a better or different decision next time?

The chain breaks when an evocative promise leads to a trivial bonus, when the
important cost is hidden, or when the outcome cannot be traced back to a choice.

## Seven Player Needs

### Agency

Players need their decisions to alter the situation. Agency is not unlimited
control and does not require success. A costly retreat, a failed bargain that
reveals a feud, or an injury that changes the expedition can preserve agency.

Audit:

- Could a different reasonable choice have changed the outcome?
- Did the player have useful information before committing?
- After failure, can the player still pursue goals?
- Does the rule invite decisions or merely demand compliance?

### Competence

Investment should create dependable advantage and recognizable identity without
removing uncertainty. Competence is felt when a specialist can attempt more,
risk less, interpret better, or solve a problem in a distinctive way.

Avoid making expertise:

- a tiny modifier the player rarely notices
- automatic success that eliminates play
- permission to act while everyone else is forbidden
- universal breadth that absorbs several niches

### Fairness

Players judge procedural fairness more strongly than equal outcomes. They accept
severe consequences when warnings, rules, and causal links are consistent.

Fair danger is:

- signaled before commitment
- resolved by a stable procedure
- proportionate to what could be known
- followed by an intelligible consequence

Randomness feels unfair when the player could neither anticipate nor mitigate
it, especially when it removes a character from play for a long period.

### Discovery

Discovery requires a gap between clue and answer. Give concrete evidence,
conflicting testimony, physical traces, and behavior patterns. Let players form
and test theories.

Three useful information levels:

1. **Invitation:** enough to know that something is worth investigating.
2. **Leverage:** enough to choose a preparation or approach.
3. **Confirmation:** enough to act with confidence after earning it.

Do not disguise required information behind one roll or one NPC.

### Expression

Players express identity through what they choose to risk, protect, master, and
refuse. Support multiple effective postures: force, caution, cunning, endurance,
care, status, craft, occult knowledge, negotiation, and retreat.

An option damages expression when it is so efficient that informed players feel
foolish for choosing the more flavorful alternative.

### Belonging

Cooperation should create reciprocal dependence, not permanent hierarchy.
Command, healing, crafting, scouting, and lore support should produce moments in
which the supporting player makes decisions and receives visible credit.

Ask:

- Who rolls?
- Who chooses the target or timing?
- Who receives narrative recognition?
- Who bears the cost if it fails?

If one character chooses and shines while another pays, resentment is likely.

### Spotlight

Spotlight includes time, decision authority, fictional importance, and unique
problem ownership. A mechanically balanced option can still be unhealthy when
it requires repeated solo procedures or settles every important question.

Treat spotlight as a session budget. High spotlight is acceptable for a climax
or rare specialty; repeated automatic priority is not.

## Choice Quality

A real choice needs:

- two credible options
- different benefits and failure modes
- a reason the answer changes by circumstance
- a consequence that persists long enough to matter
- adequate information, including visible uncertainty

### False choices

Common false choices include:

- pay a negligible cost or suffer disaster
- take the numerically superior talent or knowingly fall behind
- push when failure blocks the only route forward
- accept the GM's planned hook or receive no playable content
- choose among cosmetic outcomes with identical state changes

Repair false choices by changing costs, adding alternate goals, revealing more
information, or making consequences qualitatively different.

### Decision fatigue

Choices lose value when repeated too often. Use a decision only if context can
change the answer. Otherwise establish a stance, loadout, camp posture, marching
order, or standing instruction once and reuse it until circumstances change.

## Loss, Risk, and Harsh Play

Loss aversion means players feel losses more strongly than equivalent gains.
Forbidden Lands uses this deliberately through pushing, resource depletion, and
injury. Make the bargain legible.

Good risk offers:

- a visible prize
- a known or inferable danger
- a way to reduce or redirect danger
- ownership of the final decision

Bad risk reveals the real cost only after the player commits. Do not use
unannounced permanent loss as a routine surprise.

### Agency loss

Stun, paralysis, Broken states, captivity, maiming, and exile can be powerful.
Measure them in lost meaningful turns, not fictional duration alone.

Mitigations include:

- a choice among consequences
- an active escape or recovery procedure
- a new role during incapacity
- scene-level rather than session-level duration
- a visible route to adaptation, retirement, or transformed play

## Push Psychology

Pushing works because it is an owned escalation: the player chooses to expose
attributes and gear for another chance. Preserve that emotional contract.

Do not:

- make pushing mandatory for ordinary competence
- hide a special penalty until after the push
- refund all push risk through an automatic loop
- punish refusal so severely that the choice is fake
- add so many reroll variants that the core decision becomes opaque

New push interactions should deepen one of three questions: how badly is this
needed, what can I afford to damage, or what new danger am I willing to invite?

## Advancement and Identity

Advancement choices carry strong regret risk because they are persistent and
infrequent. State prerequisites, exclusions, use frequency, and rank trajectory
clearly.

For rare identities:

- keep common paths broadly available
- signal the special path before the gate closes
- make access rare but not arbitrary
- let acceptance express a character decision
- avoid requiring players to know unpublished optimization facts

Lifepaths should create interesting unevenness, not hidden winners. Rare results
may grant unusual access; they should not create a character who is broadly
superior before play begins.

## Onboarding and Cognitive Load

Teach in this order:

1. What is happening?
2. What can I choose?
3. What do I roll or spend?
4. What can happen?
5. What exception matters now?

Do not lead with edge cases. Put the default procedure before modifiers and
exceptions. Use one worked example when timing, ownership, or state transitions
are not obvious.

Track:

- new terms
- simultaneous states
- lookups per resolution
- conditional modifiers
- arithmetic steps
- decisions that require knowledge elsewhere in the book

Complexity is justified when it creates identity or a changing choice. Complexity
that only models detail is a tax.

## Emotional Rhythm

A campaign needs alternation:

`anticipation → commitment → danger → consequence → interpretation → recovery or new ambition`

Constant punishment creates numbness. Constant success removes suspense. Use
safe moments to reveal what is worth protecting and recovery to create new
plans, debts, relationships, and vulnerabilities.

Rewards should change capability or direction, not merely erase prior losses.

## Table-Experience Review

For each rule, answer:

1. What does the player expect before first use?
2. What information supports the decision?
3. What is the most tempting choice?
4. What choice is actually strongest?
5. Can the player explain the outcome afterward?
6. How many minutes and turns does it consume?
7. Whose spotlight grows or shrinks?
8. What emotion repeats after the tenth use?
9. What happens to an unlucky character?
10. Does failure produce a new decision?

Rate each as **healthy**, **watch**, or **harmful**. A harmful agency, fairness,
or participation result blocks publication even when the mathematics are sound.

## Playtest Questions

Ask behavior-first questions:

- What did you think your options were?
- When did you know the risk?
- Which choice felt obvious?
- What did you wish you could do?
- Why do you think the outcome happened?
- Who made the decisive choice?
- When did attention drift?
- Would you choose this option again? Why?

Do not ask only whether the rule was fun. Observe actual choices, hesitation,
lookup time, repeated lines, and post-session recall.

## Quality Gate

- [ ] The advertised fantasy appears in ordinary use.
- [ ] Costs are visible before commitment.
- [ ] Success demonstrates competence without erasing uncertainty.
- [ ] Failure preserves participation or supplies a meaningful new role.
- [ ] Several identities remain respectable choices.
- [ ] Supporting characters own decisions and receive credit.
- [ ] Severe outcomes are telegraphed and causally legible.
- [ ] Repeated use does not become automatic or exhausting.
- [ ] Players can learn from consequences.
- [ ] The rule generates another choice.
