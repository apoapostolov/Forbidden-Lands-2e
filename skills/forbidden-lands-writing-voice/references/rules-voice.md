# Rules Voice: The Game Designer Register

## Two Manuscript Temperatures

The Forbidden Lands corebook was written at two distinct temperatures, and new prose must sit between them:

**Cool precision (Härenstam register):** Front-loaded definitions. Minimal ornament. Short paragraphs that do one job. Game terms used consistently without variation. This is the rules voice: compressed, linear, procedural. Rules chapters — Skills, Combat, Journeys, Stronghold — operate mostly in this temperature.

**Warm-dark imagery (Granström register):** Sensory weight, cultural texture, world depth. Flavor prose, epigraphs, worldbuilding passages. This temperature carries the narrative work.

Good new prose holds both temperatures simultaneously. The mixed bridge — the most difficult register — makes world truth and game truth identical in the same sentence. Neither flavor-then-rules nor rules-then-flavor: both at once.

When writing rules prose, the default temperature is cool. Warmth enters through concrete nouns and specific consequences, not through editorial commentary or atmospheric declaration.

## The Game Designer Persona

The manuscript's rules voice belongs to a game designer who has playtested the system until its failures are known. They write:

**Declaratively.** Not "players may wish to consider" — "roll." Not "this could potentially be used" — "use it when." Every sentence commits.

**From consequence, not from concept.** Rules are introduced in the order a player encounters them at the table: trigger → decision → roll → consequence. Not in the order the designer thinks about them: taxonomy → exceptions → examples.

**Without commentary.** The manuscript never says "this elegant mechanic" or "the design philosophy behind this rule." It states the rule and moves on.

**In second person when address matters.** "You roll for initiative" not "the character rolls for initiative." Second person keeps the reader inside the action.

**With implicit trust.** The reader is not guided through their reaction to each rule. The information is given once, clearly, and the section ends.

## The Five Manuscript Registers

Each register has a distinct job and a distinct prose temperature. Name the register before writing.

### Rules Register

**Job:** Define terms, state procedures, give exceptions. Radically front-loaded — the answer first, context second, exception last.

**Temperature:** Coolest. Clinical-precise. Active verbs, no ornamentation.

**Pattern:** One paragraph, one rule. If the rule has an exception or a timing condition, it earns its own paragraph.

**Example shape:**

> In combat, and other situations where it matters who acts first, all the PCs and their adversaries each draw an initiative card. These cards determine the order in which everyone acts.

Two sentences. First defines the trigger. Second states the effect. Nothing else.

**Target:** 35–50 words, 2–3 sentences per paragraph.

---

### Mixed Bridge Register

**Job:** Move from world truth to game operation in the same breath. The most difficult register and the most common site of AI failure.

**Temperature:** Warm-cool. The best bridges make setting fact and game permission identical — the same sentence carries both frequencies.

**Pattern:** World condition in first sentence. Game consequence in second sentence. Player advisory in third (optional). The two frequencies share the same world.

**Example shape:**

> Life as an adventurer is hard and often violent. Death can come quickly, be it from a sword, a fall, or an arrow. Before you enter combat, you should always ask yourself: Is it worth it?

The second sentence is simultaneously atmospheric (you will die violently) and mechanical (here are three damage sources). That duality is what the bridge achieves.

**Target:** 35–50 words, 2–3 sentences. The atmospheric sentence must do mechanical work. If it does not point toward a rule, cut it.

---

### Item Voice Register

**Job:** Describe what an object is, what it does, and what happens when it fails. Physical, material, tactile — written by someone who has held the object.

**Temperature:** Neutral-cool. Concrete inventory first, function second, limitation third.

**Pattern:** Material → function → limitation. The reader closes the description knowing what they are holding and what will go wrong with it.

**Example shape:**

> A short shovel, a hand pick, a hammer, a pry-iron, a few wedges, nails, cord, scraps of leather, a knife, a flask of oil. Enough to dig, split, or lever most things. None of it is fine, and none of it is meant to last.

Inventory list, functional summary, limitation. No adjectives beyond the physical.

**Target:** 40–55 words, 2–4 sentences.

---

### Example Voice Register

**Job:** Teach a rule by showing it in motion. Named characters, concrete situation, consequence immediately visible.

**Temperature:** Warm-cool. Narrative enough to care about the outcome; compressed enough to stay functional.

**Pattern:** One sentence of setup (named characters + situation + threat). Action paragraphs, one per beat. Consequence visible after each action. Stop when the lesson is complete.

**Example shape:**

> Nirmena the huntress and Tyrgar the dwarf are leaving a ruin with a handful of cheap gems when three orcs step out of the brush.

One sentence. Two named characters. A situation. An immediate threat. The example is already a story.

**Target:** 3–6 short paragraphs, each 30–50 words. One paragraph for setup, one for each action or rule hook, one for the consequence.

---

### Flavor Exposition Register

**Job:** Establish world conditions, cultural facts, or subsystem context before rules begin. The flavor exists to prepare the reader — it is not decoration.

**Temperature:** Warm. Sensory anchors, world specificity. But brevity is mandatory.

**Pattern:** First sentence names the world truth. Second sentence pivots to player relevance. Third (optional) adds danger, distinction, or cultural specificity.

**Target:** 40–55 words, 2–3 sentences. Ratio of atmosphere to instruction is roughly 1:2. If the flavor sentence does not lead anywhere, cut it.

---

## Chapter Temperature Map

Match the temperature of the chapter you are writing for. A talent description should not read like a journey passage. A critical injury should not read like a magic spell.

| Chapter | Temperature | Dominant register |
|---|---|---|
| Front Matter | Warm-dark, inviting | Fiction opener + mixed bridge |
| Your Adventurer | Neutral-warm, practical | Flavor exposition + rules |
| Skills | Cool, precise | Rules + definitions |
| Talents | Cool-warm, dense | Rules + flavor bridges |
| Combat | Harsh, compressed | Rules + examples |
| Critical Injuries | Cold, clinical-honest | Tables + consequences |
| Magic | Warm-dark, wonder-adjacent | Fiction + rules + bridges |
| Journeys | Weather-textured, procedural | Mixed bridge + rules |
| Stronghold | Practical, building-focused | Rules + item descriptions |
| Gear | Grounded, tactile | Item description + tables |
| Appendix | Reference, minimal | Tables + compressed rules |

## Paragraph Shapes

The manuscript uses four paragraph shapes in rules-adjacent prose. Name the shape before writing the paragraph.

**The hammer.** One or two sentences. Hits hard. Used for warnings, hard truths, chapter openers, calls to action.

> "Before you enter combat, you should always ask yourself: Is it worth it?"

**The tool.** Three to four sentences. Defines, explains, closes on practical guidance. The manuscript workhorse for rules definitions. One paragraph, one rule.

> Most skills and rules definitions follow this shape.

**The window.** Two to three sentences. Opens a view into the world, then closes on game relevance. Used for flavor exposition and mixed bridges.

> "There are no mints in the Ravenlands — and no banks either. There are no agreed-upon exchange rates, no standardized price-lists, and no coins verified by a royal stamp. Markets in the Ravenlands run like early medieval exchanges: local, personal, and shaped by scarcity more than coin."

**The inventory.** One sentence listing multiple concrete items, conditions, or consequences. Used for gear kits, environmental details, spell components.

> The Makeshift Tools description.

If you cannot name your paragraph's shape, you have not decided what it is for. Name the shape. Name the job. Then write.

## Multi-Rule Sequence Writing

When an example must teach several rules inside one short scene, follow this pattern:

1. Establish who is acting and what the immediate pressure is.
2. Move through the action in real timing order — the order the players experience it, not the order rules appear in the chapter.
3. Mention only the rule hooks that actually change the outcome.
4. Show consequences immediately after the triggering action.
5. Stop as soon as the lesson is learned. Do not exhaust the subsystem.

**Good shape:**

> The raider has already spent both actions, so he cannot parry when Heme swings.

**Bad shape:**

> As explained above, characters normally have two actions, and because of the reactive-action structure established earlier, the raider is no longer eligible to parry.

The first teaches through the scene. The second teaches through cross-referencing its own explanations. The first trusts the reader. The second does not.

### Action order in combat examples

- Start with the tactical position, not with a lecture about the rules being demonstrated.
- Name which action is fast and which is slow only when that distinction changes the outcome.
- Show defensive limits only when they affect the result.
- Convert extra successes into immediate bodily consequence: armor absorbs, wound opens, bone breaks. Not: "the excess damage is applied as additional attribute reduction."
- End once the interaction is clear.

### Journey and recovery sequences

Journey examples follow the same logic: state the pressure chain (travel quarter, weather roll, supply spend, exhaustion mark) in the order the table experiences it. Recovery examples track timers and countdowns as they actually tick — not in tutorial order.

## Progressive Disclosure Architecture

Complex rules fail readers when everything is front-loaded. Introduce subsystems in five layers:

**Layer 1: The core loop.** What does the player do, what does the GM do, what happens? One paragraph at most. No exceptions, no modifiers. Just the spine.

**Layer 2: The first example.** A short scene that walks through the core loop with named characters. The reader sees the rule in motion before encountering any modifiers.

**Layer 3: Modifiers and common variations.** Now that the reader has a mental model, introduce the factors that shift it — tables, conditions, situational bonuses. These land because the reader has something to attach them to.

**Layer 4: The richer example.** A second scene, using the same characters or situation, that demonstrates the modifiers in practice. It should feel like a natural continuation — the same session, the same journey, the same fight — not a fresh scenario.

**Layer 5: Edge cases and GM advice.** Corner cases, optional rules, unusual situations. Only readers who have absorbed the base system will reach this layer.

**Bad order (front-loaded):**

```text
Definition → Full modifier table → Exception list → Edge cases → Example
```

The reader drowns before reaching solid ground.

**Good order (progressive):**

```text
Core mechanic → Short example → Modifiers → Richer example → Edge cases
```

The reader stands on each layer before the next is added.

### Gauging depth by audience

- A beginner can read Layers 1 and 2, then start playing. The example carries enough of the rule to get through the first session.
- An intermediate player reads through Layer 4 and understands the full working system with modifiers.
- An experienced GM reads Layer 5 for the edges, the advice, and the design reasoning.

Do not label sections "beginner" or "advanced." Order the text so that stopping early still leaves the reader with a working version of the rule.

## Recurring Characters in Examples

The same 2–5 characters across a chapter or across the book reduce setup cost, build cumulative stakes, and give abstract mechanics a mnemonic anchor. Readers remember stories about people, not stories about systems.

### Rules for the cast

- **Establish characters early.** First example in any major subsystem: name 2–4 characters. Give each a one-sentence identity — profession, weapon, visible trait — in the way another character would notice them. "Garmelda, a wolfkin with a raven's voice and a long knife" is enough for a lifetime of examples.
- **Keep identities stable.** Tyrgar fights with a broadsword in Chapter 5 and Chapter 8. Garmelda speaks at every gate. Consistency teaches role differentiation without stating it.
- **Let characters accumulate consequences.** If Siga took a critical injury in Chapter 6, the Chapter 8 journey example can note that she is still healing. This teaches condition persistence without a lecture.
- **Limit the cast to 3–5 characters.** More, and readers confuse them. Fewer, and examples cannot show role differentiation.
- **Do not kill a recurring character** unless the death teaches a rule that requires it.

### The cast from the original manuscript

The corebook uses: **Nirmena** (huntress), **Tyrgar** (dwarf fighter), **Heme**, **Garmelda** (spokesperson, wolfkin), **Mormelan** (educated swordsman), and **Haggamur** (soldier). New examples should use these characters before inventing fresh ones. The reader already knows them.

## Interconnected Narrative Examples

When a subsystem spans multiple sections, the examples across those sections should feel like scenes from one session, not isolated demonstrations.

**Thread structure:**

- **Example A:** Fellowship arrives — first trigger of the mechanic.
- **Example B** (later in the chapter): Fellowship engaged — second rule in the chain.
- **Example C:** Consequence — downstream effect of scores set in A and B.

Each example must stand alone: a reader who skipped Example A still understands Example B. The connection enriches; it must not create a prerequisite chain.

Use time transitions, not recaps: "An hour later, the fellowship sits in the headman's hall." Never summarize previous examples.

## What AI Gets Wrong in Rules Prose

### The fiction-rules separation

AI writes three sentences of flavor, then switches to clinical rules voice. The manuscript holds both frequencies simultaneously — every flavor sentence points toward a rule, and every rule sentence carries a trace of the world.

### The absence of specific names

AI defaults to "the adventurer," "the character," "the player." The manuscript says Nirmena, Tyrgar, Mormelan. Named characters carry weight that generics cannot.

### The fear of commitment

AI hedges: "may," "could," "potentially," "in some cases." The manuscript commits: "you will," "this costs," "the result is." State facts. Game design is a series of definitive statements.

### The impulse to explain significance

AI adds a sentence explaining why the preceding paragraph matters: "This makes combat one of the most engaging aspects of the game." The manuscript never does this. The reader determines significance.

### The summary instinct

After presenting information, AI restates it in summary form. The manuscript never summarizes itself. The information is given once, clearly, and the section ends.

### The decoration reflex

AI adds an adjective or adverb to almost every noun and verb. The manuscript uses bare nouns and strong verbs. "The blade" is enough. "The sharp gleaming blade" is two words of nothing.

## Checklist Before Submitting Rules Prose

- [ ] The core loop is stated in the first one or two paragraphs — before tables, modifiers, or edge cases.
- [ ] The first example appears within the first page of rules text, not at the end.
- [ ] Modifiers and tables appear after the reader has seen the base case in action.
- [ ] At least two examples share characters or situation, creating narrative continuity.
- [ ] A reader who stops after the first example has a playable version of the rule.
- [ ] Edge cases and GM advice appear last.
- [ ] No example introduces more than two new rules at once.
- [ ] Each example ends on a concrete game state, not a system observation.
- [ ] No sentence uses "may" or "could" where the rule is definitive.
- [ ] No paragraph restates the information given in the preceding paragraph.
- [ ] Every flavor sentence points toward a game consequence.
- [ ] Second person used for player-facing instructions.
- [ ] Register identified for each paragraph before writing it.
- [ ] Paragraph shape named before writing it.
