# World Vocabulary

This document translates the Forbidden Lands setting vocabulary
into prose practice. It answers not "what are the correct names"
but "how do characters use those names in speech, in writing,
and in thought."

Sources: `skills/forbidden-lands-lore/references/kin-deep-dive.md`,
`gods-and-religions.md`, and `setting-ravenland-and-human-peoples.md`.

## 1. The Three Human Peoples

### Alderlander

**In speech (self-reference):** "Alderlander" when speaking to
outsiders or to authority. In village speech, clan or village
name more often: "Karnsman," "the Salthorn people." The identity
is local before it is cultural.

**In speech (other-reference):** Alderlanders call Aslenes
"riders" or "the western people." They call Ailanders "the
eastlanders" or "the old stock."

**In writing and authority documents:** "Subject of Alderland,"
"of Alderland birth," or simply the village or fort name.

**In thought:** An Alderlander character does not think of
themselves as "Alderlander" in the middle of a practical problem.
They think by the task. Cultural identity enters in moments of
cultural pressure — when dealing with a Rust Brother, when
meeting a stranger from the west, when someone uses a custom
they do not know.

**Physical tells in prose:** Pale. Dressed in wool. Organized
tools and arranged gear. Knows the written terms of things. Can
read; may not write well. Defaults to hierarchy — "who is the
head here?" is an Alderlander first question.

---

### Aslene

**In speech (self-reference):** "Aslene" in formal and
cross-cultural speech. In own community: family name, horse-band
name, or clan name. A Galdene does not call himself Aslene first;
he says "Galdene" as the first identity and will correct you if
you conflate the two.

**In speech (other-reference):** Aslenes call Alderlanders
"wall-builders" or "the southers." They call Ailanders "the
field-people." These are descriptive, not necessarily contemptuous.

**In writing:** Aslenes are less committed to written records than
Alderlanders. Obligations are spoken and witnessed. A written
contract from an Aslene is unusual and signals that the Aslene
has spent time in Alderlander territory.

**In thought:** An Aslene character thinks in terms of what is
owed and by whom. Obligation-tracking is the Aslene cognitive
baseline. Who is their family? Who are their horses? What was
promised? What has been paid?

**Galdene distinction:** Galdenes are western Aslene nomads whose
lands were razed. They are bitter, specifically, about the loss of
**Ashen Vale**. In speech they may reference Ashen Vale as the
standard of comparison for anything good: "better than what we
had in the Vale" is approval. "Not as good as the Vale" is
permanent contempt.

**Physical tells in prose:** Dark hair, Mongolic bone structure.
Braided or tied. Layered clothing. First action with a horse is
to check its condition before their own. Gold or bronze jewelry
over silver. Color in their dress where an Alderlander has none.

---

### Ailander

**In speech (self-reference):** "Ailander" is used primarily
by non-Ailanders describing them. Among themselves, the identity
is village, family, or the Raven. A true Ailander self-identifies
as "a Raven-keeping people" more readily than by the geographic
name.

**In speech (other-reference):** Ailanders call Alderlanders "the
wall people" or "the southern faith." Calling a Rust Brother
"Alderlander" is an insult by implication — it conflates the
Rust faith with Alderland origin, which is both historically
complex and politically charged.

**In thought:** Ailander characters think by obligation and
witness. Who saw this? Who owes whom? The oral contract, the
witnessed promise, carries what the written document does for
Alderlanders.

**Physical tells in prose:** Short, straw-blond or light brown
hair. Wide-shouldered. Plain wool. Unhurried. A woman who stops
working to answer a question and then returns to it without
commentary is a Le Guin-appropriate Ailander detail.

## 2. Non-Human Kin

### Elves

**What they are called:** "The Stillelves" or simply "elves."
They are not "the fair folk" or "the ancient ones" or any generic
fantasy elevation. They are a specific people who withdrew from
the world after the Shift.

**In speech:** Characters do not address elves casually. The
correct address for a Stillelf elder is silence first —
wait to be recognized. Elves address humans first when they
choose to; the reverse is a social transgression most humans
know to avoid.

**The Golden Bough and Order of Maidens** are the druidic orders.
"The Bough" in speech; "a Maiden" for an Order of Maidens druid.
Maidens have an emerald shard in their forehead — a visible
identifier. Characters who recognize this know what they are.
Characters who do not may not.

**Elvenspring:** Half-elves of Ravenland origin. They despise
being called "half-elves" in mixed company. Among themselves
they may use it. To others they give their order name (Golden
Bough, Order of Maidens) rather than the heritage designation.
They find humans embarrassing but feel responsibility. In prose:
their speech to humans is patient in a way that is slightly
wrong — more patient than a human peer, as if speaking to
someone slower than expected.

---

### Dwarves

**What they call themselves:** The full cultural identity is
"servant of Huge" but in speech this is shortened to the clan
or the craft. "Belder clan" is a named dwarven group. "Stone
Singer" is a dwarven sorcerer-priest.

**Oath and exclamation:** The canonical dwarven oath is
**"Huge's whiskers!"** Use it. Do not invent generic
dwarven oaths.

**In thought:** Dwarves think by craft and material. A dwarf
encountering a problem frames it as a construction problem. What
is the material? What is the correct joining? What is the
structural weakness? This applies to social problems as much
as to stone-work.

**True Grit tells in prose:** Dwarves do not complain about
physical damage. They may stop and take note of it with a
single clause — "his arm was done, he noted" — and continue.
The injury is inventory, not drama.

---

### Halflings

**Cultural tension in naming:** Halflings are secretly kin to
goblins. This is not public knowledge. The **Penita** sect
seeks the lost moonstones of Eor's creation myth. In speech,
halflings identify by community (village name, family name)
before kin name.

**In prose:** Halflings appear pastoral and agreeable. The
wildness underneath is the point — do not announce it. Let
it arrive in specific behavior.

---

### Wolfkin

**How they refer to themselves:** By pack, by territory, by
kill. "Pack of the North Road" is more natural than "wolfkin
of this region." The hunger is not metaphorical to them — it
is the primary relationship to the world.

**In speech with humans:** Brief, specific, contemptuous of
softness. A wolfkin who speaks at length to a human is unusual
and should signal something (a debt, a specific need, a
political situation).

---

### Orcs

**Cultural identity:** Ex-slave culture with deep grievance.
Dwarves kept orcs as servants in exchange for land. The orcs
are now free. In speech, orcs do not reference the servitude
unless forced to. The history is present in everything they do
and is not discussed.

**In prose:** The word "free" carries specific weight in orc
speech. Not rhetorical freedom — literal, specific, recent.
"We are free" is a statement of present fact that has not yet
lost its newness.

## 3. Divine Names in Use

### The Protector (Three Faces)

The key rule: **the same deity is called three different names by
three traditions that have gone to war over which name is correct.**
Characters do not say "the Protector" in normal speech. They say
the name their tradition uses.

- **Wyrm** — used by the Congregation. In speech: "the Wyrm's
  will," "by the Serpent," "the Congregation teaches." The symbol
  is the ouroboros. In narrative: a Congregation member who refers
  to "the Raven" is either deliberately ecumenical or is in enemy
  territory.

- **Raven** — used by the Raven Church and Ailanders. In speech:
  "Raven keep you," "the Raven's road," "a Raven blessing." The
  Raven Church's priesthood is the **Raven Sisters.** Their cry
  in emergency or ceremony: unnamed in the canon — but a Raven
  Sister blesses with "Raven-wing" as a verb: "Raven-wing the
  dead." (Author-proposed usage consistent with setting.)

- **Rust** — used by the Rust Brothers. In speech: "Rust protect,"
  "iron against the dark," "by the Rust." The Rust Brothers also
  swear "by the iron" in casual speech. A Rust Brother who says
  "the Raven" is either quoting an enemy or has converted, which
  is a major event.

**The Blackwings (Reapenters):** Their cry is canonical:
**"Nevermore!"** Use it. Do not invent variations.

---

### Heme

Goddess of hunt and fertility. The **Sisters of Heme** are
her order. In speech, Heme is invoked at hunts, at births, and
at sacrifices. **Crude figurines** appear at roadside shrines.
In prose: a character passing a roadside Heme-shrine does not
explain who Heme is. They may touch it, or not. Their gesture
tells us their faith.

Characters from Ailander backgrounds may leave something at a
Heme shrine as a matter of custom, not deep devotion — the same
way a person might sign a cross out of long habit.

## 4. The Older Gods

The Older Gods are pre-Protector worship. Characters in the
eastern Forbidden Lands keep them as primary faith. In the west,
under Rust Brothers influence, the Older Gods are semi-forbidden
but still practiced privately.

The naming convention for the Older Gods is single-word, capitalized,
elemental:

| God | Domain | In Speech |
|---|---|---|
| **Huge** | Forge, creation, dwarves | "Huge's whiskers!" (oath); "Huge willing" (hope) |
| **Clay** | Life, creation, gender-less | "Clay-formed" (describing something alive); "by Clay" (mild oath) |
| **Wail** | Wind, air | "Wail take it" (frustration); invoked before a sea crossing |
| **Flow** | Water, springs | "Flow's Delight" (a spring shrine); "Flow's blessing" at a well |
| **Nightwalker** | Moon, smoke, dreams | Almost never invoked aloud. Associated with bad luck. |
| **Horn** | Volcano, fire, horses | Aslene oath: "Horn's breath!" |
| **Eor** | Moon, deception, lies | Invoked secretly by goblins; halflings are ashamed of it |
| **The Red Wanderer** | A wandering star | Named when something is unplaceable or threatening |

**Prose rule:** Characters do not explain the Older Gods while
invoking them. The invocation carries the knowledge. A traveler
who invokes Flow at a well is not eccentric or archaic — it is
the correct thing to do at a well if you have grown up here.

**The Nightwalker:** This god is the only one not invoked in normal
speech. You do not call the Nightwalker by name casually. If a
character invokes the Nightwalker, they are either in extremis or
they are from a tradition that specifically works with him (certain
necromantic orders). Characters who see smoke behaving strangely
do not explain it is the Nightwalker's spies. They notice it and
move differently.

## 5. Factions and Orders

### How They Refer to Themselves

**Rust Brothers** — call themselves "Brothers" to each other. To
outsiders: "the Brotherhood" or "servants of the Iron." They do
not say "Rust" as a self-description in most speech; that is
what outsiders call them.

**Raven Sisters** — call themselves "Sisters" and each other by
name. In formal speech: "a Healer of the Raven," "a Speaker of
the Raven." The Sisters are specific in their function — healing
Raven Sisters and shapeshifter Raven Sisters have different
social positions.

**Golden Bough** — "a Bough-druid" or simply their given name.
They do not announce themselves. Their role is visible in their
tools and their behavior with animals.

**Order of Maidens** — identified by the emerald shard in the
forehead. In speech: "a Maiden" or "one of the Maidens." Formally:
"of the Order." They are aggressive and specific; their
conversation opens with what they are there to do.

**Iron Guard** — the Rust Brothers' military arm. Refer to
themselves by rank within the Guard. To outsiders, "a Guard"
or "of the Iron Guard." Plate armor, closed helmet, Rust-sigil
shield — visually distinct.

### How They Refer to Each Other

- Rust Brothers on Raven Sisters: "the heretics," "the bird-keepers,"
  or simply "Ravens" (dismissive noun).
- Raven Sisters on Rust Brothers: "the iron-binders," "the
  Zygofer-servants," or "Brothers" (said flatly, with the
  theological charge in the tone).
- A village headwoman referring to a Rust Brother in his presence:
  "Brother Aldric." His name. Correct form. She does not say
  "the Rust Brother" while he is standing there.

## 6. Currency and Economic Vocabulary

**Currency:** Silver coins are the primary currency. There are
no silver mines in Ravenland. All coins are old — Alder War
remnants, worn and clipped. Gold exists but is treated with
suspicion (limited local deposit, no trade chain backing it).
Copper also circulates.

**In prose and speech:**

- "Silver" is the word. "Coin" is the generic. "Copper" is low
  value. Nobody in Ravenland says "gold pieces" unless they are
  describing found treasure from before the Mist.
- Prices are described specifically: "half a copper for a day's
  water" tells you more about the village than "the water was
  cheap."
- A character who quotes prices in gold to a village merchant
  is either from outside Ravenland or is performing a social
  status claim.

**Economic tells by culture:**

- **Alderlander:** Written contracts. Knows the price of grain,
  iron, and passage-toll. Can calculate compound debt.
- **Aslene:** Values in horses and obligations before coin.
  "Worth a good horse" is high praise. Worth two is exceptional.
- **Ailander:** Barter-base. Coin is secondary to obligation.
  "She will owe us" is an acceptable payment in Ailander
  village speech.

**The grain economy:**

In any village scene, grain is the baseline economic fact.
It is not mentioned for atmosphere — it is the actual limit
condition of survival. A reference to the state of the grain
store (full, quarter-full, nearly empty) tells you whether the
village can survive the winter without a specific number of
characters dying.

## 7. Social Rank and Authority Terms

**The headwoman/headman:** The village authority. In most
Ailander and mixed villages, this is a woman as often as a man.
Alderlander settlements more often defer to a male head. The
title is: "headwoman," "headman," or in formal speech "the head
of [village name]." Not "the chieftain." Not "the elder" unless
that is a specific title in that village's tradition.

**Raven Sisters** hold authority over birth, burial, and healing.
Their authority is parallel to but separate from the headwoman's.
A headwoman who ignores a Raven Sister's judgment on a burial
is taking a political risk.

**Rust Brothers** hold tithe authority and heresy authority. A
Rust Brother's authority over a village is economic (the tithe)
and punitive (he can declare a person a heretic). He cannot
legally conscript the village without Iron Guard backing, but
in practice he can make life unlivable until someone leaves.

**The Quards** are the Aslene hierarchical warrior-riders. Their
ranks are internal and do not translate to Alderlander authority
structures. A Quard of rank meeting an Iron Guard of rank is
a diplomatic problem with no clean precedent.

**Named titles in use:**

| Title | Who Uses It | Context |
|---|---|---|
| Headwoman / Headman | Village authority | Direct address and third-person reference |
| Sister | Raven Church | Direct address to a Raven Sister |
| Brother | Rust Brotherhood | Direct address to a Rust Brother |
| Rust Lord | The Rust Brotherhood's leader | Formal title; not used in casual speech |
| Psychopomp | Congregation priesthood | Direct address and function description |
| Maiden | Order of Maidens | Direct address; also used in third person |
| Stone Singer | Dwarven sorcerer-priest | Function description, not a social title |

## 8. Place Names and How to Use Them

**Canonical settlement names:** Weatherstone, Harga, Ravenford.
These names are used in the canonical setting and have history.
Weatherstone in particular has a specific legendary weight — King
Algarod standing watch there.

**Manuscript-introduced settlement names** (used in vignettes):
Hollowford, Ashfall, Blackwood, Greymark, Redrun, Yellowdew,
Varbeck, Salthorn. These are in use within this manuscript and
can be referenced in prose.

**Naming conventions for invented places:**

- Compound noun constructions: [Terrain] + [Function] or
  [Event] = Greymark, Salthorn, Hollowford.
- Single-element names common for geographical features:
  Stillmire, Amber Peaks, White Peaks.
- Do not use Tolkienesque Elvish-sound names (Lothlindra,
  Miredhel) — they are wrong for this setting's register.
- Do not use generic fantasy names (Darkwood, Shadowfen,
  Grimhold) — they are the wrong kind of compound.
- Do use: weather conditions, mineral or material qualities,
  geographical facts, historical events. "The place where the
  ford was before the river shifted" becomes Oldford or
  Dryford, not something heroic.

**Geographical tells:**

- **Stillmire** (also written Stillmist): waterlogged ground,
  bad footing, cold. Characters arriving in this region are
  described as wet from the knees down within a paragraph.
- **Amber Peaks**: highland region. Travel to it is two to
  three days harder than the flat. A character who has been
  to Amber Peaks mentions the altitude cold.
- **The Southern Range**: the only way south. Two crossings:
  the Stillmist elven pass and the Winding Ways (dwarven cave
  system). A character who knows which crossing to take has
  either been there or has a specific intelligence source.

## 9. Register Rules by Culture

How each group patterns speech in prose. Not dialect parody —
register tells.

### Alderlander register:

- Formal when establishing authority or asking for it.
- "Who is the head here?" is a natural opener, not an insult.
- Written contract vocabulary in speech: "the agreement," "as
  was settled," "on the terms."
- Quotes precedent. "The old law holds" or "the way it was done
  in my father's time."

### Aslene/Galdene register:

- Names the horse in conversation before the human.
  "My horse needs water. After that, I could eat."
- Obligation-explicit. Does not leave an obligation implicit.
  "You gave us water. We owe you water." The transaction is
  spoken immediately.
- The Galdene register has the Ashen Vale grief underneath
  everything. A Galdene who says something is "good enough"
  may mean "better than nothing in the Vale" — the comparison
  is there but unstated.

### Ailander register:

- Observation before judgment. Names what is seen before
  offering an opinion on it.
- The Raven comes into speech naturally. Not as an oath —
  as a fact. "The Raven willing" is not piety-performance; it
  is the baseline epistemological frame.
- Hesitates before naming the Rust Brothers. Will use
  "the Brotherhood" in their presence and "the iron-men" or
  "the Brothers" (with a specific flat tone) behind their backs.

### Rust Brother register:

- Legalistic and declarative. Every sentence is either a
  judgment or a fact.
- "The tithe is due." Not "I was hoping you might be able
  to settle the tithe." The Rust Brother does not hope. He
  records.
- Cites chapter and order law when convenient. The specific
  chapter name need not be canonical; the citation habit is.

### Raven Sister register:

- Warm but not soft. The Sister knows what she knows.
- Names the condition before the treatment. "You have fever
  in the chest. We will need ash and the white root and
  three days."
- Uses "we" for community actions. "We will see to the
  burial." Not "I will take care of it."

## 10. Forbidden Vocabulary

These words and phrases do not belong in this manuscript.
Remove on sight:

**Generic fantasy register:**

- Arcane, mystical, eldritch, shadowy, legendary, immemorial
- "The dark lord," "chosen one," "destiny," "the ancient power"
- "Evil lurks," "shadows gather," "the darkness grows"

**Modern psychological register:**

- Trauma, triggered, toxic, boundaries, closure, processing
- "She needed to heal," "he struggled with his demons"
  (unless literal demons)

**Promotional/marketing register:**

- Vibrant, rich, thriving, diverse tapestry, living history
- "A place where traditions run deep"

**Adventure-module register:**

- "The party might choose to..." "This presents an opportunity..."
- "The villagers desperately need someone to..."
- "Rumors of strange happenings" as a scene-setter

**Meta-reference:**

- Do not reference the game system in narrative prose.
  Not "Willpower," not "a Push Roll," not "the Gamemaster."
  These terms belong in rules sections, not in fiction
  or narrative prose.

**Anachronistic reference:**

- No terms postdating approximately 1200 CE that would be
  recognizable as modern conceptual vocabulary.
- Exception: The game system uses post-medieval terminology
  for its mechanics. That vocabulary stays in the mechanics
  sections only.
