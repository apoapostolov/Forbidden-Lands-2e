# Sentence Architecture

## Overview

This reference specifies sentence construction for the six-author
tradition that this skill synthesizes. Each author has a distinct
sentence architecture. The Forbidden Lands target voice draws from
specific authors at specific registers.

The sentence is not a container for information. It is an experience.
The reader finishes a sentence knowing one thing that changes their
understanding of the scene. If the sentence ends and the reader knows
nothing new that matters, the sentence is not doing its work.

---

## Author Sentence Profiles

### Andrzej Sapkowski

**Length:** 12-20 words average. Medium. Considered, not economical.

**Structure:** Often a subordinate clause or observation, followed
by a short declarative result sentence.

> The tracks were fresh, pressed deep by a heavy weight. Wrong weight
> for a man. He followed them toward the tree line.

**Characteristic pattern:** Observation that implies a conclusion;
short sentence that states it without commentary.

**Opening tendency:** Enters the scene mid-observation. Not at the
beginning of a sequence; at the moment where the observation is
already in progress.

**Ending tendency:** Short, declarative, often ending on an action
verb or a specific noun. Not a summary; a fact.

**Paragraph rhythm:** Dense. Multiple observations per paragraph.
Accumulates to a picture before any conclusion is drawn.

**Diagnostics:**

- Does it end on a fact, not a commentary? ✓
- Does the short sentence land after the longer setup? ✓
- Is the interiority revealed by what is observed, not stated? ✓

---

### Guy Gavriel Kay

**Length:** 18-35 words average. Longest of the tradition.

**Structure:** Multiple subordinate clauses building to a weighted
landing image or weighted verb.

> She had stood on this hill before, once, in a different time,
> and had not known then what she would come to understand standing
> here now: that the dead have no interest in the living's grief.

**Characteristic pattern:** Temporal construction — then and now
layered; the sentence reaches across time.

**Opening tendency:** Omniscient or wide observation. Situates
the scene in a larger context before narrowing.

**Ending tendency:** Ends on the weighted noun or a statement
of irreversible fact. "The ship was gone." "She did not know."

**Paragraph rhythm:** Architecturally deliberate. Each paragraph
has an arc. The final sentence of the paragraph carries the weight.

**Diagnostics:**

- Does it earn every subordinate clause? ✓
- Does the temporal reach add weight, not confusion? ✓
- Is the ending specific, not thematic? ✓

---

### K.J. Parker

**Length:** 8-16 words average. Short to medium. Declarative.

**Structure:** Flat declarative chains. X. X costs this.
This follows. The implication, often, is left to the reader.

> The sword was old. The hilt had been replaced twice; you could
> see the different metal in the light. Someone had used it long
> enough to wear out two hilts.

**Characteristic pattern:** Physical observation → material inference →
human implication. The human implication is stated briefly if at all.

**Opening tendency:** Begins on the most specific available fact.
Not atmosphere; the object, the situation, the material reality.

**Ending tendency:** Often ends on a dry implication or a
deliberately understated consequence. "That was inconvenient."

**Paragraph rhythm:** Short and punchy. Often a long declarative
paragraph will end on a single-sentence conclusion that is
shorter than every other sentence in the paragraph.

**Diagnostics:**

- Is the declarative chain free of decorative clauses? ✓
- Is the technical observation doing narrative work? ✓
- Is the dry understatement specific, not diffuse? ✓

---

### Ursula K. Le Guin

**Length:** 14-24 words average. Medium to long. Observational,
qualified with precision.

**Structure:** Observational statement, followed by specific
qualification that advances rather than hedges.

> The women who went to the granary first on Harvest Day were
> not chosen for the role; they had arrived at it by being the
> last surviving woman of the household over seventy, which was
> a simpler logic than any ceremony would have been.

**Characteristic pattern:** Describes a practice; specifies the
underlying logic; does not editorialize.

**Opening tendency:** Habitually enters a scene at the normal
state before any disruption. This is what happens on ordinary days.

**Ending tendency:** Ends on the specific detail that carries
the anthropological weight. The ending is quiet and dense.

**Paragraph rhythm:** Balanced and patient. Every sentence does
specific work. No sentence is there for rhythm alone.

**Diagnostics:**

- Does the qualification advance, not hedge? ✓
- Is the observation specific enough to carry meaning without
  explanation? ✓
- Does the sentence end where the observation ends, not where
  a moral would? ✓

---

### Bernard Cornwell

**Length:** 7-14 words average. Shortest consistent prose of the
tradition.

**Structure:** Short declaratives for action; longer analytical
sentences for tactical reasoning.

> We had three hundred men and they had five hundred. The math
> was wrong. But we had the ditch.

**Characteristic pattern:** Fact — implication — specific mitigating
detail. The mitigation is never abstract; it is a physical feature
of the landscape or situation.

**Opening tendency:** Opens on the physical condition or the
logistical situation. What is the ground? What is the weather?
How many men?

**Ending tendency:** Ends on a concrete noun or on a verb of
action. Not analysis; the thing itself.

**Paragraph rhythm:** The shortest paragraphs in the tradition.
Often two or three sentences. The reader moves fast; the world
is pressing.

**Diagnostics:**

- Is every sentence earning its length? ✓
- Is the tactical analysis grounded in physical detail? ✓
- Does it end on the concrete fact, not the observation of it? ✓

---

### John Gwynne

**Length:** Variable — 5-12 words for action, 15-28 words for
memory and reflection.

**Structure:** Tight declaratives for immediate experience;
compound sentences with subordinate clauses for reflection.

> She stepped over the body.
>
> She remembered, later, how light the man had seemed — lighter
> than any man she had ever killed before, as though the dying
> had already taken the weight from him.

**Characteristic pattern:** Action in the present tense rhythm;
reflection in past-layered construction.

**Opening tendency:** Opens on the specific body state. Temperature,
hunger, pain, fatigue. One specific physical thing before any
orientation to the situation.

**Ending tendency:** Action sentences end on verbs. Reflection
sentences end on specific images, usually sensory.

**Paragraph rhythm:** The rhythm shifts between two modes and
the shift is itself a signal. Action paragraphs are compressed;
reflection paragraphs expand.

**Diagnostics:**

- Is the body-state in the first sentence? ✓
- Is the sentence length signaling the temporal mode? ✓
- Is the action stripped to minimum words? ✓

## The Forbidden Lands Target Sentence

The manuscript's target register is Cornwell's physical pragmatism
with Sapkowski's investigative depth and Le Guin's ecological specificity.
Not Kay's lyrical length except at designated registers (vignettes,
epigraphs, grief scenes).

**Target specifications:**

- **Length:** 12-18 words average
- **Ending:** concrete noun or action verb; no trailing qualifiers
- **Physical noun requirement:** at least one specific physical
  noun per sentence that advances the scene
- **Interiority:** through observation catalog, not emotional statement
- **Qualification:** advances meaning; does not hedge

**Diagnostic questions for any sentence:**

1. Does it end on weight? (A noun or verb that matters, not "somehow"
   or "in a way" or "as best she could.")
2. Does it contain a physical noun? (Not "the situation," "the feeling,"
   "the truth" — the sword, the fire, the mud.)
3. Is it doing one job? (If it is doing two things, consider whether
   it should be two sentences.)
4. Is the qualification earning its place? (Every subordinate clause
   advances the meaning. If removing it changes nothing, cut it.)
5. Does it end where the observation ends, not where an explanation
   would? (The explanation is left to the reader.)

## Rhythm Techniques

### The Short Sentence as Landing

After three or four sentences of medium length, a short sentence
delivers a fact. The fact lands because the rhythm sets it up.

> The tracks came from the north, turned at the ridge, then
> continued toward the treeline at the speed of something that
> was no longer hurrying. The mud was fresh. Whatever made them
> had been here in the last hour. It was still here.

The final sentence is seven words. Its weight comes entirely from
the rhythm that precedes it.

**Use it for:** revelations, physical danger, moral fact.
**Do not use it for:** atmosphere, description, backstory.

---

### Variation by Register

Vary sentence length consciously according to the scene's register.

| Scene register | Dominant length | Secondary length |
|---|---|---|
| Action/combat | 5-12 words | none |
| Investigation | 12-18 words | occasional 5-word landing |
| Village life | 14-22 words | none |
| Grief/memory | 18-28 words | 5-8 word landing |
| Vignette/epigraph | 18-30 words | none |

Mixing these registers creates the rhythmic contrast that makes
the short sentence land when it arrives.

---

### The Cornwell Sentence Triad

Cornwell's most characteristic rhythm is the sentence triad:
two observation sentences and one implication sentence.

> The ford was shallow and wide. The far bank was open ground
> for fifty paces. Then the tree line began.

Three sentences. Two observations (the ford, the bank). One implication
(the tree line that could hide men). The implication is stated as an
observation; the reader draws the tactical conclusion.

**Use it for:** tactical geography, village first impressions, building
descriptions, approach to any location that matters.

---

### The Kay Temporal Sentence

Kay's long sentences often have a temporal structure: the then and
the now layered within the same grammatical construction. The sentence
crosses time.

> He had not thought of her in four years, and he thought of her
> now, standing in the market where he had last seen her, with the
> same cold wind from the north and the same smell of old fires,
> and found that four years was not enough.

The temporal crossing is available for specific scene types: first
return to a significant place, reunion of characters separated by loss,
the moment a character understands that something is over.

**Do not use it for:** action, village life, combat.

---

### The Le Guin Qualification

Le Guin's sentences contain qualifications that specify rather than
hedge. The structure is: observation — "not because X but because Y."
The correction advances the meaning by naming the more precise truth.

> She had not asked the questions that would have told her what she
> needed to know, not from fear of the answers but from not having
> found the language for them yet.

The "not because X but because Y" pattern is a precision tool, not
an admission of uncertainty. It tells the reader: the simpler
explanation was wrong; here is the specific truth.

**Do not confuse with:** the hedging stack (Pattern 12), which
reduces commitment. Le Guin's qualification increases specificity.

## The Sentence-Ending Diagnostic

The ending of a sentence carries the most weight. Read any sentence
ending and ask:

- **Concrete noun:** Ends on a specific thing in the world. ✓
  ("The sword was old." / "The door did not open.")
- **Action verb:** Ends on something that happened. ✓
  ("He did not answer." / "The fire went out.")
- **Temporal marker:** Ends on a relationship in time. ✓ (Kay only)
  ("That was three years before the siege.")
- **Abstract noun:** Ends on a concept. ✗ Unless forced.
  ("...the weight of her grief." → "...behind her ribs.")
- **Trailing qualifier:** Ends on a limitation. ✗ Always cut.
  ("...as best she could." / "...in a manner of speaking.")
- **Emotional summary:** Ends on named emotion. ✗
  ("...she felt afraid." → "Her hands were shaking.")

## Forbidden Sentence Structures

The following sentence structures produce AI writing tells
regardless of content:

**1. The Elegant Variation ending:**
"...the arcane implement" / "...the sacred artifact" /
"...the storied blade"
→ Repeat the specific noun. "the sword" again. Always.

**2. The Weight-Word ending:**
"...carried the weight of history" / "...bore the mark of fate"
→ Name the specific thing that has weight. "Three hundred dead."

**3. The Balanced Final Clause:**
"...through fire and shadow alike" / "...in fear and in hope"
→ End on one specific thing. The balance is an AI pattern.

**4. The Trailing Softener:**
"...or so she believed" / "...at least for now" / "...perhaps"
→ Cut every trailing softener. Commit.

**5. The Thematic Summary Landing:**
"...which was, perhaps, the real lesson."
→ This is the worst possible sentence ending in the entire
   list. Cut it and cut the impulse that produced it.

---

## Variation Strategy

Monotonous sentence length is an AI tell. It signals that the
sentence was generated at uniform cadence rather than composed
for rhythm.

**Rule:** No more than three consecutive sentences of the same
length type (short/medium/long).

**Rule:** Every page must have at least one short sentence
(under 10 words) that is not a paragraph opener.

**Rule:** Every scene must have at least one sentence that
changes length register — a deliberate move from medium
sentences to short, or from short to medium.

**Rule:** The first sentence of any scene is not allowed
to be the same type as the first sentence of the previous scene.

Measured from prose paragraphs in the Forbidden Lands corebook,
excluding headings, tables, and block quotes. These are baselines
that reveal the original authors' habits. When a draft's metrics
diverge sharply, the prose has probably drifted from the manuscript voice.

| 05 Combat and Damage | 195 | 44.1 | 2.9 |

**Working range:** 35–60 words per paragraph, 2–4 sentences.
Drafts consistently above 60 words per paragraph are over-explaining.

**The two outliers:**
Chapter 04 (Talents) averages 93 words because each talent rank
packs definition, condition, effect, and exception into a single
block. The density is earned by content volume, not by flabby prose.
Chapter 07 (Magic) averages 38.7 words because spell descriptions
compress hard: name, cost, effect, complication. The brevity is
the format, not the style.

### Target by Register

**Rules prose:** 35–50 words, 2–3 sentences. One paragraph, one
rule. If a second paragraph is needed, the rule has an exception
or timing condition — give it its own paragraph.

**Flavor exposition:** 40–55 words, 2–3 sentences. First sentence
names the condition or world fact. Second pivots to player relevance.
Third adds danger, distinction, or cultural specificity if needed.

**Item descriptions:** 40–55 words, 2–4 sentences. Material first,
function second, limitation or distinction third.

**Talent descriptions:** 60–100 words acceptable per rank block.
Each sentence must do exactly one job.

**Spell descriptions:** 30–45 words for simple spells, 45–70 for
complex. Format compresses: name → cost → effect → complication.

**Fiction openers and epigraphs:** No target length. Length follows
image density. A four-sentence epigraph with a reversal is longer
by nature. A one-sentence opener that names a place and stops is
shorter. Length follows content, not convention.

**Example scenes:** 3–6 short paragraphs, each 30–50 words.
One paragraph for setup, one for each action, one for consequence.
The example teaches through sequence, not explanation.

### Structural Warning Signals

**Paragraph length creep:** Three consecutive paragraphs each
over 70 words means over-explanation. Check for: re-stated
definitions, embedded rationale, hedging, trailing qualifiers.
Fix: cut the last sentence of each paragraph.

**Sentence count uniformity:** Every paragraph at exactly
3 sentences is AI template behavior. Vary between 2 and 5
by purpose: a definition needs one sentence; a conditional
rule needs three; an inventory list needs one long sentence.

**Heading density:** Roughly 1 heading per 3–5 paragraphs in
rules chapters, 1 per 5–8 in flavor chapters. If a reader must
hold more than two concepts simultaneously without a break, add
a heading.
