# Register Contrastive Analysis

This document renders one narrative situation in each of the six
author registers analyzed in this skill. Reading the six versions
side-by-side shows exactly what register selection changes.

The six renderings are not blended. Each is written to demonstrate
the primary techniques of that register only, applied faithfully.

---

## The Situation

*A character arrives at a village after dark. Something is wrong.
The gate is shut. No dogs. No fire-light at any window.*

The situation is the same in all six renderings. The character,
the village, and the discovery are the same narrative material.
What changes is: point of entry, sentence architecture, what the
narrator notices first, what is withheld, what is given immediately,
and where the rendering stops.

---

## Register 1: Ursula K. Le Guin

*The gate was a timber frame with a cross-bar, the kind
that took two people to lift. She had passed through it
twice this year, and both times it had stood open from
before dawn. It was shut now. The bar was down.*

*She stood on the road and listened. The Kend kept dogs
in the lower yard — the whole village kept dogs — and at
this hour they should have been at the fence. She heard
nothing from the lower yard. She heard nothing from anywhere.*

*There was a custom here, as in most villages on the road:
a fire at the gate-post for late travellers, a sign that
the gate-warden would answer a knock. No fire. She looked
at the gate-post and saw that the bracket where the lantern
hung was empty and the bracket itself was new iron, put
in within the last season. Someone had replaced the bracket
and then not used it.*

*She did not knock. She went around the east side of the
palisade until she found the place where the stakes had
thinned from timber-cutting. The gap was not a gate. It was
a fact about this village's winter.*

---

**Analysis — Le Guin register:**

Techniques applied:

- **Thick description (Custom Over Drama):** The gate's mechanism
  is described before the dramatic implication. The reader learns
  how the gate works before learning it is wrong.
- **Absence as Presence:** No dogs. No fire. The new iron bracket
  with nothing in it.
- **Specific custom:** The fire at the gate-post is a named practice,
  not general atmosphere. It exists independently of the arrival.
- **Ecological specificity:** The gap in the palisade as evidence
  of how this village uses its timber — practical, not symbolic.
- **Unstated lesson:** No commentary on what "this means." The
  character acts.
- **Sentence length:** 14-22 words, unhurried qualifications that
  advance rather than hedge.

The Le Guin rendering enters through the gate's mechanism and
stays at the level of social practice and physical observation.
It never names what the danger is, or whether there is danger.

## Register 2: Bernard Cornwell

*The gate was closed. I had not seen it closed before, not
at this hour, not in summer.*

*The dogs were quiet. That was worse than the gate.*

*I moved along the palisade to the east where the stakes were
lower. I went over in three moves and came down on the inside
into mud that came over my boot-top. The lower yard. I waited.
No sound from the houses. The shutters were all down. I could
see light at one of them — a candle's worth of light coming
through the join — which meant people inside but no one
coming out.*

*The granary door was open. Not ajar. Open, and swinging.*

*I knew what that meant, or I thought I did. I had been wrong
before.*

---

**Analysis — Cornwell register:**

Techniques applied:

- **First-person survivor voice:** Retrospective certainty from
  an established narrator. "I had not seen it closed before"
  grounds the wrongness in personal experience, not in abstract
  wrongness.
- **Tactical geography:** The fence assessment (lower stakes on
  the east) is navigational, not descriptive. The narrator is
  solving a practical problem.
- **Death without ceremony (implied):** The open granary door
  in a single sentence, then the short sentence of implication.
- **Sentence length:** 7-14 words, shortest in the tradition.
  "The dogs were quiet. That was worse than the gate." Two
  sentences, six words, more weight than a paragraph of description.
- **Deliberate withholding:** "I had been wrong before" — the
  narrator does not supply the conclusion. The reader is ahead
  of the narrator here, which is unusual for Cornwell and creates
  specific dread.
- **Temperature/proprioception:** Mud over the boot-top is the
  one sensory detail, and it is specific and physical.

The Cornwell rendering enters through the narrator's prior
experience and stays at the level of tactical problem-solving.

## Register 3: Andrzej Sapkowski

*She had heard of Crestwood from a ferryman at Ashford, who
had said the village was unremarkable except for the mill,
which belonged to a man named Felden who had married poorly
and was still paying for it. She had not asked further. It
had not seemed relevant.*

*The gate was barred. No fire. The dogs — she had counted
four on her last pass through — were not visible or audible.*

*She waited on the road for long enough to be sure, then
moved to the east side of the palisade. The stakes there
had been repaired recently with pine rather than oak, which
told her something about the timing. She noted this.*

*There were several explanations for a barred gate and quiet
dogs. Most of them were ordinary. One of them was not. She
ranked the explanations in order of probability and began
working toward the least probable, because that was the one
that had brought her here.*

---

**Analysis — Sapkowski register:**

Techniques applied:

- **Investigation before violence:** The entry is not dramatic.
  It is methodical. The narrator is assembling evidence, not
  reacting.
- **The False Threshold:** The ferryman's gossip about the miller
  is the misdirection — it's mentioned specifically because it
  seems irrelevant, and that irrelevance is itself a technique.
- **Price mechanism implied:** "Married poorly and was still
  paying for it" — the economy of the world in a single clause.
- **Physical evidence as information:** Pine vs. oak in the
  palisade repair is not atmosphere. It is data about timing.
- **Dry wit as ethical distance:** "She noted this." — the
  narrator's understatement at the end of an observation signals
  the register.
- **Monster-as-Contract framing (setup):** The final paragraph
  structures the narrative around probability ranking and the
  worst case. Investigation logic, not horror logic.
- **Sentence length:** 12-18 words, mid-range, ends on a specific
  action or concrete noun.

The Sapkowski rendering enters through prior intelligence and
stays at the level of methodical assessment. The danger is
already categorized; the work is confirming the category.

## Register 4: K.J. Parker

*I had been to Crestwood three times over the years. The first
time, the mill was running and the gate was open until midnight.
The second time, the price of flour had gone up by a quarter
and the miller was explaining this to everyone who would listen.
The third time — now, tonight — the gate was barred and the
dogs were silent, which represented an unexpected development
in what had been a fairly predictable situation.*

*I went over the palisade on the east side. The stakes there
were pine, which someone had substituted for the original oak,
presumably because pine was cheaper or more available. The pine
was already splitting. It would need replacing in two years.
This would create a maintenance liability for the village of
approximately twelve man-days. I noted this without any particular
sense of relevance.*

*The granary door was open. I looked at it for a while.*

*The granary contained, by my estimate, enough rye to feed
the village through a normal winter with a small surplus. The
door being open meant that either the grain was gone or that
the people who would normally close it were not in a position
to do so. Both possibilities were, from a certain perspective,
equally interesting.*

---

**Analysis — K.J. Parker register:**

Techniques applied:

- **Unreliable pragmatist narrator:** The narrator quantifies
  the palisade maintenance liability (twelve man-days) in the
  middle of a scene of potential massacre. This is not comic
  relief; it is the narrator's genuine cognitive style — and
  it is horrifying.
- **Economics as plot engine:** Flour price going up. Granary
  contents estimated. Both sides of the disaster are expressed
  in economic terms.
- **Technical procedure as character:** Palisade assessed, not
  for defence, but for maintenance cost.
- **Dark comedy through incongruity:** "Equally interesting" in
  the final line is the Parker register's signature move — the
  terrible thing described in the vocabulary of mild intellectual
  curiosity.
- **The Calculated Understatement:** "I looked at it for a while."
  This is the Parker pause — the moment where the narrator notices
  something significant and the register refuses to escalate.
- **Sentence length:** 8-16 words declarative chains; the
  maintenance liability sentence is longer by design, accumulating
  the absurdity.

The Parker rendering enters through prior economic observation
and never loses that perspective even as evidence of disaster
accumulates.

## Register 5: John Gwynne

*The gate was barred. Sigrid heard the bar before she saw it —
the absence of sound on the other side, the wrong quiet of a
place that should have had dogs and firelight and the smell
of a cooking fire.*

*Her hand found her spear-butt without her telling it to.*

*She went east along the palisade. The moon was behind cloud.
The stakes were close-set here, close enough that she had to
turn sideways and breathe out to fit between them. The cold was
in her chest now, deeper than her skin, the kind of cold that
came when the blood was moving fast and the sweat was drying.*

*The lower yard was empty. The goats were in the pen, which
was wrong — goats moved when something came through, they
pressed to the back and made noise. These were still. One
of them was looking at the granary door.*

*The granary door was open.*

*Sigrid looked at the goat. The goat looked at the granary
door. She thought about her brother, who had taught her to
watch where animals looked.*

---

**Analysis — Gwynne register:**

Techniques applied:

- **Body-first narration:** Hand finds the spear-butt "without
  her telling it to" — the body responds before the mind names
  the threat.
- **Proprioception as primary sense:** Cold in the chest, deeper
  than skin, sweat drying. Physical state as the dominant sense.
- **Grief as physical (foreshadowed):** The brother appears
  through a learned lesson — a memory delivered as present-tense
  practical knowledge, not as a flashback. The grief is implied
  by the past tense.
- **The named observation:** "the wrong quiet" — Gwynne names
  the category of wrongness before the specific facts, which
  is his oral-culture tell.
- **Ecological attention through oath-culture lens:** The goat
  watching the granary is a loyalty-equivalent — what an
  animal's attention means to someone trained in that reading.
- **Shield-wall perspective narrowed to sensory horizon:** The
  whole scene happens within earshot and sight-range; nothing
  is narrated from outside the body's immediate perception.
- **Short for action:** "The granary door was open." alone on
  its own paragraph — five words, maximum weight.

The Gwynne rendering enters through sound (or its absence) and
stays inside the body throughout.

## Register 6: Guy Gavriel Kay

*It would later be said that no one saw her arrive at Crestwood
that night, which was true, though not for the reason that
story suggested.*

*The gate was barred. No dogs, no fire. She stood at the edge
of the road in the dark and looked at what the village had
become since she was last here, and thought about the ferryman's
daughter she had sheltered there in the autumn, who had been
afraid of exactly nothing and had eaten an enormous amount of
bread.*

*There are nights that hold still the way water holds still
before it freezes. This was one of them. The dark did not press.
It simply was, all the way to the treeline, absolute and complete.*

*She went over the palisade. The gate's bar was iron, she saw
from the inside — new iron, which had not been iron the last
time she was here. Someone had been afraid enough to spend that
silver. It had not been enough.*

*At the granary, she stopped.*

*The door was open. She would remember this moment, years later,
as the last moment before she understood what the Mist had left
behind in this part of the world. She did not understand it yet.
She would.*

---

**Analysis — Kay register:**

Techniques applied:

- **Elegiac omniscience:** "It would later be said... which was
  true, though not for the reason..." — the narrator stands in
  the future and looks back. The scene is already historical.
- **The "Last Time" Temporal Marker:** "The last moment before
  she understood" — explicit, deliberate, melancholy. Used only
  once in the rendering.
- **Sacred Geography (implied):** The village is not described
  as scenery; it is described as "what the village had become,"
  which assumes the narrator's prior relationship with its previous
  state. The geography holds a before-state.
- **Long Memory as texture:** The ferryman's daughter detail —
  a single specific person, named only as "the ferryman's daughter,"
  who "ate an enormous amount of bread." This is Kay's technique:
  an individual, specific, small, rendered in the middle of
  historical weight, to make the historical weight personal.
- **Parallel construction as emotional intensifier:** "It had not
  been enough." — the sentence is short because it is landing,
  not elaborating.
- **Death in subordinate clauses (implied):** What the granary
  door means is not stated. The temporal marker ("she would
  understand") is the weight. The granary door is the subordinate
  clause.
- **Sentence length:** 18-35 words for the reflective sentences;
  the short landing sentences are 5-8 words and heavily weighted.

The Kay rendering enters through future retrospect and stays in
the register of accumulated historical weight. It is the only
rendering that explicitly knows more than the character knows.

## Cross-Register Notes

**What changes across all six:**

- **Point of entry:** Le Guin enters through mechanism; Cornwell
  through prior experience; Sapkowski through intelligence gathered;
  Parker through economic history; Gwynne through body sensation;
  Kay through retrospective knowledge.

- **What is noticed first:** Le Guin: the gate's mechanism.
  Cornwell: the silence of the dogs. Sapkowski: the ferryman's
  gossip. Parker: the price of flour. Gwynne: the wrong quiet
  (sound). Kay: the historical future of the moment.

- **The granary door:** All six use the open granary door as the
  key image. Le Guin describes the gap in the palisade as the
  way in. Cornwell notes the granary immediately and draws a
  provisional conclusion. Sapkowski calculates probability.
  Parker estimates contents and maintenance costs. Gwynne sees
  it through the goat. Kay makes it a temporal marker.

- **What is withheld:** Le Guin withholds the nature of the
  danger; Cornwell withholds his conclusion. Sapkowski withholds
  the worst probability by ranking it last. Parker withholds
  emotional response. Gwynne withholds everything after the
  granary door. Kay withholds the revelation by deferring it
  to the future.

- **Register for this scene in the manuscript:** Le Guin-primary
  for the approach and discovery; Cornwell-secondary for the
  physical entry; Sapkowski-secondary if the character is an
  investigator. Kay register is wrong here — save it for the
  retrospective account of what was found.
