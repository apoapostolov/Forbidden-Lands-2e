# Worked Examples: Wrong vs. Correct

This document extends the composite-voice worked examples with pairs
specifically targeting the highest-frequency AI patterns in genre
fantasy prose. Each pair names the pattern, shows what an LLM
generates by default, and shows what a human writer produces instead.

Pattern numbers reference `anti-ai-humanizer.md`.

The four composite-voice examples (village introduction, monster
opening, combat opening, grief scene) are not repeated here. This
document covers different scenes and different failure modes.

---

## Format

Each entry:

- Names the pattern (with number)
- Shows WRONG (plausible LLM output)
- Shows CORRECT (human-quality fix)
- Adds one analysis sentence

---

## Example 1 — Pattern 1: Significance Inflation

**Scene:** A character arrives at a ruined temple.

**WRONG:**

*The ancient temple loomed before her, its crumbling stones radiating
an immense and primal power. The sacred ruins whispered of a
legendary past, vast and mysterious, filled with the profound weight
of ages gone by. She felt the deep significance of the place settle
around her like a shroud.*

**CORRECT:**

*The roof had gone in three places. Grass had taken the floor
through the cracks and was working on the altar stone. Someone
had used the north wall as a fire-shelter at some point — there
was ash, and a hole in the stone where the smoke had worn
through. She did not know when. A long time ago.*

Analysis: The "ancient, primal, profound" adjectives are replaced
with specific evidence of time passing — grass, ash, a hole worn
by smoke — and the sense of age accumulates from those physical
facts without being announced.

## Example 2 — Pattern 7: Em-Dash Cluster

**Scene:** Approaching a village at night.

**WRONG:**

*The village ahead — dark against the tree-line — seemed quiet, the
silence broken only by the wind — a bad sign — and the distant
sound of dogs that had gone still. She pressed forward — there
was no going back now — hand on the hilt.*

**CORRECT:**

*The village was dark. The dogs had gone quiet. She stood at the
edge of the road and counted the houses she could see — three
lit, four dark, one with a shutter banging loose. She moved
toward the loose shutter.*

Analysis: Five em-dashes are replaced with short sentences; the
abruptness lives in the periods, not the dashes, and the specific
detail (three lit, four dark, one loose shutter) replaces the
atmosphere gesturing.

## Example 3 — Pattern 8: Rule-of-Three

**Scene:** Describing a mercenary.

**WRONG:**

*He was a large man, scarred, and dangerous-looking. His eyes
were cold, calculating, and utterly without mercy. He carried
a sword, an axe, and a long knife at his belt.*

**CORRECT:**

*He had a scar from his left ear to the corner of his mouth
that had healed badly, pulling that side of his face down.
He kept one hand resting on his thigh, close to the knife.*

Analysis: Three adjective-clusters and a three-item inventory
are replaced with two specific physical observations that place
the character's body and imply experience without listing traits.

## Example 4 — Pattern 9: Negative-Then-Positive Clarification

**Scene:** Describing a keep.

**WRONG:**

*It was not a fortress. It was a statement. Not a military
installation, but a declaration of presence. It was not
built to defend. It was built to intimidate.*

**CORRECT:**

*The walls were twice the height they needed to be for defence.
The gates were iron-banded oak. The banner over the gatehouse
had been new within the last year — you could tell from the
colour. Someone was spending.*

Analysis: Four negation-correction pairs are cut; the same
implied meaning (it's about display, not defence) arrives
through specific observations about proportionality, fresh
ironwork, and a new banner.

## Example 5 — Pattern 17: Topic-Sentence Paragraph

**Scene:** A village's relationship to the local temple.

**WRONG:**

*The village's relationship with the Rust Brotherhood was
complex and fraught. The Brothers provided essential services,
including mediation of disputes and care for the sick, but they
also collected tithes and expected compliance with their
restrictions. This created a tension that pervaded every aspect
of daily life.*

**CORRECT:**

*The Rust Brother who wintered here was named Aldric and he
slept in the granary and ate with the Karrens. He collected
the Harvest tithe and the Spring offering and kept a ledger
that no one else was allowed to read. The headwoman said he
was fair. Her tone when she said it did not confirm this.*

Analysis: The thesis-elaboration-landing structure is replaced
with three specific observed facts (where he sleeps, what he
collects, the locked ledger) plus one behavioral observation
(headwoman's tone), and the reader assembles the tension without
being told it exists.

## Example 6 — Pattern 19: Thematic Restatement Ending

**Scene:** End of a day's hard travel.

**WRONG:**

*As they made camp that night, exhausted and footsore, they
understood that the road ahead would test them further. But
they had endured this day, and they would endure what came
next. The journey had shown them something about their own
resilience.*

**CORRECT:**

*Tilde found water in a hollow two hundred paces off the road.
She did not mention it until she had checked for camp space
and a clear sight-line to the road in both directions. Then she
said: "Water." They made camp.*

Analysis: The thematic closing (endurance, resilience, the lesson
of the journey) is replaced with a specific competent action and
a single word of dialogue — the lesson, if any, is in how she
checks before she speaks.

## Example 7 — Pattern 23: Emotion Announcement

**Scene:** A character learning their village has been burned.

**WRONG:**

*A wave of pure anguish washed over her as the truth of what
she was being told sank in. She felt a profound sense of loss,
mixed with anger and a deep, overwhelming grief. Tears welled
in her eyes.*

**CORRECT:**

*She asked him to say it again. He said it again. She turned
away and looked at the far wall and kept her eyes there. She
asked him if he was sure. He was sure. She asked if anyone
had gotten out. He named three people. She repeated the names
in the wrong order and then stopped talking.*

Analysis: Every named emotion is removed; the same interior
reality is shown through a specific sequence of questions and
a behavioral tell (repeating the names wrong) that no narrator
label could achieve.

## Example 8 — Pattern 22: Backstory Dump on First Mention

**Scene:** First appearance of a local elder.

**WRONG:**

*The elder was a woman named Signe who had been the headwoman
for thirty years, ever since her husband Vorkel died in the
fighting at the Iron Ford, where she had first demonstrated
her leadership by rallying the survivors and leading them back
to the village, a feat that was still spoken of by the older
generation. She had governed firmly but fairly ever since,
weathering two droughts, a bandit raid, and the long winter
of 1085 that had killed a third of the livestock.*

**CORRECT:**

*The headwoman — Signe, someone said — came in without sitting
down. She stood at the end of the table and looked at each
of them in turn, the way you look at a number that does not
add up.*

Analysis: Thirty years of exposition is replaced with a single
physical arrival and one image of her manner; the backstory can
arrive in fragments over subsequent scenes.

## Example 9 — Pattern 28: Expressive Dialogue Tag

**Scene:** A tense negotiation.

**WRONG:**

*"Three silvers," he said, his voice hardened by years of
difficult trades and a lifetime of seeing people try to
cheat him.
"Two," she replied, her tone carefully neutral, concealing
the urgency beneath.
"Two and a half," he answered, his eyes calculating, trying
to read how badly she needed this.*

**CORRECT:**

*"Three silvers."
"Two."
He looked at her hands. She had put them flat on the table.
"Two and a half," he said.*

Analysis: All three expressive tags are removed; the character
observation (he looks at her hands) replaces the tag-emotion
and tells us more about both of them than the elaborated versions did.

## Example 10 — Pattern 33: Generic Atmospheric Opener

**Scene:** Dawn before a march.

**WRONG:**

*The morning was grey and cold, a heavy mist hanging low over
the fields as the first pale light crept over the horizon.
It was the kind of morning that made the world feel ancient
and full of secrets.*

**CORRECT:**

*Rolf was the first up, which surprised no one. He had the
fire going and a pot on it before the sky had any colour.
The smell reached the sleeping blankets before the light did.*

Analysis: The detached atmospheric description is replaced with
a specific person doing a specific thing; the same cold grey dawn
is implied by the fire and the smell, and we immediately know
something about Rolf.

## Example 11 — Pattern 37: Village-as-Set

**Scene:** A stranger's first view of a village.

**WRONG:**

*The village of Ashfield was the kind of place that
adventurers often stopped at on their way somewhere more
important. The locals were wary of strangers but not
hostile, and might be persuaded to share information
about the surrounding region. There were rumors of strange
happenings in the nearby forest.*

**CORRECT:**

*The first thing she noticed was that the gate was barred
from the inside in the middle of the afternoon. The second
thing was that all the washing had been taken in, though
the sky was clear. The third thing was the smell.*

Analysis: The adventure-module setup (wary locals, regional
information, nearby rumors) is replaced with three specific
observations that imply a village in crisis — on their own terms,
not in terms of what use they might be to a traveler.

## Example 12 — Pattern 39: Sentimental Resolution

**Scene:** After a burial.

**WRONG:**

*It was a hard day, and a sad one. But as the sun finally
broke through the clouds in the late afternoon, casting
a warm golden light over the fresh earth of the grave,
there was a sense that Torkel would have appreciated
the gesture. He had always loved this place. They found
comfort in that.*

**CORRECT:**

*The grave was done by midday. Birna said they should
say something. No one said anything. They stood there
for a while. The soil was clay here and would not settle
flat — it would mound up and stay mounded. Birna touched
the wooden marker and then pulled her hand back. They
walked back to the village.*

Analysis: The warm light, the consoling sense, and the
sentimental summary are replaced with physical discomfort
(clay soil that mounds), a failed attempt at ceremony, and
a physical withdrawal — the loss stays present.

## Example 13 — Pattern 43: Lesson Stated Directly

**Scene:** Close of a monster negotiation.

**WRONG:**

*As they walked away from the creature's lair, she
realized she had learned something important. The
monster had not been a monster at all — not really.
It had been a victim of circumstances, like all of
them. She understood now that the world was more
complicated than it seemed.*

**CORRECT:**

*They walked out while the thing was still watching
from the dark. Mikael did not look back. She looked
back once, at the entrance — the shape of it, the
size of the bones near the threshold. She had agreed
to keep the children away from the east pasture
from now until the spring. That was the arrangement.*

Analysis: The lesson ("the world is complicated, monsters
aren't really monsters") is cut; the moral content arrives
through the specific terms of the agreement (east pasture,
spring) and the detail of bones near the threshold — the
reader does the work.

## Quick Diagnostic

Use this checklist against any draft passage to catch
the highest-frequency patterns before submission:

- [ ] Any adjectives that inflate without specifying?
  *(ancient, primal, vast, profound, sacred, immense)* — Pattern 1
- [ ] More than one em-dash per page? — Pattern 7
- [ ] Any three-item lists? Reduce to one specific. — Pattern 8
- [ ] Any "It is not X, it is Y" pairs? Collapse. — Pattern 9
- [ ] Paragraphs that open with topic sentences?
  *(The village's relationship was..., The situation was...)* — Pattern 17
- [ ] Any scene endings that state the theme?
  *(she understood, he had learned, they would endure)* — Pattern 19
- [ ] Any named emotions?
  *(she felt fear, grief washed over, joy surged)* — Pattern 23
- [ ] Any backstory block on first character mention? — Pattern 22
- [ ] Any expressive dialogue tags?
  *(he said, his voice heavy with; she replied, her eyes shining)* — Pattern 28
- [ ] Atmospheric scene opener disconnected from a body? — Pattern 33
- [ ] Village described as a location for adventurers? — Pattern 37
- [ ] Warm or consoling close after a difficult scene? — Pattern 39
- [ ] Final sentence that states what the reader should conclude? — Pattern 43
