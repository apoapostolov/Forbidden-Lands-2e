# Dialogue Mechanics

## Overview

This reference specifies how dialogue works in the six-author tradition
this skill synthesizes. Dialogue is not transcription of speech; it is
the compressed representation of social interaction under specific cultural
constraints. Each author models a distinct social register. The Forbidden
Lands manuscript draws from each depending on scene type.

The core rule: **every significant exchange has something at stake that
is not being said directly.** Dialogue that simply transfers information
is narration in quotation marks. Real dialogue is negotiation, obligation,
evasion, or declaration — and the reader always knows which one.

---

## Author Dialogue Profiles

### Sapkowski: The Negotiation

Sapkowski's dialogue is a game of incomplete information. Both speakers
want something; both are withholding something. Every line is a move.
The dialogue reads as conversation; it functions as exchange of leverage.

**Characteristics:**

- Deliberate incompleteness. Important things are not named. The topic
  is addressed from around its edges.
- Register difference. Peasants speak with superstition and indirection.
  Nobles speak formally. Mages use technical precision and contempt.
  Witchers ask questions and make short statements.
- The question-as-move. Geralt asks questions that appear neutral
  but are designed to extract information without conceding that he
  doesn't have it.
- Silence as acknowledgment. "I'm aware of it" acknowledges a subject
  without confirming details. The interlocutor must decide whether
  to press.

**Example pattern:**

> "The miller's girl has been in the wood again," the headman said.
>
> "How many nights?"
>
> "Three. She comes back clean. No marks."
>
> Geralt said nothing.
>
> "The other children won't go near her now," the headman said,
> which was the thing he had been trying to say for five minutes.

The fifth line is the one that matters. Everything before it was
working toward it.

### Kay: The Declaration

Kay's dialogue is for moments of historical weight. Characters
who speak in Kay are aware of the significance of what they are
saying. The words are chosen carefully; the formality is deliberate.

**Characteristics:**

- Elevated register throughout. Even informal exchanges carry weight.
- Long silences are present but not marked. The beat is in the prose.
- The declaration as formal act. When a Kay character makes a
  commitment, they are making it for the record.
- Understatement of terrible things. "She is dead" without elaboration.

**When to use:** Oath-making, farewell, first sight of a ruined sacred
place, confrontation of historical injustice.

**When not to use:** Daily village life, minor transactions, humor.

---

### Parker: The Technical Exchange

Parker's dialogue is between people who are focused on the task.
The emotional content of their situation is not suppressed; it is
simply not the current agenda. The current agenda is grain supply
or siege timeline or the tensile strength of the available rope.

**Characteristics:**

- Specification and quantification. What exactly, how many, by when.
- Functional brevity. The pleasantries are either absent or compressed
  to single words.
- Dark comedy through incongruity. The technical conversation is held
  in the middle of a catastrophe. Neither speaker acknowledges the
  catastrophe directly; both are aware of it completely.
- Institutional register. Characters speak as representatives of roles,
  not as individuals. "The guild requires..." / "The ordnance's position
  is..."

**Example pattern:**

> "How many dead?"
>
> "Forty-seven confirmed. Some of the ones in the river we won't
> be sure about until spring."
>
> "Right. So we're at four hundred and twelve functional, if Tessell's
> wound closes properly."
>
> "Tessell's wound isn't going to close properly."
>
> "Four hundred and eleven, then."

The grief is present in the precision of the accounting. Neither
character names it.

---

### Le Guin: The Culturally Specific

Le Guin's dialogue is embedded in specific cultural structures.
People speak within the constraints of their social role and their
cultural obligations. What they can say to whom, in what form,
is determined by kinship structure, age, debt, and sacred obligation.

**Characteristics:**

- Custom governs form. There are specific forms of address, specific
  moments when speech is appropriate, specific silences that are
  required.
- The outsider misreads. A character from outside the culture hears
  the words and misses the social content.
- What is not said is as legible as what is said, to those who
  know the culture.
- Proverbs and ritual phrases are specific to the culture, not
  generic.

**Application:** When writing village dialogue, establish one cultural
constraint that shapes the exchange. What can this person say to this
other person, given their relative positions? What can they not say?

---

### Cornwell: The Direct Register

Cornwell's warriors say what they mean. Tactical situations require
clarity; social hierarchies are expressed through order and compliance,
not through political language. The lower rank obeys; the complaint
happens after, between equals, quietly.

**Characteristics:**

- Short exchanges. Orders, confirmations, questions, answers.
- Dark humor as comradeship. Between equals, the jokes are dark
  and specific.
- Retrospective irony from Uhtred. The narrator knows how this
  ended; occasional dry comment on past self's certainty.
- The insult is specific and personal. Not general contempt; the
  specific fault.

**Example pattern:**

> "Can we hold the ditch?" Osferth asked.
>
> "Probably not," I said. "But we're going to try anyway."
>
> He considered this. "Right."

The dialogue is functional. Two lines. Both characters understand
the situation fully. The reader understands it from what they say
and from what they don't contest.

---

### Gwynne: The Honor Culture

Gwynne's dialogue is shaped by oath culture and honor accounting.
Speech is formal where formality matters: oaths, challenges, acceptance
of obligations. Informal speech between warriors is physically direct
and humor-adjacent. The distinction between formal and informal
registers is itself a social signal.

**Characteristics:**

- Oaths are specific and binding. When a character swears, they say
  exactly what they swear.
- Challenges are ritualized. The form matters; breaking the form
  is itself an offense.
- Informal warrior speech is blunt, specific, physically grounded.
- Proverbs and saying-phrases are offered as proof or warning.
  "As the old saying goes" is an appeal to authority.

## Cross-Cutting Dialogue Rules

### Rule 1: The Default Tag Is "Said"

Every attribution should default to "said." The expressive tag
(shouted, murmured, scoffed, breathed) is an AI tell (Pattern 28).
When the register of the speech is clear from content, no tag is
needed. When a tag is needed, "said" carries it.

**The action beat instead of the emotive tag:**

> "You knew," she said. ← acceptable
>
> "You knew," she said, her voice breaking. ← weak; state it differently
>
> She sat down before she said it. "You knew." ← strong; action
> carries the register

---

### Rule 2: Dialogue That Explains Is Not Dialogue

The "as you know, Bob" pattern — where characters explain things
both of them already know for the reader's benefit — is the single
most common dialogue fail (Pattern 29). Every character in the
conversation already knows their world. They do not explain it
to each other.

The test: would this character say this to this other character in
this situation? If the only reason for the line is reader orientation,
cut it and find another way to convey the information.

---

### Rule 3: Register Is Social Position

Characters speak in the register appropriate to their social position
relative to the person they are addressing. The village headman
speaks differently to the Witcher than to the dockhand. Both
registers are maintained consistently throughout the exchange.

The registers that exist in this manuscript's world:

- **Supplicant register:** hedged, indirect, full of deference
  markers. "If it please you..." / "I don't know if this is my
  place to say..."
- **Peer register:** direct, shorthand, shared assumption of equal
  knowledge
- **Authority register:** declarative, specific, expects compliance
- **Sacred register:** formulaic, ritual phrase, specific conditions
  for when it is used
- **Negotiation register:** formally courteous, specific about terms,
  careful about commitment

---

### Rule 4: Perfect Grammar Is a Social Marker

Characters who speak in complete, grammatically perfect sentences
are either educated, performing status, or AI-generated (Pattern 30).
Ordinary speech is incomplete. It trails off. It assumes the listener
can complete the sentence. It uses forms that are specific to region
and class.

This does not require phonetic spelling, which reads as contemptuous
toward the characters. It requires incomplete constructions and
informal grammar without marking them as defective.

> "She went to the mill? Last night?"
>
> "Aye. Or I thought. Couldn't say for certain."

The grammar is not marked; it is simply not formal.

---

### Rule 5: Silence Is Dialogue

What is not said carries as much weight as what is. Le Guin and
Sapkowski both use deliberate silences — the pause before answering,
the question that is not asked, the acknowledgment that does not
confirm.

Mark silence through:

- Action beats that occur where a response would be expected
- A character answering a different question than the one asked
- A response that addresses the surface but not the substance
- Physical response in place of verbal ("She looked at her hands.")

---

### Rule 6: When to Skip Dialogue Entirely

Not every exchange needs to be rendered in dialogue. Options:

- **Summary:** "They negotiated the contract for an hour. Geralt
  left with twelve marks and the promise of silence."
- **Implication:** "He came back with new information." (The reader
  understands that a conversation happened.)
- **Reported speech:** "She told him what the miller had said, which
  was not the same as what the miller had done."

Dialogue is expensive — it slows pace and requires careful management.
Use it for the moments that need to be experienced, not summarized.

## Dialogue for Specific Scene Types

### Peasant-to-Outsider

The peasant addressing a stranger (which in this manuscript often
means addressing the player characters) uses the supplicant register
combined with specific evasions. They know more than they are saying.
They are deciding, throughout the conversation, how much to reveal
and at what cost to themselves.

The evasion pattern in peasant speech:

- Names things indirectly ("the thing in the wood" rather than
  naming the creature)
- Offers partial information and waits to see what the outsider
  already knows
- Uses religious evasion ("the gods know" / "what happens happens")
  to avoid committing to a statement that might be wrong
- Defers to absent authority ("you'd have to ask the headwoman")

---

### Authority-to-Petitioner

A character in authority addresses a petitioner with controlled
information release. They rarely refuse directly; they redirect,
delay, and impose conditions.

Pattern:

1. Acknowledge the petition formally
2. Name the complication (real or constructed)
3. Name the condition under which help might be possible
4. End with a statement that is technically a door left open but
   functionally a refusal

---

### Warrior-to-Warrior

Between characters of the same martial culture, the dialogue is
dense with shared reference and shorthand. They do not explain their
world to each other. The exchange is either tactical or relational.

Tactical: "The ford. Dawn. Eight men on the far side?"
Relational: humor, insult, the specific forms of acknowledgment
that serve as expressions of trust and affection.

---

### Negotiation

Negotiation dialogue has two simultaneous registers: the polite
formal surface and the exchange of actual positions. The reader
should be able to track both.

Pattern (Sapkowski model):

- Both parties establish their formal positions (politely)
- Both parties probe for information they do not have (indirectly)
- One party makes a move that changes the terrain (the price named,
  the information released, the refusal stated)
- The other party adjusts (accepting, countering, or declining)
- The conversation ends with a new situation, not the same as
  when it began

---

## Dialogue Fails to Avoid

**The Recap Line:**
A character summarizes what just happened for a character who was
present. "So what you're saying is, the creature has been here for
decades and nobody ever challenged it?" Characters who were present
do not recap for each other. This is for the reader's benefit only;
the reader doesn't need it.

**The Emotional Declaration:**
"I am afraid." / "I love you." / "I don't know if I can do this."
Named emotion in dialogue is flat. The emotion is present in the
physical behavior and in the subtext of what is said around it.
Characters can name their emotions occasionally; they cannot do it
often or the effect is gone.

**The Philosophy Break:**
A character pausing the scene to explain the moral or philosophical
implications of the situation. The moral framework is present in
the action; it does not need to be verbalized. Characters who explain
their ethics while the situation requires decision are AI-generated.

**The Perfectly Timed Revelation:**
Information delivered at exactly the right narrative moment,
for exactly the right purpose, in exactly the right words.
Real information arrives awkwardly, incompletely, in the wrong
order, and the person delivering it is not sure what the most
relevant part is.
