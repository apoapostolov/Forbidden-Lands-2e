# Audit: Politics of the Forbidden Lands and Battles & Sieges

## Scope

This audit examines the new `02-gamemasters-guide/11-politics.md` as the missing precursor layer for `02-gamemasters-guide/12-battles-and-sieges.md`.

The question is not whether Chapter 11 is interesting by itself. The question is whether it does the work that Chapter 12 needed in front of it: turning roads, tribute, legitimacy, oaths, levy, and stored grain into armies that feel earned rather than conjured.

## Verdict

The chapter works.

It gives Chapter 12 the missing political and logistical cause that mass combat needs. The new faction engine explains where armies come from, why settlements comply, how a ruler spends legitimacy to force war through, and why a battle may begin with one side already half-rotten from poor levy, cut roads, thin stores, or hated rule.

More importantly, it does not try to replace Chapter 12. It stops at the correct line. Chapter 11 handles pressure, mustering, coercion, and campaign setup. Chapter 12 still owns battlefield procedure, siege action, troop dice, and hard supply math.

That separation is healthy.

## What combines well

### 1. The escalation ladder is now visible

The new feud track gives a clean ladder from grievance to mustered war. That is exactly what the battle chapter lacked. Armies no longer appear because the GM decided a fight should happen. They appear because a chain of political acts failed, hardened, or were pushed too far.

### 2. Settlement obligation now has a real middle layer

Chapter 12 already knew villages and strongholds could feed war, but it did not say how a lord earned or forced that support. Chapter 11 supplies that answer with settlement status, tribute, protection, vassalage, and occupation.

That makes campaign burden feel like government under strain rather than arbitrary requisition.

### 3. Low attributes produce strong pre-battle color

The most useful compatibility move in the chapter is not the faction sheet itself. It is the instruction that low `Mandate`, `Iron`, `Road`, or `Hearth` should visibly deform the army before Chapter 12 begins.

- low `Mandate` weakens levy quality and willingness
- low `Iron` thins numbers and staying power
- low `Road` delays contingents and spoils concentration
- low `Hearth` weakens supply and repair

This is the right kind of handoff. The politics chapter does not need its own battle engine. It needs to put bruises on the army before the battle engine takes over.

### 4. Mercenary integration is finally contextual

The mercenary chapter already handled what a band is. Chapter 11 gives reasons rulers hire bands, and just as importantly, reasons they should fear hiring them on thin stores or bad legitimacy.

That creates a proper chain:

1. faction cannot rely on willing levy
2. faction hires professionals
3. treasury and stores are strained
4. Chapter 12 inherits an army with better teeth and worse long-term stability

That is excellent pressure.

### 5. Infrastructure now matters before battle, not only during it

Barracks, training grounds, shrines, ferries, roads, bridges, stables, and markets now shape what kind of force can exist in the first place. This is much stronger than treating troop quality as a battle-only statistic.

### 6. The chapter returns cleanly after battle

The new post-war guidance is short, but it is enough. Once Chapter 12 resolves the fighting, Chapter 11 can absorb the result into ruined standing, broken roads, hungry villages, and weakened legitimacy.

That closes the loop properly.

## Remaining design pressure points

These are not fatal problems. They are the places most likely to need table experience.

### 1. Treasury remains partly abstract

Chapter 11 wisely avoids full kingdom bookkeeping, but that means a GM still has to judge when a faction's `Treasury` rating should step down under sustained pay.

This is acceptable for manuscript play, but it is the main place where two tables may diverge. One GM will be harsh. Another will let a rich-looking lord float too long.

### 2. Single-contingent war needs judgment

The chapter now states that one contingent is enough to enter Chapter 12 if it is facing another formed force. That is useful clarity. Still, very small wars will sometimes feel more like an armed expedition than a true battle.

In practice, GMs will still need to choose whether a fight wants Chapter 12, the normal combat rules, or a lighter skirmish treatment.

### 3. Dread-based powers may want future morale texture

The system correctly keeps `basis of rule` mostly political rather than tactical. Still, dread regimes and shrine-raised armies may eventually want more explicit battle morale flavor in Chapter 12.

That is not a hole in this pass. It is simply the clearest future expansion point.

### 4. Coalition war is only lightly implied

The chapter handles vassals, allies, and hired bands well enough, but multi-faction coalitions will still require the GM to lean on Chapter 12's command structure and on the mercenary host rules.

Again, this is workable. It simply is not yet a dedicated subsystem.

### 5. Occupation can become the heaviest campaign loop

This is good design, but it should be said plainly: occupation may generate more long-term friction than open battle. A faction that can win wars but cannot afford occupation will now destroy itself in a believable way.

That is a strength, not a flaw, but it will surprise groups used to bloodless conquest accounting.

## Small fixes that improved the fit

The current draft already includes several clarifications that materially improve the bridge into Chapter 12.

- campaign burden is now stated as a seven-day measure
- cutting supply now has quantified immediate effects
- levy quality now reacts more clearly to low `Mandate`
- the contingent-to-troop handoff is named explicitly
- Chapter 11 now states more clearly that Feud 4 does not automatically mean the battle chapter has begun
- campaign-state recovery is now treated as mostly paused unless the faction pays dearly for relief or repair

These are the right kind of fixes: small, sharp, and bridge-focused.

## Independent recommendation

Do not redesign the chapter before playtesting it.

The current manuscript is already good enough to test in campaign use. The next useful questions are not abstract design questions. They are table questions.

### Playtest questions worth asking

1. How fast does `Mandate` actually collapse under repeated forced collection?
2. Are GMs comfortable deciding when `Treasury` and `Stores` should step down, or do they want a firmer cadence?
3. Does one campaign week of levy service feel right across village sizes and seasons?
4. Do occupation and tribute create the right amount of resentment, or too much?
5. Do mercenary bands feel like a tempting fix with a delayed political cost, as intended?
6. Do low `Road` and low `Hearth` produce enough visible pre-battle pain before the armies meet?
7. At what size do groups naturally prefer Chapter 12 over ordinary combat?

## Final judgment

Chapter 11 now does the job Chapter 12 was waiting for.

It turns army-making into politics, politics into pressure, pressure into burden, and burden into war. That is the right chain. It also keeps each chapter responsible for its own scale: Chapter 11 for the making of conflict, Chapter 12 for its open decision.

That is a strong integration result.
