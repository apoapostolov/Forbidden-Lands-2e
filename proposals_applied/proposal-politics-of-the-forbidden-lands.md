## Part 1: What Chapter 11 Must Do

### 1.1 The Missing Layer

The current manuscript already has three useful scales of play:

- the **fellowship scale**, where adventurers bargain, travel, build standing, and survive
- the **mercenary band scale**, where an armed company must recruit, pay, feed, and discipline itself
- the **army scale**, where hosts fight field battles and sieges

What is still missing is the **political scale between them**.

Chapter 11 should provide that middle layer: not a modern state simulator, and not a generic domain game, but a medieval-fantasy power game for Ravenland. It must answer the practical questions that produce later wars:

- who rules this place, and why are they obeyed?
- what villages, roads, shrines, markets, and strongholds support them?
- what dues can they collect without breaking the land?
- what promises do they owe in return?
- how do they feud, threaten, negotiate, marry, compel, forgive, or betray?
- how do they call men to arms?
- how do they feed them once called?
- when do tensions become raids, musters, sieges, and open war?

Chapter 12 already resolves the battle. Chapter 11 must create the reasons, the burdens, and the armies.

### 1.2 The Chapter's Job Statement

**Politics of the Forbidden Lands** should be the chapter that lets the GM or players run:

- kings with thin authority and hungry retainers
- clan chiefs and warleaders
- cults, shrine-federations, and militant orders
- merchant leagues and road-lords
- free companies and hired captains in permanent service
- raider confederacies and predatory hosts
- demonic, undead, or sorcerous dominions
- any established faction that already exists in the world and now needs rules support

The chapter should explicitly support **existing factions first**. Generation tools are useful, but the core need is to stat and run powers that already stand in the setting.

### 1.3 Design Principles

The subsystem should obey the following principles.

#### Native to Forbidden Lands

Use the game's existing grammar wherever possible:

- four faction attributes
- sixteen faction skills
- five-rank talents
- standing and reputation
- quarter days, weekly burdens, and seasonal recovery
- silver, food, and actual physical logistics
- pushes, banes, and attribute damage

#### Medieval, Not Bureaucratic

Power in Ravenland should come from:

- blood right
- oath and witness
- fear
- cult sanction
- payment
- protection
- hostage-taking
- roads and storehouses
- the visible ability to punish disobedience

It should **not** read like ministries, policy sliders, or abstract nation-state administration.

#### Broad, But Not Spreadsheet-Heavy

The system must cover a faction's whole life without turning every session into ledgers.

A chapter that cannot handle tribute, tolls, oaths, musters, and logistics is too thin.
A chapter that requires accounting every chicken separately is too thick.

#### Clean Handoff to Chapter 12

The chapter must produce the exact things Chapter 12 needs:

- troops
- commanders
- supply lines
- siege capacity
- morale context
- reasons for local villages to support or resist war

### 1.4 Exact Boundary With Adjacent Chapters

This chapter should not replace adjacent systems. It should sit between them.

- **Use `01-corebook/08-journeys.md`** for local standing, hospitality, rumor, petitioning authority, crime, and settlement encounters.
- **Use `01-corebook/09-the-stronghold.md`** for buildings, territory, labor, local infrastructure, and stronghold functions.
- **Use `01-corebook/12-mercenaries-of-forbidden-lands.md`** for one band, one captain, and the economics of a roving armed company.
- **Use this chapter** once a power controls settlements, imposes dues, binds allies, and can field more than one band or war contingent.
- **Use `02-gamemasters-guide/12-battles-and-sieges.md`** once a muster becomes a real army action.

This should be stated plainly in the chapter introduction.

## Part 2: Core Engine Proposal

### 2.1 What a Faction Is

A faction is a durable power in the world. It is not just a named leader. It is:

- a **seat**
- a **basis of rule**
- a **body of dependents**
- a **web of obligations**
- a **means of collection**
- a **means of coercion**
- a **way to turn local support into armed force**

A faction may be a petty crown, a fortified household, a cult, a mercenary host in permanent service, an orc confederacy, a dwarf hold, a trading league, or a demon court.

### 2.2 The Faction Sheet

Every major faction should have a sheet with the following fields.

#### Identity

- Name
- Seat
- Public face or ruling figure
- Type
- Basis of rule
- Current season or campaign state

#### Core Ratings

- 4 faction attributes
- 16 faction skills
- 2–4 faction talents

#### Holdings and Dependents

- Directly ruled settlements
- Protected settlements
- Tributaries
- Vassals or oath-bound lesser factions
- Strongholds, shrines, bridges, ferries, markets, roads, mines, pasture, and other key sites

#### Resources

- Treasury
- Stores
- Available labor
- Available levy
- Professional retainers or house troops
- Mercenary contracts in force

#### Political State

- Reputation in relevant circles
- Standing with important settlements and factions
- Oaths sworn and owed
- Active grievances or feuds
- Claim or strategic aim

### 2.3 Faction Attributes

The faction should still use four governing attributes for ease of play, but it should **not** use the player sheet's names. A faction is not a large adventurer. It is a structure of rule, force, movement, and maintenance.

Using `Strength`, `Agility`, `Wits`, and `Empathy` invites the wrong instinct at the table. The GM starts imagining a kingdom as a person with a bigger sword arm. Chapter 11 should instead give factions their own political and military vocabulary.

| ATTRIBUTE | FACTION-SCALE MEANING |
| --- | --- |
| **Mandate** | The accepted right to command: legitimacy, obedience, oath-weight, prestige, and the willingness of lesser powers to comply |
| **Iron** | Armed weight: levy strength, house troops, coercive force, readiness for violence, and the ability to keep fighting after loss |
| **Road** | Reach beyond the seat: scouts, couriers, patrols, trade movement, redeployment, and how far the faction's pressure can travel |
| **Hearth** | The lived base of power: grain, silver, labor, workshops, stores, repair capacity, and the ability to restore what war has spent |

This is not metaphorical fluff. These ratings should be damaged and tested in play.

### 2.4 Faction Skills

The chapter should keep the coverage of a sixteen-skill engine, but the skills should be native to politics, logistics, coercion, and war. They should not be lifted from the player sheet and explained away afterward.

#### Mandate Skills

| SKILL | FACTION USE |
| --- | --- |
| **Claim** | Blood right, title, law, memory of old boundaries, heraldry, temple sanction, and all arguments for why this power has the right to rule |
| **Decree** | Internal command, appointing stewards, enforcing judgment, compelling subordinates, and turning decisions into obedience |
| **Accord** | Fealty, treaties, hostages, marriage bargains, tribute pacts, peace terms, mercenary retainers, and negotiated submission |
| **Rite** | Coronations, sermons, war proclamations, oath ceremonies, public justice, funerary statecraft, and every performance that makes power visible |

#### Iron Skills

| SKILL | FACTION USE |
| --- | --- |
| **Muster** | Calling levy, gathering retainers, counting available men, compelling attendance, and turning obligation into bodies under arms |
| **Discipline** | Keeping troops in line, preventing desertion, absorbing hardship, holding a garrison together, and surviving the long ugly middle of a campaign |
| **Assault** | Raids, punitive expeditions, coercive violence, hard collection under arms, storming weak places, and using force as a political act |
| **Siegecraft** | Walls, camps, ditches, engines, breaches, sappers, fortified camps, and all the works by which a war becomes positional |

#### Road Skills

| SKILL | FACTION USE |
| --- | --- |
| **Watch** | Patrol nets, frontier warning, scouting screens, road eyes, river watchers, and knowing what moves before it arrives |
| **Intrigue** | Spies, blackmail, forged messages, bribed gatekeepers, quiet killings, deniable pressure, and all hidden work between open acts |
| **Relay** | Couriers, beacon chains, march timing, redeployment, reserve concentration, and how quickly news and orders travel |
| **Traffic** | Caravan protection, toll roads, ferries, bridges, market movement, safe-conduct, and control of the routes that carry wealth and rumor |

#### Hearth Skills

| SKILL | FACTION USE |
| --- | --- |
| **Yield** | Tax, tithe, rent, tribute assessment, weighing what a settlement can give, and taking due without killing the source |
| **Stores** | Granaries, treasuries, supply trains, rationing, draft animals, remounts, pack columns, and the actual keeping of campaign means |
| **Works** | Roads, bridges, mills, walls, repairs, clearing routes, labor drafts, and the physical maintenance of the faction's base |
| **Relief** | Healers, plague control, refugees, burial, resettlement, restoring damaged communities, and rebuilding strength after famine or war |

### 2.5 Faction Talents

Faction talents should be five-rank legacies like character talents, but they represent either the ruling style of the faction or the tradition imposed by its leader.

They should not all be written in the chapter as bland administrative bonuses. They should create recognizable political identities.

Candidate faction legacies:

- **Legacy of the Claimant House** — dynastic legitimacy, succession, oath-weight, named retainers
- **Legacy of the Tribute Throne** — regular dues, tolls, pressure, punitive exactions, tributary webs
- **Legacy of the Mustering Crown** — faster levy calls, reserve officers, discipline, demobilization control
- **Legacy of the Walled Seat** — fortification, repair, roads, siege preparation, war stockpiles
- **Legacy of the Toll Court** — trade reach, safe-conducts, supply movement, merchant influence
- **Legacy of the Sacred Seat** — tithes, sermons, religious courts, zeal, sanctified claims
- **Legacy of the Veiled Court** — spies, blackmail, stolen messages, sabotage, quiet removals
- **Legacy of the Riding Lord** — riders, relays, scouts, huntsmen, aggressive pursuit
- **Legacy of the Moot Seat** — moot authority, oath-helping, weregild, outlawry, precedent
- **Legacy of the Dread Throne** — dread, brittle obedience, harsh collection, exemplary punishments
- **Legacy of the Black Throne** — demonic or undead rule, sorcerous coercion, unnatural musters

The chapter does not need twenty talent paths at first integration. Eight to twelve good ones are enough.

### 2.6 Faction Rolls

Faction actions should use the normal roll structure:

**attribute + skill + asset die + modifiers**

The chapter should define faction-scale asset dice as the equivalent of gear dice. Common sources:

- a stronghold or function relevant to the action
- a trusted named lieutenant personally handling the task
- an allied settlement or sworn helper
- a temple sanction, market charter, or road network
- a hostage compact or favorable standing already in place

A faction should normally gain **one main asset die** for strong support, not a pile of them.

### 2.7 Pushing and Attribute Damage

This chapter should use pushing. It is too native to the engine to abandon.

When a faction pushes:

- banes on base dice damage the relevant faction attribute
- banes on asset dice damage or endanger the asset, worsen standing, or create a grievance

This gives the system real teeth.

Examples:

- A lord pushes a **Yield** roll to wring another season's grain from a protected village. He succeeds, but takes **Hearth** damage. The grain comes in. The land is poorer.
- A cult pushes a **Rite** roll to turn pilgrimage into crusade. It wins bodies, but takes **Mandate** damage as zeal runs ahead of obedience and lesser elders begin to fear the center.
- A raider chief pushes a **Relay** roll to outrun a rival host and seize the ford first. Success brings position; **Road** damage means blown mounts, spent couriers, and gaps in the watch line.

### 2.8 What It Means for a Faction Attribute to Hit Zero

This is the right place for the chapter to feel sharp.

| ATTRIBUTE AT ZERO | RESULT |
| --- | --- |
| **Mandate** | The faction suffers open disobedience. One settlement, vassal, captain, or temple circle immediately refuses command, defects, or demands new terms. |
| **Iron** | The faction cannot impose itself by force. It cannot call further levy, threaten collection, or sustain offensive action until it recovers. |
| **Road** | The faction loses distant reach. It can no longer redeploy quickly, keep pressure on remote ground, or trust its own courier net. Its action contracts toward the seat. |
| **Hearth** | The base begins to fail. Treasury, granary, labor, or repair capacity has given way, and the faction cannot sustain a campaign or rebuild losses until order returns. |

This is much more alive than a generic stress track.

### 2.9 Recovery

Faction attributes should recover slowly and specifically.

- **Mandate** recovers through kept oaths, visible justice, remission of burdens, public mercy, ritual recognition, and victories that prove the ruler still has the right to command.
- **Iron** recovers through replacement, rest, remounts, repaired arms, fresh captains, and ending the kind of hard service that hollows troops out.
- **Road** recovers through patrols, repaired bridges, reopened ferries, rebuilt relay lines, fresh scouts, and time spent securing movement instead of merely using it.
- **Hearth** recovers through harvest, regularized dues, reopened trade, repaired works, relief of damaged settlements, and patient rebuilding of the faction's productive base.

A quiet season should usually recover 1 point in one or two damaged attributes, not all of them automatically.

## Part 3: Political Structure and Obligations

### 3.1 Basis of Rule

Every faction should declare why people accept its authority. This is not flavor only. It tells the GM how the faction collects, what insults it cannot ignore, and what kind of failures damage its **Mandate**.

Recommended bases of rule:

- Blood
- Conquest
- Protection
- Wealth
- Cult sanction
- Old custom
- Oath confederacy
- Sorcerous dread

A petty king, a shrine-cult, and a mercantile road-league should not all break for the same reasons.

### 3.2 Settlement Statuses

The chapter needs a clean table for what kind of bond a faction has with a settlement.

| STATUS | WHAT IT MEANS | WHAT THE SETTLEMENT OWES | WHAT THE FACTION OWES |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ruled** | Direct authority, steward, garrison, or household officer present | regular tax, labor, levy | justice, defense, road or order maintenance |
| **Protected** | Local elder stays in place under pact | tribute, provisions, warning, shelter | protection against outside threat, arbitration, restraint |
| **Tributary** | Pays because resistance is dearer | food, silver, hostages, passage | usually little beyond not being attacked immediately |
| **Vassal** | Not a settlement relation but a lesser power bound upward | military service, attendance, oath obedience | support, legitimacy, aid in feud |
| **Occupied** | Held by force with garrison or terror | whatever can be taken | nothing stable except pressure |
| **Allied** | Independent but bound by common cause | aid when agreed | reciprocal aid and non-aggression |

This single table solves much of the chapter's basic clarity.

### 3.3 Oaths and Political Instruments

The chapter should present a small set of medieval political tools that factions use constantly.

#### Protection Pact

A settlement pays dues because a stronger power promises protection, patrol, and judgment.

#### Fealty Oath

A lesser lord, shrine, clan, or captain binds to a higher one.

#### Hostage Compact

A child, sibling, or trusted subordinate is placed with the stronger side as proof that the bond will hold.

#### Fosterage or Betrothal

A long-term peace device that creates kin-like ties rather than immediate military obedience.

#### Market Charter or Toll Right

Authority over a crossing, road, ferry, or market day becomes a steady political income source.

#### Temple Sanction or Interdict

A cult-backed power can bless, legitimize, shame, or isolate others.

#### Outlawry

A ruler strips a subject or rival of recognized protection. This should be linked to the justice tools already established in `01-corebook/08-journeys.md`.

These instruments should be framed as recurring procedures, not side notes.

### 3.4 Reputation and Standing

This chapter should explicitly lean on the journeys rule that reputation can belong to circles and factions, not only settlements.

That rule already exists. Chapter 11 should adopt it and elevate it.

Use:

- **Faction Reputation** in circles and regions
- **Faction Standing** with important settlements, powers, orders, and kin groups

This gives the chapter a native way to model:

- feared but famous conquerors
- trusted but little-known local lords
- cults respected by the faithful and hated by nearby villages
- mercenary captains welcomed in castles and barred from market towns

### 3.5 Feud Track Between Factions

The mercenary chapter already uses a useful feud ladder. Chapter 11 should scale it up for faction politics instead of inventing a wholly different conflict language.

Suggested feud ladder:

| STEP | STATE | MEANING |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | **Cold Peace** | Tension exists, but neither side is yet moving openly |
| 1 | **Grievance** | Insults, claims, missed dues, or border incidents are active |
| 2 | **Pressure** | Threats, raids, bribes, hostage-taking, and outside mediation begin |
| 3 | **Armed Feud** | Retainers gather, roads are dangerous, supply or tribute is interfered with |
| 4 | **Mustered War** | Open campaign, siege, or major battle becomes imminent |

This gives the chapter a concrete escalation path.

### 3.6 The Faction Turn

The chapter should present a clear procedure, not merely a bag of actions.

The recommended cadence is:

- a **season turn** for normal rule, recovery, diplomacy, and slow pressure
- a **campaign week** once a feud reaches armed movement, levy, or active war footing

#### Season Turn Sequence

Each season, every major faction resolves:

1. **Recover** — recover damaged faction attributes where peace, repair, justice, or relief allow it.
2. **Count Dues** — collect routine taxes, tithes, tolls, and agreed tribute.
3. **Check Obligations** — note oaths due, hostages demanded, vassal service owed, and unresolved grievances.
4. **Choose Political Acts** — usually one major act and one minor act.
5. **Resolve Consequences** — adjust Standing, feud, settlement burden, and readiness.

This keeps the world moving even when no army is in the field.

#### Campaign Week Sequence

Once a faction is in pressure, muster, or campaign state, resolve weekly instead:

1. **Pay and Feed** — determine whether troops, retainers, or hired bands are actually sustained.
2. **Move or Concentrate** — marches, courier runs, escort of stores, redeployment, or raid movement.
3. **Take One Campaign Act** — raid, call additional levy, cut supply, negotiate surrender, reinforce, or invest a place.
4. **Check Burden** — village extraction, attrition, disease, and local resentment.
5. **Escalate or Hand Off** — if armies engage, move into Chapter 12.

This weekly loop is the practical bridge into battle and siege play.

### 3.7 Major and Minor Acts

The chapter should distinguish between what changes the map and what merely supports it.

#### Major Acts

Examples:

- swear fealty
- call levy
- impose new tribute
- hire a mercenary band
- raid a rival's road or granary
- occupy a settlement
- declare truce, peace, or armed feud

#### Minor Acts

Examples:

- send gifts
- dispatch messengers
- hear petitioners
- shift a garrison
- prepare stockpiles
- spread rumor
- issue safe-conduct

This distinction matters because it keeps a faction from doing everything at once while still allowing texture.

## Part 4: Revenue, Tribute, and Burden

### 4.1 The Chapter Must Distinguish Tax From War Exaction

Not all dues are the same.

A stable ruler does not extract from a village the same way an army on campaign does.

The chapter should distinguish:

- **regular dues** — taxes, tithes, tolls, labor, routine supply obligations
- **campaign burdens** — emergency levies, wagon duty, army food, passing-host contributions
- **forced exactions** — punitive tribute, emergency confiscation, or outright looting

This matters because different actions should damage Standing and **Mandate** differently.

### 4.2 Hard Ceiling Already Established in Chapter 12

The proposal should explicitly adopt the existing burden limits in `02-gamemasters-guide/12-battles-and-sieges.md`.

Villages can contribute, per week:

- up to **three times population in supplies** without immediate collapse
- up to **five times population in copper** as the acceptable high limit
- up to **ten times population** only through destructive over-extraction, causing hunger, poverty, resentment, and possible death

This should become the chapter's hard physical ceiling for campaign burdens.

### 4.3 Normal Dues

Regular peacetime dues should usually sit **well below** the Chapter 12 war ceiling.

The chapter does not need a giant economic simulator. It needs a clear principle:

> A settlement that is ruled well can be made to support power for years. A settlement squeezed like an army-base can do so only briefly before it begins to fail.

So the final integrated chapter should recommend that stable dues be set at roughly **half or less of war-time acceptable burden**, adjusted by prosperity, market access, faith structure, and fear.

### 4.4 What Factions Can Collect

The chapter should give a short list of standard dues:

- grain or preserved food
- silver or copper coin
- livestock
- labor days
- wagon or pack service
- ferry and road tolls
- market percentages
- temple tithe
- hostages
- military service
- shelter and passage rights

This lets the chapter cover both mundane and harsh politics without bloating into a trade game.

### 4.5 Consequences of Over-Collection

The subsystem must punish over-collection in the right way.

Likely consequences:

- Standing loss
- Mandate damage
- feud advance
- population flight
- spoilage or starvation
- reduced future levy
- neighboring settlements pre-emptively hiding stores or closing gates

This is how a political chapter becomes a living-world chapter instead of a tax button.

## Part 5: Levies, Retainers, and Musters

### 5.1 Sources of Armed Force

Chapter 11 should divide armed force into clear medieval sources.

#### Household or Retainer Troops

Permanent sworn men, house guards, shrine guards, noble retainers, cult enforcers.

#### Levy

Short-term military obligation drawn from villages, clans, or dependents.

#### Specialists

Hunters, riders, engineers, archers, sappers, surgeons, smiths, priests, beast handlers.

#### Hired Bands

Mercenaries taken from `01-corebook/12-mercenaries-of-forbidden-lands.md`.

#### Fanatics, Thralls, Undead, or Monstrous Forces

For cults, demonic powers, and sorcerous factions.

### 5.2 What the Chapter Must Answer About Levy

The chapter needs procedures for:

- who can be called
- by what right they can be called
- for how long they can be held in the field
- what happens to the land while they are away
- what part of the levy is badly equipped peasantry and what part is already war-ready

This is the actual precursor to war.

### 5.3 Proposed Muster Interface With Settlement Size

The chapter should not duplicate Chapter 12's troop-building rules. It should tell the GM what kinds of troops a faction can raise from what it controls.

Recommended approach:

- **Hamlets and isolated steadings** do not produce full formed troops. They provide scouts, shelter, guides, and a few armed locals.
- **Villages** can raise short-service levy and a few hunters.
- **Large villages** can raise levy plus one better-equipped contingent if infrastructure allows.
- **Towns and strongholds** can field trained soldiers, specialists, cavalry, or more durable garrisons.
- **Mercenary bands** join as already-defined forces with their own economics and leaders.

### 5.4 Infrastructure Must Matter

A faction should not field good troops just because the sheet says so. The existing stronghold chapter already contains the right inputs.

The following should clearly upgrade musters:

- **Barracks / Housing** — larger permanent garrison
- **Training Grounds** — better infantry quality
- **Shooting Range** — archers or crossbowmen
- **Stables / Pasture / Horse herds** — cavalry or mounted messengers
- **Forge / Mine / Market** — better arms and reliable upkeep
- **Temple** — zeal, obedience, or cult manpower
- **Road / Bridge / Pier** — easier concentration and supply movement

### 5.5 The Army Conversion Rule

The chapter should include one clear conversion rule:

> Chapter 11 determines what a faction can credibly put in the field. Chapter 12 determines how those troops fight once assembled.

The easiest implementation is:

- Chapter 11 gives each faction a list of available troop sections by source
- each source points to an existing Chapter 12 unit profile or equivalent
- named leaders with `Commander`, `General`, `Engineer`, or `Chief of Riders` become the important characters attached in Chapter 12

This keeps the systems separate without disconnecting them.

### 5.6 Mercenary Integration

This chapter should make explicit that factions may:

- hire mercenary bands for patrol, escort, punitive work, siege support, or temporary garrison
- bind a band by retainer rather than single contract
- fold a band into a host without making it cease to be politically separate

This creates a very Ravenland answer to army-building:

> Not all strength comes from obedient subjects.
> Some of it comes from silver and hunger.

## Part 6: Logistics and Campaign Burden

### 6.1 Every Muster Must Answer Four Questions

Before a faction enters Chapter 12, Chapter 11 should require four answers:

1. **Who feeds this force?**
2. **Who pays it?**
3. **What base does it draw from?**
4. **What roads, rivers, or escorts keep it supplied?**

If the faction cannot answer these, it should still be able to march — but at immediate logistical risk.

### 6.2 Campaign State

The chapter should distinguish a faction in quiet rule from a faction in campaign footing.

Suggested states:

- **Peace** — ordinary dues, local justice, patrols, diplomacy
- **Pressure** — raids, bribes, covert action, stockpiling, partial levy
- **Muster** — levy called, supply gathered, roads secured, armies concentrating
- **Campaign** — field forces active, regular logistics burden in effect

This creates a natural rhythm before battle ever begins.

### 6.3 Supply Should Reuse Chapter 12, Not Replace It

Once the army exists, this chapter should hand straight to Chapter 12's supply-line rules.

Chapter 11's job is earlier:

- declaring the base
- gathering the stockpile
- assigning road, river, or escort support
- deciding whether the army lives off stores, tribute, forage, or plunder

### 6.4 Local Consequences of Feeding Armies

This is essential.

When armies are fed from villages, the faction should be forced to choose:

- feed the army lightly and move carefully
- feed it hard and damage the region
- pay in silver and preserve standing if silver exists
- promise plunder and create a more dangerous army later

This is proper medieval politics and makes later aftermath meaningful.

### 6.5 Disease, Sanitation, and Recovery

The chapter should not leave all disease to siege sections only.

A faction with poor **Relief** should suffer more from:

- camp sickness
- marsh campaigning
- wounded retention
- spoiled provisions
- over-crowded musters

That gives **Relief** real political-military meaning at faction scale.

## Part 7: Political Acts the Chapter Must Cover

This chapter needs an explicit list of political acts. Without it, “politics” becomes vague conversation and loses procedure.

### 7.1 Core Political Acts

Recommended action list:

- **Collect Due** — call in tax, toll, tithe, or tribute
- **Grant Protection** — bind a settlement under oath and service
- **Press a Claim** — blood right, temple right, old law, border claim, inheritance
- **Swear Fealty** — bind lesser power upward
- **Give Safe-Conduct** — protect passage and trade
- **Take Hostage** — secure compliance or peace
- **Call Moot or Council** — settle disputes and consolidate rule
- **Judge or Outlaw** — impose justice, weregild, or public exclusion
- **Send Gift or Bride-Price** — soften conflict or open alliance
- **Hire Mercenaries** — purchase force without subject loyalty
- **Spy or Suborn** — covertly alter another power's cohesion
- **Call Levy** — transform authority into bodies under arms
- **Raid** — damage stores, standing, roads, or confidence without full battle
- **Invest a Place** — begin siege or coercive encirclement
- **Negotiate Truce or Peace** — end a feud before it becomes a campaign

### 7.2 Suggested Skill Pairings

The chapter should note likely skill uses, while allowing style differences.

Examples:

- Collect Due — **Yield** or **Decree**
- Grant Protection — **Accord**
- Press a Claim — **Claim** or **Rite**
- Take Hostage — **Accord**, **Intrigue**, or **Assault** depending on method
- Spy or Suborn — **Intrigue**
- Call Levy — **Muster**, often supported by **Rite** or **Decree** depending on the regime
- Raid — **Assault**, **Watch**, or **Relay** depending on objective
- Negotiate Peace — **Accord** supported by **Claim**, current Standing, or both

### 7.3 Political Failure Must Be Concrete

A failed political act should not just mean “nothing happens.”
It should mean something got worse.

Examples:

- a village complies but grows cold
- a vassal stalls and secretly bargains elsewhere
- the hostage compact holds, but a feud deepens
- the council splits openly
- a levy is raised, but too late and half-heartedly
- the peace is sworn, but one side keeps raiding through deniable retainers

That is where the drama is.

## Part 8: Existing Factions and Quick-Stat Procedure

### 8.1 Existing Factions Should Be Fast to Build

The chapter should include a quick-stat procedure for powers already in the campaign.

Recommended questions:

1. What is this faction called?
2. Where is its seat?
3. Why do people obey it?
4. What land, road, shrine, or market feeds it?
5. What settlements are ruled, protected, tributary, allied, or hostile?
6. What is its current claim or immediate aim?
7. What is its present shortage or weakness?
8. What army could it put in the field today?

Once those are answered, assign:

- attribute array
- skill emphasis
- 2–4 talents
- current standing and feud state
- treasury, stores, levy, and retainers

### 8.2 Suggested Faction Tiers

To keep prep fast, the chapter should offer three quick arrays.

#### Thin Power

A local household, weak cult, small warband, shrine circle, or bandit lord.

- attributes centered around **3, 3, 2, 2** or similar
- 2 strong skills, 4 moderate skills, rest low
- 2 talents

#### Established Power

A castle lord, strong clan, militant shrine, town league, or serious raider confederacy.

- attributes centered around **4, 3, 3, 3**
- 4 strong skills, 6 moderate skills
- 3 talents

#### Dominant Power

A regional crown, Rust Brother war machine, great dwarf hold, major cult, or demon court.

- attributes centered around **5, 4, 4, 3**
- 6 strong skills, 6 moderate skills
- 4 talents

These do not replace custom building. They make the chapter actually usable at the table.

### 8.3 Template Faction Types

The final chapter should include quick templates for at least the following.

| TYPE | RULE BASIS | MAIN INCOME | MAIN MUSTER |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Petty Crown** | blood, protection, old custom | tax, toll, levy dues | retainers, levy, household troops |
| **Castle Lordship** | landholding and oath | tax, toll, market, bridge, ferries | garrison, levy, mercenaries |
| **Clan Confederacy** | kin loyalty and war prestige | herds, tribute, raid, seasonal movement | levy, riders, household champions |
| **Militant Faith** | cult sanction and ritual law | tithes, gifts, temple lands | zealots, shrine guards, sworn captains |
| **Merchant League** | wealth and route control | toll, charter, caravan profit | guards, hired bands, skirmishers |

| **Sorcerous or Undead Dominion** | terror, magic, unnatural claim | plunder, forced due, cult extraction | thralls, monsters, undead, fanatics |

This is the simplest path to covering kings, cults, warlords, and demon armies in one system.

## Part 10: Exact Chapter 12 Handoff

### 10.1 Minimum Data Chapter 11 Must Export

Before Chapter 12 begins, the GM should be able to lift the following straight from the faction sheet:

- who is the general?
- which named captains or officers are present?
- what troop sections are fielded?
- what quality are they?
- what base feeds them?
- what line of supply connects base to army?
- are they levies, retainers, fanatics, mercenaries, or undead?
- are nearby settlements helping willingly, grudgingly, or under force?

If Chapter 11 can produce that, the handoff works.

### 10.2 Recommended Conversion Logic

- **Faction holdings** determine availability
- **Stronghold functions and settlement type** determine unit quality and specialization
- **Mercenary contracts** add professional troops or officers
- **Faction talents** unlock unusual options or battlefield advantages
- **Standing and recent burdens** determine how willingly villages keep feeding the army
- **Current attribute damage** colors the campaign state

Examples:

- low **Mandate** means levy reluctance, vassal hesitation, captains testing the leash, or treaties that no longer bind cleanly
- low **Iron** means fewer bodies, weak reserve, brittle garrisons, or an army that cannot force the issue
- low **Road** means late concentration, slow response, broken courier lines, or poor control of crossings and routes
- low **Hearth** means poor supply handling, ruined stores, exhausted labor, weak repair capacity, or a countryside already spent by war

### 10.3 Siege and Occupation Feedback Loop

Chapter 11 should also absorb the aftermath of Chapter 12:

- lost troops become reduced levy and retainers
- pillaged villages lose future value
- unpaid armies create grievance and splintering
- strongholds damaged in siege weaken the next campaign
- captured settlements shift status to occupied, tributary, or newly ruled

This closes the loop properly.

## Part 11: Final Recommendation

This chapter should be written as a **practical political engine**, not an essay on statecraft.

It should let the GM answer, in order:

1. what powers exist here?
2. what do they own, protect, exploit, or fear?
3. what can they credibly demand from nearby settlements?
4. what happens if they demand too much?
5. what kind of force can they raise right now?
6. how does that force get fed?
7. when does the current dispute become a raid, a siege, or a battle?

If Chapter 11 does that, it becomes the chapter that Ravenland currently lacks.

It will connect:

- settlement politics
- stronghold economics
- mercenary warfare
- and large-scale battle

into one coherent progression.

That is exactly what Book 02, Chapter 11 should do.
