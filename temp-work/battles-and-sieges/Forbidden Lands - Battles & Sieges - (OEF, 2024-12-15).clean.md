## Order of Battle

Battles are fought by two or more sides, each presenting itself to the enemy in a predetermined order planned by its commanding general.

Armies have an order of battle that involves organizing their forces into lines and sections, which play a fundamental role in the development of the battle and the rules system.

In this chapter, we will explore all aspects of battles from a broad, distant perspective, viewing their unfolding and conclusion from afar, so that in subsequent chapters we can delve deeper into the details of those who are bleeding in the heat of combat.

## Game Time

As in the original rules, battles are resolved in 15-minute increments, meaning each die roll represents what occurred during one turn.

At the end of each turn, after applying any modifiers corresponding to decisions and events, the opposing sides will each make a roll for each part of their order of battle, for a total of three rolls.

If it becomes necessary to focus on what happens to one or more particular characters, the flow of time shifts back to normal combat rounds.

## Army Lines

In essence, lines represent the depth of an army on the battlefield. They allow for staggered tactics, covering fronts in response to enemy movements, situation assessment, adaptability by the commanders, and providing relief for those exhausted by combat, as well as protecting the rear. Primarily, however, this setup prevents dozens or hundreds of warriors from crowding the front line, which would be chaotic and counterproductive.

Since the maximum number of dice an army can gain from its total number of troops is `+5`, any units beyond that point should be placed in a second line of battle, or a third if there are still more leftover units, and so forth. Thus, if every 20 soldiers grants `+1` die, once you reach 100 soldiers, the maximum `+5`, the excess should form a new line until reaching another 100 soldiers. If this logic is not entirely clear yet, it will become simpler once all the rules are explained.

In terms of rules, any lines behind the first, that is, not in the vanguard, are considered not directly involved in the battle and therefore are not counted in each side's roll. Again, you can only apply a maximum of `+5` due to troop numbers.

Given the relatively small army sizes in the Forbidden Lands, it's likely that few sides will be able to field more than one line in a battle, especially considering that each line is divided into sections.

## Battle Line Sections

Each battle line of an army is divided into three sections: left, center, and right. Each turn of the battle, both sides roll once for each section, resulting in a total of three rolls per turn.

Considering that, as stated, the maximum number of dice a side can gain from the number of troops is `+5` for 100 warriors, each section can apply this `+5` separately. As a result, a full battle line of an army can have a maximum of 300 soldiers, 100 per section of a line. The group of soldiers making up a single section of a line is called a **troop**.

Under normal conditions, each section of an army faces off against the corresponding enemy section. However, due to a lack of warriors or one section's victory over another, this can change as the battle progresses. In such cases, sections will attack the nearest enemy section, enjoying a clear numerical advantage. Other than that, the rules remain exactly the same.

## Troops Replacement

As a troop's soldier count diminishes, one might think that rear-line troops would fill in these gaps with fresh warriors. But this usually doesn't happen in reality.

In real battles, it is more orderly and efficient to replace one entire troop with another rather than swapping individual soldiers. Frontline troops withdraw to make room for a fresh, fully intact troop. If necessary, they can regroup in the rear to form a new troop with the remaining soldiers.

To clarify this procedure: you cannot compensate for the loss of 20 soldiers, equivalent to `+1` die, by adding 20 soldiers from another troop in the rear. Such specific movements would disrupt the different formations and chains of command essential for maintaining effectiveness in battle.

The real decision is how long a troop should remain fighting before being replaced by another. This is determined by the army's general or the troop's commander, if they have that authority.

If a frontline troop is defeated, flees, or is replaced, the next troop in the rear should take its place in the following turn. If the army has no secondary line, that section remains empty, allowing the enemy section to attack the nearest section instead.

## Troop Regrouping

A troop can stop fighting for one of three reasons: being defeated, fleeing due to demoralization, or receiving an order to withdraw. In the first case, the troop has been destroyed and no longer has warriors capable of continuing the fight or even escaping. However, in the case of fleeing or withdrawal, troops can regroup in the rear.

## Troop Uniformity

Although every Forbidden Lands army includes soldiers with slight differences in their equipment and skill, troops should be composed of warriors who share the same general characteristics.

For example, a single troop can only be made up of infantry units with similar gear and level of training. If there are mounted units in the same army, they must form their own separate troop rather than being mixed in with infantry troops.

## Troop Dice

In each turn of a battle, the clash between troops is resolved with a dice roll. Each troop engaged in combat is entitled to one roll per turn.

To determine the dice a troop can roll, we need to know its composition as well as any orders it may have received.

## Base Dice

Base dice are determined by the number of units that make up the troop. The number of units required to gain `+1` die depends on the **type** of troop. Since the maximum number of base dice a troop can have is 5, its type also determines the maximum number of soldiers it can contain.

You can easily determine the types of troops and the number of units they consist of using the **Types of Troops** table.

**Infantry:** In the Forbidden Lands, infantry troops are the most common in all armies. They are soldiers who fight on foot in close combat.

**Skirmishers:** Skirmishers are troops that attack their enemies from a safe distance using bows, crossbows, slings, or javelins. Their main advantage lies in their effectiveness at range, but they are highly vulnerable if forced into melee combat.

**Cavalry:** Cavalry troops consist of mounted soldiers who typically engage in close combat, benefiting from their height advantage and the power of their mounts. However, they can also harass their enemies with ranged weapons, taking full advantage of their mounts' superior mobility.

**Monster:** Monsters in the Forbidden Lands vary greatly, but this category only includes those with monster attacks and significant size. Creatures that function as standard characters, such as skeletons or ghouls, are placed in one of the previous troop categories.

**Cost:** This represents the daily cost in copper coins to keep each unit of the troop fed. This amount doubles if the unit is also paid a salary, which is uncommon. Otherwise, the promise of reward is based on plunder.

## Advantage Dice

Troops have specific advantages in combat, represented by their **advantage dice**. Each qualifying characteristic grants `+1` advantage die, up to a maximum of `+5`.

However, these extra dice can never exceed the modifier granted by the troop's **base dice**. For example, if a troop has 3 base dice, its advantage dice cannot exceed 3, regardless of the number of advantages it possesses.

Remember, troops must consist of units with similar characteristics, meaning all or most soldiers in the troop should share the same advantage to apply each advantage die. If a specific advantage cannot reasonably apply to all units, you should either separate them into different troops or not apply the advantage.

**Well-Trained Units:** Soldiers who have undergone at least three months of training or dedicate their lives to combat are considered well-trained. These troops consist of professional warriors, such as mercenaries or guards, or units whose prestige or social standing is tied to their martial prowess.

**Orc or Dwarf Infantry:** It is widely known that both orcs and dwarves possess exceptional strength and resilience, allowing them to perform better than average under the grueling conditions of battle.

**Demon or Demorph Infantry:** These beings are the most terrifying infantry units on the battlefield, possessing supernatural qualities that make them significantly stronger, more unpredictable, and far more fearsome.

**Elven Skirmishers:** Elves possess exceptional vision and dexterity, making them outstanding archers.

**Religious, Racial, or Regional Fervor:** This advantage die is granted to a troop with a particular motive that increases its fervor in battle, resulting in superior performance. Examples include religious missions, deep-seated hatred, or pride tied to racial or regional causes. Minor rivalries are not sufficient to grant this die. It must stem from an exceptionally high fervor, such as the Rust Brothers' zeal against heretics.

**Includes an Important Character:** The troop includes one of the story's important characters. This applies not only to PCs but also to important NPCs, including antagonists on the enemy side.

**Larger Units:** A troop attacking one composed of smaller units receives a bonus. This often occurs when cavalry faces infantry or when monsters fight against smaller beings.

**Flanking Attack:** Troops most commonly flank an enemy when they have won their section of the battlefield and can turn to attack others. Other narrative circumstances may also allow for flanking.

**Rear Attack:** When an enemy troop is engaged with another, it is possible to attack it from the rear with a special maneuver.

**Fighting for Survival:** When cornered, even animals become more dangerous. If soldiers are fighting for their lives against certain annihilation, they gain a fervent boost to save themselves from death.

The equipment of a troop is a fundamental aspect to consider on the battlefield, as it defines its combat capabilities against enemy units. Each troop possesses a specific weapon type chosen from the possible options in the **Weapon Type** table.

**Attacks First:** This is a significant advantage in combat, allowing the troop equipped with this weapon to roll their combat dice first, potentially inflicting casualties on the enemy before they can roll. If two troops have weapons that attack first, the effect is canceled, and both roll their dice simultaneously.

**Cost:** Weapon types add an additional cost to the unit. This amount, expressed in copper coins, is added to the base maintenance cost of the unit type. For example, an infantry unit with a polearm will cost 10 copper coins per soldier. In a troop of 100 soldiers, that means 1,000 copper coins per day. Maintaining an army is no cheap endeavor.

**Attack +X:** Some weapons provide a bonus to the troop's attack, usually conditional based on the weapon or the type of enemy unit they are facing. These dice are added as if they were advantage dice, but they can exceed the base dice limit.

**Attacks Second:** A major disadvantage that forces the troop to always wait to roll their dice until after their enemies have rolled, suffering casualties before they can inflict any. Note that this effect only applies to melee combat.

**Free Attack:** Before engaging in melee combat, the troop can make an attack without the enemy troop being able to retaliate. It also does not suffer any morale-based casualties. This attack can be made either when approaching the enemy or when the enemy approaches them. This ability can be acquired more than once, but it can only be used once per battle for each instance acquired. This effect is resolved in the same turn that the troops engage and make their normal attack rolls.

**Range:** We want to keep the flow of battles abstract, including troop movement and distances. However, we emphasize the advantage of ranged weapons with the Range effect. This allows troops with such weapons to attack the enemy before that enemy troop can strike. In a way, this effect can be understood as adding extra battle turns before the enemy reaches close combat. With Range 1, one additional turn is added before the front line can attack. With Range 2, two turns are added. Troops with these weapons cannot attack the enemy's rear lines and do not suffer morale losses from these attacks. Any troop with these weapons is considered a Skirmisher unless mounted.

**Two-Handed:** Two-handed weapons prevent the units in the troop from carrying shields or throwable weapons.

**Demoralized:** This effect means that the troop cannot use Morale Points to avoid suffering morale-based losses. This effect applies in certain circumstances, especially when a troop equipped with ranged weapons is forced into melee combat.

## Protection Type

In Ravenland, most units brought to battle wear simple linen clothing and carry a cheap yet effective weapon, such as a spear. This is due to the limited resources available to leaders and armies. These troops fall like flies when facing better-equipped troops capable of effective defense. Protections are expensive, reserved for the most privileged warriors, but they significantly increase effectiveness in battle.

**Protection X:** These are the extra dice provided by armor or shields when defending against an attack. Sometimes they are conditional, depending on the type of weapon the units are resisting. The troop can always roll their protection dice, even when not in direct combat, to avoid taking losses.

**Morale +X:** Increases the troop's starting Morale in battles due to the sense of security provided by their protection.

## Battle Sequence

It's time to address the sequence of a battle according to the new rules in this book, from troop deployment to troop combat, and finally, the retreat of the defeated army.

## Battle Preparations

First, you must organize your troops, dividing your units into groups of the same type (infantry, cavalry, skirmishers, or monsters), preferably with the same advantages. If, for any reason, you need to group units with different advantages, those dice do not count and are lost, at least until the condition is met.

The exception to this rule is the case of **important characters** , as they act as the commander, hero, or leader of the troop. However, in this case, the character sacrifices all their traits to provide the troop with an extra die.

Once the troops are organized (which you likely already had prepared in advance), you must position them on the battlefield, assigning them to the left, center, and right sections, and placing them in the necessary ranks.

There may be cases where armies are so large that it makes sense to deploy more than one troop per section. In such cases, both generals should have the opportunity to place the same number of troops per section. This is recommended if an army exceeds two ranks in its order of battle.

The order in which the armies are positioned on the battlefield is determined by an **insight** roll from the generals. The winner of this roll sets the conditions of the battle, including the exact location and the advantage of positioning their troops after seeing those of the enemy. Ties are resolved with another roll.

If the sections are extended as mentioned earlier, this must be clarified before the generals' roll. It should not come as a surprise to either side.

## General’s Speech

Once the armies are assembled and facing off for battle, the generals deliver their speeches to motivate their warriors. The speech consists of a few words or a short address, followed by a **performance** roll. The successes rolled grant the army's troops an equivalent amount of **morale points** .

These speeches, especially those affecting armies with PCs involved, should be roleplayed in some way to create a memorable and warlike atmosphere for the battle, particularly if a PC is leading the army. While no one expects to be a medieval military leader, making an effort to set the tone before a dramatic slaughter would add an interesting element to the campaign.

It is entirely possible to push the roll to gain more successes and further increase the troops’ morale. Keep in mind that Morale Points are crucial for keeping troops fighting and preventing them from fleeing (or suffering morale-related casualties).

If a character is a competent commander but lacks the charisma to rally the troops, it may be best for them not to assume leadership of an army.

## Battle Turns

As previously mentioned, battles unfold over several turns (15 minutes each). During each turn, troops attack and are attacked, rolling dice to inflict damage and defend against it.

During a turn, orders may be given, and various actions or events may occur. At the end of each turn, dice are rolled after accounting for the troop’s current situation in the battle.

## Troop Dice

Each troop has a set of dice determined by its characteristics and equipment. As with standard characters in Forbidden Lands, you should use dice of different colors: one color for **base dice** , another for **advantage dice** , and a third for **protection dice** .

BASE DICE: These correspond to the number of units in the troop, which depends on the troop type. Whether 20 units (infantry and skirmishers), 5 units (cavalry), or 1 unit (monsters), base dice increase by 1 for every threshold reached, up to a maximum of 5.

ADVANTAGE DICE: These are gained through specific circumstances involving the troop or the battle. Any modifiers to the roll are applied to these dice, from those listed in the Advantage Dice table to weapon bonuses (noted as Attack +X). These dice can never exceed the troop's base dice.

PROTECTION DICE: These dice allow the troop to mitigate damage from the enemy. They are granted by shields and armor. Protection dice can be used even when not in direct combat, such as against projectile attacks. Each success on these dice reduces damage by 1, and rolls of 1 have no effect.


## Battle Roll

At the end of each battle turn, troops in combat combine their base dice, advantage dice, and protection dice and roll to determine successes. Under normal circumstances, all troops in combat roll their dice simultaneously, unless a ranged attack, Attacks First, or Attacks Second effect is in play.

To maintain order, add drama, or simply for practical purposes, it is often best to resolve each troop clash separately. The concept of simultaneous rolls reflects that both troops attack and defend at the same time, causing casualties simultaneously.

BASE SUCCESSES: Successes from base dice inflict 1 point of damage on the opposing troop. Each point of damage reduces the enemy troop's base dice by 1, representing the death of enemy units unless blocked.

ADVANTAGE SUCCESSES: Successes from advantage dice are compared with the enemy's, canceling each other out. Any remaining successes for one side inflict 1 point of damage on the opposing troop for each success.

PROTECTION SUCCESSES: Each success on protection dice cancels 1 point of damage that the troop would have otherwise received from the roll.

DEMORALIZATION: Rolls of 1 on base dice indicate a loss of morale for that portion of the troop, potentially driving them to flee.

## Morale Points

Through both the general's speech and their protections, each troop has Morale Points during the battle. These points serve a single purpose: to nullify results of 1 that cause demoralization. When a troop runs out of Morale, demoralization results will cause one die (representing a corresponding number of units) to flee the battlefield and seek refuge in the rear.

Each point of damage a troop takes that cannot be blocked (i.e., each die lost due to casualties) reduces its Morale Points by 1.

DEATH TO COWARDS: The general can issue an order to execute any soldiers who flee from the enemy. This is a drastic and cruel decision that will reduce the army's numbers but also give remaining warriors a reason to keep fighting (no escape, it’s victory or death). Each fleeing die (or group of soldiers) can be eliminated by a troop stationed in the rear: one die for each fleeing die per turn. For each die executed, the Morale Points of all other troops in the army increase by 1. After the battle, the general and commanders who carried out this order must deal with the discontent of their soldiers if the decision was unjustified.

## Troop Regrouping

At the start of each battle turn, army leaders can attempt a **regroup** roll to convince fleeing soldiers to return to the fight. The general is entitled to one roll per turn, while troop commanders (if they are important characters) may also make one roll. This is only possible if the character is not actively engaged in combat on the front line.


The roll is with **performance** or **manipulation** . Each success convinces one base die to regroup and prepare to fight again when ordered, positioning itself in the rear. However, each result of 1 causes one die to permanently leave the battle. In both cases, the character decides which result applies to each group.

These 1s also apply to skill dice, so while you can push these rolls, any 1s rolled on dice cannot be re-rolled. Otherwise, apply the normal rules for pushing rolls.

Demoralized units that are not regrouped by the end of the second turn after fleeing leave the battlefield permanently.

## Important Character Die

Important characters grant 1 advantage die to the troop they are in. For the sake of the story, you may need to determine what happens to them during each turn of the battle. To do this, you can use a die of a different color to represent the character alone. This die is still treated as an advantage die, but it can also have special effects. These rules are optional.

All these details help build a unique and unforgettable experience.

## Commanders Movement

Important characters have the ability to switch troops depending on the battle's situation. At the start of each turn, they can move to a different troop, granting that troop their associated advantage die. Battlefields can theoretically be traversed in one turn, allowing a commander to move to any allied troop in any section or rank.

This also applies to regrouping demoralized units. A character can leave their troop at the start of a turn to move to the rear and make a **regroup** roll. However, only one movement is allowed per turn, so they cannot return to the fight after attempting to regroup.

## Range

When troops with the Range effect are present, additional turns are played in the battle during which only these troops attack. Their enemies may roll their protection dice to avoid damage and use their advantage dice solely to cancel the successes of the skirmishers' advantage dice.

The logic behind this is that while units are advancing to engage their enemies on the battlefield, skirmishers can leverage their ranged weapons to inflict casualties.

The Range effect only applies to troops actively moving into combat, whether they are advancing from the first rank or moving up from another rank to engage.

We aim to keep the battlefield abstract to simplify the portrayal of these scenes. However, you can divide the map into zones and create a more realistic simulation of events if you prefer.

## Skirmishing Cavalry

Cavalry troops equipped with ranged weapons have a significant advantage on the battlefield, as they can harass enemies and retreat to a safe distance with ease thanks to the mobility of their mounts.

This is represented by what we call the **mobility advantage** . Skirmishers with this advantage do not suffer the effects of Attacks Second or Demoralized from their ranged weapons as long as their enemy does not surpass them in successes during a **battle roll** . If, in any turn, the opposing troop surpasses the skirmishing cavalry in successes, they lose their mobility advantage and must apply the negative effects of their weapons. To determine the loss of this advantage, only successes from base dice and advantage dice are considered, regardless of the damage inflicted after reductions from protection.

It is possible to regain the mobility advantage. If, in a subsequent turn, the troop surpasses its enemy in successes, it means they have executed a maneuver to maintain distance once more. In the following turn, they will once again benefit from their mobility advantage until they lose it, as explained above.

## Sieges

Sieges involve an army attempting to assault a fortified position, while the defenders use every means at their disposal to resist.

The rules for sieges slightly modify the general battle rules previously explained. Below, we address the specific considerations for this type of conflict.

## Units Per Troop

Warriors within a fortress are defending a position, allowing them to maximize their numbers to repel the enemy. To gain one **base die** , infantry and skirmisher troops need 10 units instead of 20. The maximum base dice remain 5, but this adjustment allows for the formation of twice as many troops of these types.

## Order of Battle

In sieges, the battlefield consists of the walls, the fortress courtyard, and the surrounding terrain where the attackers lay siege. Because of this, the order of battle changes entirely.

Sections are no longer divided into three but are instead organized as needed for the defense and assault of the fortress, depending on the attackers’ disposition as they approach the walls.

For sieges, the attacking army always places its troops first, without exception (generals do not roll to determine the order). Once this is done, the defenders position their troops to repel the assault. There will be as many sections as the attackers establish, based on what makes sense for the besieged location. The Gamemaster has the final word in this matter.

This grants an advantage to the attackers, as they can open multiple fronts if they outnumber the defenders. However, the defenders gain the benefit of seeing the enemy’s disposition and can form smaller, equally or more effective troops for battle.

Sections remain in place even if the attackers breach the walls and the battle moves inside. Sections may also change if the narrative progression of the battle warrants it. Again, the Gamemaster must remain flexible to handle these situations.


## Walls

Walls grant defenders the ability to **attack first** , as well as advantage dice based on their size. However, attackers can attempt to scale the walls to reach the top and fight on equal footing.

If, in any battle turn, an infantry troop achieves more successes than the defenders, it means they have secured their position on the walls. The walls no longer provide their advantage, at least against that troop. The bonus cannot be regained, as the fight is now taking place on the walls themselves. However, units can no longer retreat, meaning demoralization results in automatic deaths.

If a fortification has more than one wall, the bonus is considered separately for each one. Wall modifiers do not stack.

We will address the specific effects of walls in the chapter on Strongholds.

## Siege Engines

Siege engines are essential tools for any army attempting to assault a stronghold. Each one provides various effects that greatly benefit the attackers, either compensating for the advantages of walls or rendering them ineffective.

Each engine must be operated by a troop within the army, which is responsible for moving, operating, and protecting it. Engines that attack from a distance are typically assigned to cavalry or skirmisher troops, while those meant to directly engage the walls are assigned to infantry.

## Engineers

Every siege engine constructed and operated requires an engineer. Construction can only continue while an engineer is assigned to the project. This is particularly critical for tunnels, which need constant supervision.

For catapults and trebuchets to function effectively, an engineer must be calibrating the weight of the stones and the range. Without this oversight, their attack dice are reduced to 1/4. Battering rams and siege towers, on the other hand, can be used without engineers once construction is complete.

Engineers are special characters who charge a small fortune (5 silver coins per day) but are indispensable for any besieging army. Only a character with the Engineer talent can perform this role. This talent is explained in the following chapter.

## Cavalry and Skirmishers

When assaulting a wall, horses and arrows are of little use. Troops of these types simply cannot fight as attackers until the gates are opened and the battle moves inside. These troops are often assigned to operate catapults and trebuchets.


## Blockade

The safest but slowest way to conquer a stronghold is to starve its defenders into surrender. To establish an effective blockade that prevents resupply, the attacking army must outnumber the defending army by three to one. If the stronghold has access to the sea, it must also be blockaded by ships, or the siege will be ineffective.

Once the blockade begins, the stronghold relies entirely on its reserves for survival. However, the besieging army must also sustain its soldiers during the slow passage of weeks or months, making this strategy more challenging than simply waiting.

## Supplies

One of the primary concerns an army must address to succeed in a military campaign is how to feed its members. Running out of supplies is a direct path to the absolute destruction of a military force without the enemy needing to draw a single sword. This applies not only to defenders holding a stronghold against an enemy attempting to starve them into surrender but also to attackers who must keep their troops well-fed to maintain their effectiveness in battle and prevent desertion or death.

Given the critical importance of food and access to it during war, the following rules adapt this fundamental aspect.


## Supply Units

Each army has a number of **supply units** . These are obtained from their bases of operations or by foraging and looting the surrounding area.

Each infantry and skirmisher unit in the army consumes 1 supply unit per day, cavalry units consume 2, and monsters consume 20.

To understand the production and storage of these supplies, we will provide specific rules in the chapter on Strongholds.

Once per day (during any quarter of the day), the troops must be fed. For every unit in your army, reduce your supply units by 1.

## Starving Troops

When your supply units are insufficient to feed your entire army, you must decide which units to feed and which to leave hungry.

This is done by selecting troops, deciding that none of the units in those troops will receive food for that day, thereby saving the corresponding supply units.

Each troop with unfed units suffers demoralization with results of 1 and 2 causing this effect. Additionally, all enemy troops fighting against them gain 2 advantage dice that are not limited by base dice.

These drastic measures can be taken not only when you are already out of supplies but also when you anticipate running out within a certain number of days unless consumption is reduced. It is up to you to decide when and how to leave your troops without food.

## Desertion and Death

A troop that has not been fed for a day must make a roll for **desertion** and **death** . Roll the troop’s **base dice** : On one hand, each result of 1 causes desertion, removing that die from the troop. On the other hand, add the number of days the troop has gone unfed to the same result: each result of 10 or higher means the death of the units it represents. These rolls are independent, so the days of hunger do not make it impossible to roll results of 1.

## Buying Supplies

Acquiring a supply unit (such as in a village before starting a military campaign) costs 2 copper coins, equivalent to a simple yet high-calorie meal. The specific situation of a village or region, depending on supply and demand, might alter this price.

## Foraging for Supplies

An army's soldiers can be sent to forage and hunt for food. This is a much more common and necessary task than it might initially seem. The environment is crucial for an army to avoid rapidly depleting its reserves.

When a general orders a forage for supplies, the involved troops roll their **base dice** (skirmisher dice are doubled): the results on the dice are multiplied by 10. The total sum of these results is the number of supply units the army obtains in a quarter day.

The natural resources of a hex are gradually depleted by this predatory method of gathering food. For each quarter day an army forages in a specific hex, the result of each base die rolled is reduced by 1. This penalty recovers at a rate of 1 per week.

If a troop is sent to forage in another hex, the army must divide, leaving troops unable to assist each other if battles are initiated to exploit this division. The rules regarding this will be explained later in the section on army movement.


## Supply Line

Armies are connected to a base, which can be a stronghold, a fort, a village, or even a dungeon, any location capable of gathering and providing resources for the army.

Part of the logistics of leading an army involves securing a supply line to transport provisions from this base to the troops in the field. As long as this connection is maintained, these troops can receive supply units and reinforcements to bolster their ranks or replenish troops that have suffered casualties.

The base’s capacity to provide supplies and reinforcements depends on the specific case, which will be addressed in a later chapter. For now, note that the supply line follows the shortest possible path between the hexagon where the base is located and the army’s current position.

MARCH: The normal march of an army corresponds to its slowest troops. Infantry can move 1 hexagon per quarter day, while cavalry can move 2 hexagons per quarter day.

FORCED MARCH: Armies can march for a second quarter day, but this is considered a forced march. If the army enters battle on the same day or the next, its base dice will suffer increased demoralization, with results of 1 triggering the effect with an additional +1. Given all that an army must accomplish during a marching day, forced marches are generally not advisable.

COOKING: Armies usually carry raw or dried food, particularly grain, as it is easier to transport. Food obtained from the same hex is raw and may spoil, so it must be cooked as soon as possible. Regardless, dedicating a quarter day to cooking and eating is fundamental to an army's routine.

## Cutting Supply Lines

The most effective way to wear down an army without engaging it in direct combat is to sever its connection to its base, preventing it from receiving supplies. This strategy can be especially frustrating for large invading armies, which will soon have to resort to ravaging the surrounding environment to feed themselves.

How to achieve this depends on the circumstances. Generally, it is sufficient for the enemy to pass through one of the direct hexagons between the army and its base. A small skirmish occurs, and the supplies are stolen. If reinforcements are also being sent, a larger battle might occur. Since supplies are typically delivered once a week, cutting the line denies the army food for that duration.

This tactic is often carried out by cavalry troops, as their speed reduces the likelihood of being intercepted by the enemy army.

CAMPING: An army on the move must break camp in the morning and set it up again at the location where it plans to spend the night. This process takes an entire quarter day. An army that does not set up a proper camp is far more vulnerable to surprise attacks, especially at night.

SLEEPING: Troops need to sleep, typically for one quarter day, most likely at night. If poorly rested, a troop will suffer increased demoralization, with results of 1 triggering the effect with an additional +1 due to exhaustion.

ACCUMULATED DEMORALIZATION: Note that due to poor nutrition, forced marches, and lack of sleep, a troop can accumulate demoralization effects to the point where results of 1 through 4 on its base dice trigger this effect.

## Weekly Income

Stronghold income is calculated on a weekly basis, as it provides a reasonable timeframe without becoming unmanageable or tedious. If you prefer a longer period, simply multiply the benefits accordingly.

The income listed in the table for resources transformed into other goods already accounts for the loss of the original resource in the process. If the stronghold does not produce the original resource, it should purchase it from a nearby village or rely on a Market.

We understand that this method of generating income for each resource-producing function removes the possibility of a realistic stronghold economy. However, it simplifies management, allowing focus on aspects more engaging for players. Managing each resource separately and dealing with supply and demand logistics would be more immersive but would significantly alter the campaign's objectives and progression.

## Stockpiling Supplies

As mentioned earlier, armies require not only their wages but also food. For this, the stronghold relies on its food production functions.

For a more realistic approach, you can reserve the food units produced as supplies. The simpler option is to convert everything into copper coins and purchase supply units once you tally the total income: each supply unit costs 3 copper coins.

Prudent leaders should keep one or more pantries stocked with supplies to prepare for a siege. If a siege drags on, the pantries will deplete quickly, but it’s better than starving.

Strongholds often extend their influence over surrounding lands, offering protection or imposing threats on nearby settlements. Their responsibilities or tensions are often resolved through the payment of tributes.

As armies advance, they frequently pass through villages to request or demand resources and supplies, if they do not outright pillage them.

## Construction of Walls

Walls are the primary defense of any stronghold. They come at a very high cost but provide advantage dice until they are breached or damaged by attackers.

The base cost for walls is 800 copper, but both height and thickness can be increased, multiplying the cost. With each increment, the number of advantage dice they provide also increases, up to a maximum of +4, representing truly imposing walls.

There is no restriction against building multiple walls for a stronghold, creating defensive sections. Each wall is constructed separately following the same rules.

## Salaries

Controlling a large army in the Forbidden Lands is nearly impossible for one simple reason: the cost is extraordinarily high. The subsistence economy that characterizes most settlements makes maintaining an armed force large enough to dominate these lands an overly ambitious and doomed endeavor.

For this reason, strongholds typically maintain only a small garrison during peacetime, and wars must, by necessity, be very brief. A prolonged war can devastate an entire region, as too many people would be devoted solely to killing each other rather than working to produce food or wealth, relying on those who do.

It is possible that the politics of the Ravenland may gradually evolve into a feudal system, allowing for regional control through bonds of servitude. Even so, the prohibitive costs of armies would remain largely unchanged.

## Payment of Salaries

As previously mentioned, army units demand salaries in copper coins. Payment is typically expected on a weekly basis rather than daily and can sometimes be made at the end of an entire month. We recommend weekly payments to align with the income calculated from strongholds and tribute collection.

Salary agreements can vary depending on circumstances. Often, warriors are promised everything acquired during the campaign through looting, a predatory promise highly destructive to the civilian population that eventually ruins entire regions.

Another common method is to pay soldiers in full at the end of the campaign, usually at the start of winter. However, this is not well-received by soldiers, as the failure to fulfill such promises is frequent.


There are many possible payment arrangements, but they are typically established during recruitment. Changing the terms with the army mid-campaign is rarely a good idea, as it generates dissatisfaction and distrust, though sometimes it may be the only option.

## Failure to Pay

With so many varied salary arrangements, it is impossible to establish a universal rule for what happens when soldiers are not paid their due. Generals may renegotiate payments or make new promises.

When a section of the army realizes they will not receive their rightful payment as agreed, desertion begins. This can manifest as troops abandoning the army or turning against their general in pursuit of revenge or spoils.

Deserters without funds may quickly resort to looting villages, or besieging their former leader until their demands are met.

## Disease

In sieges, disease is always lurking, affecting both defenders and attackers alike. With many people crowded together, poorly fed, and with little to no hygiene, it is common for various viruses and bacteria to spread almost unchecked.

At the end of each week, roll a D6 for every 100 units in the army (rounding up). If any of the dice result in a 1, disease breaks out in the army. Each 1 in the roll adds one **disease die** .

When disease breaks out, and at the end of each subsequent week, roll the disease dice: each result of 1 kills 10 warriors, while each result of 6 increases the number of disease dice by 1. Any results other than 1 or 6 remove that die without causing any additional effects. The week after a disease disappears (when all its dice are removed), repeat the process to determine whether a new disease breaks out.

When disease breaks out or when a 6 is rolled on the disease dice, besieged strongholds double the total number of disease dice unless they have the Sewer function.

## Important Characters

When we talk about **important characters** , we often think of PCs, however this doesn’t have to be a limitation. Many other characters can be important in the context of a battle, recognizable antagonists from the story are the best examples.

It’s possible for an enemy who might never otherwise become a notable character to achieve significance through the events of a battle, or for a new character to be created during the battle to add depth to the campaign.

In this way, a game session focused on the development of a battle can be the perfect situation for a new character, previously unknown, to become a pivotal ally or enemy.

With this concept, we aim to emphasize that while established important characters in the campaign should These characters follow a basic rule in battle corresponding to the advantage die they grant their troop. Their fate, in general terms, is tied to the results of this specific die (in a different color if you wish to apply the rule).

The events in this chapter are considered separate from the destiny determined by the previously explained rules. In any case, characters must face the unique events they encounter during the battle, bearing the wounds caused by the results of their advantage die.

## Commanders

Important Characters are not always the commanders of their troop. Sometimes, a champion of the army must serve under the command of an experienced and capable officer.

Imagine a PC with the Rogue profession. In a structured army, it would be natural for them not to hold the rank of troop commander. However, their presence and combat skill


would still grant the troop the corresponding advantage die. Additionally, they would experience battle events that make their participation memorable. Even so, they might lack the appropriate personality to lead soldiers on the battlefield.

With all this explained, we aim to convey that the concepts of commander and important character do not always go hand in hand, though they often will.

At the beginning of each battle turn, important characters engaged in combat must roll a D66 to determine if they will take part in an event. Refer to the corresponding table based on their current situation.

These events may unfold just before the troop’s battle roll for the turn or during the resolution of its consequences. (To do so, make the troop roll first and weave the results into the event narrative).
